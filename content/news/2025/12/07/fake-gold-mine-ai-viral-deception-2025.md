Over the past weeks, social networks have been flooded with a dramatic headline:  
**“The largest gold mine in the world discovered — a 100 km long deposit!”**

Photos showed massive gold blocks, machinery, workers and even “on-site interviews.”  
The posts claimed that:

- a **100 km gold deposit** had been found,  
- it was the “biggest discovery in modern history,”  
- several governments were negotiating mining rights,  
- gold prices were “about to skyrocket.”

The visuals looked convincing enough that many people took the story at face value.

### 👉 But the truth is simple: the entire story is fake.

## **No reputable media outlet has reported on this**

If the world’s largest gold deposit had been discovered, Reuters, Bloomberg, CNBC, Financial Times, AP and other major outlets would be covering it non-stop.

Yet there are:

- no official reports  
- no geological data  
- no company announcements  
- no government statements  
- no financial analyses  

The story exists only on a handful of accounts that occasionally share dramatic, source-less content.

## **The ‘gold mine’ photo is AI-generated**

The image circulating online looks real at first glance — but a closer look exposes clear AI markers:

- unnatural shadows  
- repeated texture patterns  
- unrealistic reflections  
- distorted proportions  
- oddly scaled machinery  

This is not a photograph.  
It is a **synthetic AI image** designed for virality.

For this reason, we use the illustration **Gold-mine-fake.png** in this article, clearly marked as *Fake*, to highlight how easy it is to fabricate “evidence”.

## **Why do people fall for AI-generated fake news?**

1. Photographic imagery feels inherently trustworthy.  
2. Emotional narratives spread faster than fact-checking.  
3. AI mimics the style of real news reporting.  
4. Viral repetition creates a false sense of legitimacy.  
5. People tend to believe stories that match their expectations.
