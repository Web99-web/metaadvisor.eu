MEV stands for *Maximal Extractable Value* and describes how certain participants on blockchain networks can earn extra profits by manipulating the order of transactions before they are permanently recorded in a block.

What may sound like a technical detail has very real consequences for users: worse execution prices, higher hidden costs, and the sense that someone else is always one step ahead.

## How does MEV arise?

Before a transaction is finalized, it briefly sits in the so-called mempool. During this phase, it is visible to validators, miners, and specialized bots.

Those responsible for assembling blocks can:
- reorder transactions  
- insert their own transactions  
- exploit pending user activity for profit  

This power is the foundation of MEV.

## Front-running as the most common form

The best-known form of MEV is front-running. A bot detects a user’s intention to buy a token on a decentralized exchange and places its own purchase just ahead of the user. When the user’s transaction pushes the price up, the bot sells at a profit.

The user ends up paying more — without having done anything wrong.

## Why is MEV controversial?

MEV creates a structural advantage for professional actors. Although technically permitted, it:
- undermines the idea of equal rules for all participants  
- turns “fair launches” into an illusion  
- introduces a hidden tax on DeFi trading  

As a result, MEV is increasingly viewed as a systemic problem rather than a neutral optimization.

## From a technical issue to the courtroom

For years, MEV was discussed mainly among developers. As retail participation grew and memecoin platforms proliferated, the issue moved beyond technical circles.

Lawsuits now argue that platforms advertising fair trading while enabling MEV advantages for insiders may be misleading users.

If courts accept this reasoning, MEV could become a legal — not just technical — issue.
