# South Korea Delays Digital Asset Law Amid Stablecoin Issuance Dispute

South Korea has once again postponed the passage of its Digital Asset Basic Act, a cornerstone regulation intended to govern cryptocurrency trading and issuance in one of Asia’s most active digital asset markets. The delay is not driven by technical hurdles, but by a **fundamental regulatory disagreement over who should be allowed to issue won-pegged stablecoins**.

Although the law is widely viewed as the foundation of South Korea’s future crypto framework, conflicting approaches among key institutions have stalled its approval.

## The core issue: who can issue stablecoins?

The country’s central bank has taken a highly conservative stance, arguing that **only majority bank-owned entities** should be permitted to issue stablecoins. According to this view, banks are already subject to strict capital, compliance, and anti–money laundering requirements, making them best positioned to safeguard financial stability.

The financial regulator, however, has warned that such restrictions could **stifle competition and innovation**. Fintech and blockchain firms, it argues, often possess the technical expertise and infrastructure needed to build scalable and efficient digital payment systems.

## Global comparisons and regulatory pressure

International precedents are increasingly shaping the debate. In the European Union, the MiCA framework allows licensed crypto firms to issue stablecoins, while Japan has already approved yen-linked stablecoin projects led by fintech companies under regulatory oversight.

Supporters of a more open model caution that a bank-only approach could leave South Korea lagging behind in the global race to develop competitive digital asset ecosystems.

## What the delay means for the market

The legislative impasse prolongs regulatory uncertainty for crypto companies and investors operating in South Korea. At the same time, the decision over who controls stablecoin issuance could have long-term implications for the balance of power between traditional banks and the fintech sector.

South Korea’s debate reflects a broader global question: **should stablecoins primarily extend the traditional banking system, or should they serve as a regulated space for technological innovation?**
