# Robotaxis Without Power: What Happened in San Francisco and What It Means for Autonomous Driving

During a major **power outage in San Francisco**, Waymo’s robotaxis drew widespread attention after numerous autonomous vehicles came to a halt at intersections with disabled traffic lights, causing congestion and disruption in several parts of the city.

## Why did the robotaxis stop?

Waymo explained that its autonomous driving system treats inactive traffic lights as **four-way stops**, the same rule human drivers are expected to follow. In theory, this should allow traffic to continue flowing even during a blackout.

### The problem of excessive caution

In practice, however, the system proved to be **overly conservative**. A large number of robotaxis simultaneously requested additional confirmation from Waymo’s fleet response team to verify that their interpretation of the situation was correct. Because the outage affected large areas at once, these confirmation requests surged, creating bottlenecks and leaving vehicles stalled at intersections.

## How does Waymo plan to fix the issue?

Waymo acknowledged that this confirmation system was designed “out of an abundance of caution” during the early stages of robotaxi deployment and **no longer fits the scale of today’s fleet**.

### A software update as the solution

The company announced a **software update** that will provide autonomous vehicles with more context about regional power outages. The goal is to allow the system to act **more decisively in emergency situations**, without relying on human intervention, while maintaining high safety standards.

## How serious was the disruption?

While videos of stalled robotaxis quickly went viral, Waymo noted that its vehicles **successfully navigated more than 7,000 intersections with disabled traffic lights** on the same day. Still, the highly visible failures highlighted how challenging edge cases remain for autonomous driving systems.

## How would other robotaxi systems cope?

The incident naturally raises questions about how **European autonomous mobility projects** would perform under similar conditions. In Europe, discussions around robotaxis and autonomous vehicles often involve manufacturers such as **Rimac from Croatia**, as well as other European technology and automotive initiatives working on autonomous mobility systems.

The key question remains unresolved: **are these vehicles already operating on public roads**, and how would they respond to extraordinary situations such as large-scale power outages or failures of urban infrastructure? Unlike Waymo, whose robotaxis operate daily in real traffic, most European projects are still in testing phases, pilot programs, or limited demonstrations. As a result, their resilience in real-world crisis scenarios remains — **unproven**.

## A lesson for the entire industry

The San Francisco blackout served as a **real-world stress test** for autonomous driving. It showed that self-driving vehicles must not only be able to drive under normal conditions, but also function reliably when infrastructure fails and established rules suddenly become unclear.
