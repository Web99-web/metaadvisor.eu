# Money in the bank is not yours — it is the bank’s property. What you have is only a claim.

## 📌 THE TRUTH
**The moment you deposit money into a bank, it is no longer yours — it becomes the bank’s property.  
You no longer have money — you have only a claim.**

This is not a conspiracy theory, but an official accounting standard that has existed for more than 500 years.

In practice, this means:

- the bank can use your money for loans and investments  
- you hold a claim, not actual ownership  
- if the bank collapses — you become a creditor  

In the European Union:

> **Deposits are legally insured up to €100,000 per person per bank.**

Anything above that enters the bankruptcy estate.

In the United States:

> **FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000 per person per bank.**

In extraordinary cases (SVB, Signature) higher amounts were covered — but that is not the norm.

## 📌 The greatest illusion of the modern financial system — people believe their money is waiting safely in the bank

When you deposit money into a bank, it seems like it simply “sits there.”  
But the reality is completely different.

The money you think you have is **not sitting in a vault**. It is **not there at all**.  
This is one of the oldest and most important concepts in finance — yet almost no one explains it to ordinary people.

This analysis uncovers:
•	where your money actually ends up  
•	what fractional reserve banking really is  
•	why banks can fail despite “holding billions in deposits”  
•	why transfers are slow and what settlement means  
•	and why U.S. money moves as if it were still the 1970s

## 📌 The illusion of “safe money”

When you deposit money into a bank, most people imagine:
•	a “box” with their name on it  
•	a vault where their cash is stored  
•	a digital “account” functioning like a safe  

But in reality:  
The money in your bank account is **not your property** — it is a **liability of the bank** to you.

You own only a claim (liability).  
**The money you “have” is not sitting anywhere. It is already circulating.**

The bank:
•	invests it in bonds  
•	lends it out as credit  
•	uses it for interbank settlements  
•	allocates it into liquidity pools  

Your money is not waiting for you —  
**because it is not there.**

## 🏦 1) What actually happens when you deposit €1,000,000?

When you deposit one million euros, the bank does NOT do this:

❌ it does not store the million in a vault  
❌ it does not keep it “on standby”  
❌ it does not hold it separately from other customers' funds  

Instead:

✔ your million becomes **the bank’s property (assets)**  
✔ and the bank owes you one million (**liabilities**)  

In other words:

> **You hold a claim — not money.**

### Where is the “physical million”?

Most commonly:

- 0 € in cash  
- a small portion in the branch  
- the rest allocated into loans, bonds and financial instruments  

If you want proof that banks do not hold your money:

> **Imagine all clients withdrawing their money on the same day — that is a bank run.**

## 🧱 2) Fractional reserve — the key mechanism that powers the entire system

Historically, banks never held 100% of deposits — only a fraction.  
Modern systems no longer operate with fixed reserve requirements, but the principle remains:

> **Banks lend out many times more money than they hold in deposits.**

How the mechanism works:

1. Your deposit (your money) enters the bank  
2. The bank issues a loan based on it  
3. The loan becomes a new deposit in another bank  
4. That bank issues a new loan  
5. Money multiplies  

This is the foundation of the modern monetary system.  
It is called bank-created money.

This means:
•	deposits are not cash  
•	they are the result of credit expansion  

Therefore: deposit ≠ cash.  
•	the banking system is structurally fragile  
•	it remains stable only as long as there is no panic  

And this is why central banks have enormous power:  
they control the conditions under which banks create new money.

## 💶 3) Where does “physical money” go?

This is one of the most fascinating parts.  
Physical money represents only:

•	2–5% of the total money supply  
•	the rest is digital, book-entry money  
•	it does not exist as cash in vaults  

Your “million” may physically exist as:

- €3,000 in a local branch  
- €7,000 in a central treasury  
- €0 if the bank operates fully digitally  

This is why banks are NOT afraid of “empty vaults” —  
they are afraid of simultaneous mass withdrawals (bank runs).

Because:  
The banking system functions only as long as the majority of customers **do not want their money at the same time**.

## 📡 5) Authorization vs. Settlement — the key to understanding the system

### ✔ Authorization (instant)
- the merchant receives an “OK”  
- the bank reserves the funds  
- the user sees the transaction  

### ❌ Settlement (actual movement of money)
in the EU: 1–2 days  
in the USA: 2–5 days  

Therefore:
- refunds are delayed  
- account balances are not final  
- merchants do not receive funds immediately  
- banks have room for controls

## 📡 6) SWIFT vs. SEPA — why transfers are not instant

🔹 **SEPA (EU)**  
•	the message is sent instantly  
•	but actual settlement can take longer  
•	SEPA Instant exists, but not all banks support it  
•	banks sometimes “slow down” payouts intentionally (liquidity management)  

🔹 **SWIFT (global)**  
SWIFT is NOT a money-transfer system —  
it is a **messaging system**.

Example message:  
“This bank wants to send 100,000 USD to that bank.”

But the real movement of funds happens via:
•	correspondent banks  
•	balance adjustments  
•	netting systems  
•	1–5 days later  

🔹 **USA: the slowest major financial system in the developed world**  
Most U.S. payments still go through:
•	ACH (slow)  
•	Fedwire (fast but expensive)  

This is why even domestic transfers often take 1–3 days in the U.S.

## 🔐 7) Why banks block or delay withdrawals

Because of:
- AML checks  
- annual audits  
- liquidity management  
- suspected fraud  
- regulatory restrictions  

And because:

> **the money in your account is not your property — it is a claim that the bank can temporarily restrict.**
