Bitcoin has spent the past several days trading in an increasingly narrow range between roughly $85,000 and $89,000, giving the impression of a market that is both resilient and fatigued. After briefly dipping below $85,000, the price rebounded quickly, yet every attempt to break above $89,000 has met strong selling pressure, particularly during U.S. trading hours.

This pattern has repeated almost mechanically: Bitcoin tends to strengthen after U.S. markets close, only to face renewed selling as the trading day progresses. Analysts interpret this as a mix of short-term traders using the $89,000 level to take profits and institutional participants who remain reluctant to push prices into a new range without a clear catalyst.

At the same time, ongoing volatility is increasingly affecting companies and funds that hold Bitcoin as a core balance-sheet asset. Their valuations now fluctuate sharply with short-term price movements, complicating capital raising and long-term planning. As a result, 2025 is increasingly being framed as the year that will separate sustainable business models from those relying solely on rising Bitcoin prices.

Despite near-term pressure, the long-term institutional narrative remains notably strong. Citigroup, for example, sees a base-case scenario in which Bitcoin could rise toward $143,000 over the next 12 months, supported by ETF inflows and a more favorable regulatory environment in the United States. At the same time, the bank clearly outlines downside risks, including the possibility of significant declines in the event of a global recession.

For now, the market is not struggling with a lack of capital, but with a lack of conviction for a decisive breakout. Bitcoin sits between short-term selling pressure and long-term institutional expectations, and the resolution of this tension will determine whether the $85,000–$89,000 range becomes a launchpad — or a prolonged zone of consolidation.
