In recent months, some active LinkedIn users have noticed an unusual decline in the reach and visibility of their posts, particularly after the platform expanded its use of artificial intelligence and large language models (LLMs) to rank content in users’ feeds. Suspicion intensified following a series of personal experiments that quickly gained public attention.

As part of an informal test, several users temporarily changed their profile gender from female to male. According to their accounts, post visibility increased almost immediately — in some cases by more than 200% within a single day. Since the content, posting frequency, and audience remained unchanged, many concluded that gender was the only variable.

LinkedIn has officially denied these claims. The company states that its algorithms and AI systems do not use demographic attributes such as gender, age, or race as signals for determining content visibility. It also emphasizes that side-by-side feed comparisons are not representative and that reach is influenced by multiple factors, including timing and audience engagement.

Nevertheless, experts warn that algorithmic bias does not need to be intentional or explicit. If models are trained on historical data that already reflect social inequalities, those patterns can be unintentionally reinforced. This is why the debate extends beyond LinkedIn and raises broader questions about algorithmic transparency on platforms that increasingly shape professional visibility and opportunity.

### Our Take (MetaAdvisor)
- Algorithmic bias is often a data problem rather than a rule-based one.
- Platforms rarely provide enough transparency to fully address such concerns.
- While informal, these experiments highlight a real trust issue.
- As AI increasingly controls visibility, platform accountability becomes critical.
