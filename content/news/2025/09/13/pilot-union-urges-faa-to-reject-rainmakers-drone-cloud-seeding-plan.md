**Rainmaker Technology’s** bid to equip small drones with **cloud-seeding flares** is facing pushback from the **Air Line Pilots Association (ALPA)**, which urged the **FAA** to **deny** the request unless the startup accepts **tighter safety rules**.

The FAA’s ruling will signal how the regulator views **weather modification by unmanned systems** going forward — a pivotal call for Rainmaker’s **small-UAS** approach.

In its filing, ALPA says the petition **“fails to demonstrate an equivalent level of safety”** and poses **“an extreme safety risk.”**

CEO **Augustus Doricko** countered in an email that the union’s objections rely only on the **public notice**, while **non-public documents** submitted to the FAA detail the company’s **safety data, risk mitigations and operating procedures**.

Rainmaker is seeking an **exemption** from rules barring **hazardous materials** on **small drones**. Filed in July, the petition prompted an FAA **request for information** focused on operations and safety, rather than an immediate ruling.

The plan proposes two flare types — **burn-in-place** and **ejectable** — mounted on the **Elijah** quadcopter to disperse particles that **stimulate precipitation**. **Elijah’s** **maximum altitude is 15,000 feet MSL**, inside **controlled airspace** used by commercial airliners; flights there require **ATC authorization**.

Rainmaker says it will operate in **Class G** (uncontrolled) airspace **unless otherwise authorized**. **ALPA** notes the filing does not clearly specify **where** flights would occur or at what **altitudes**. Doricko says those details are covered in the materials sent to the FAA, including **ATC coordination** and work with local **meteorological services**.

**What’s next?** After reviewing responses, the FAA can approve the exemption, attach **additional conditions** (e.g., transponder/Detect-and-Avoid, NOTAM procedures, weather windows, geofencing) or **deny** the request. The outcome could set a **precedent** for **drone-based cloud seeding** in the U.S.

## Our take
- **Safety vectors:** pyrotechnics on a UAS, operating altitudes near **commercial corridors**, weather, and **ATC comms**. Minimums should include **Detect-and-Avoid**, **Remote ID/transponder**, **NOTAMs**, and strict **geofencing**.  
- **Regulatory:** without clear **LOA/COA** processes, a safe **jettison** protocol and verifiable **risk assessment**, approval is a stretch.  
- **Environment/liability:** flare chemistry, off-target precipitation and **accountability** will draw scrutiny from local authorities.  
- **Bottom line:** operational upside (more precise, cheaper than crewed aircraft) exists, but the safety bar must be **as high as** for manned ops — especially in **controlled** airspace.
