London’s tone is soothing: **no copy-paste from TradFi, but ‘tailoring’ for crypto**. Sensible on the surface, sure. Yet the real question remains: **who gets to be the gatekeeper, and on what terms?**

If the bar is raised so high that only mega-custodians and bank-aligned exchanges can clear it, that’s less about protection and more about **rebuilding the market around incumbent gatekeepers**. Genuine protection is **proportionate and tech-neutral**: it shields retail from fraud and poor custody without smothering **self-custody, P2P, and open source**. Notions like “operational resilience” are valid—until they morph into **excessive reporting, closed interfaces**, and de-facto bans on non-intermediated solutions.

Put simply: **define the exemptions, define the boundaries**. A framework with explicit **safe harbors** for open source, interoperability, and the right to self-custody preserves innovation. Without them, “tailoring” becomes **control by cadence**: **market access for the compliant**, shrinking edges for everyone else.

Over time, the price of “clarity” may be another **layer of centralisation at on/off-ramps and front-ends**—the points where a decentralised story most easily turns into **permissioning**. In the meantime, the industry does gain a clearer licensing map; the test is **who it truly lets in—and who it quietly pushes out**.

### Our take (Metaadvisor)
- **Implementation over rhetoric:** watch who actually gets licensed—and at what cost.  
- **Protection without suffocation:** demand **safe harbors** for open source, interoperability, and self-custody.  
- **Soft centralisation risk:** gatekeeping power over intermediaries extends banking influence into crypto.  
- **Builder’s playbook:** plan licensing early and **remove single points of failure** (front-ends, fiat ramps).
- History suggests that “protection” can be the velvet glove of centralisation. If requirements are ratcheted up to the point where only mega-custodians and bank-aligned exchanges can operate, that looks less like care for users and more like redesigning the market for incumbent gatekeepers. Genuine protection is proportional and tech-neutral: it shields retail from fraud and poor custody without smothering self-custody, P2P, and open source. Red flags for “control” appear when broad notions like “operational resilience” morph into excessive reporting, closed interfaces, and de-facto bans on non-intermediated solutions. By contrast, a framework with explicit safe harbors for open source, interoperability, and the right to self-custody is protection that preserves innovation. Bottom line: ignore the rhetoric, watch the implementation — who the rules actually let in, and who they quietly push out.
