**TL;DR:** Ahead of **Korea Blockchain Week**, a **KRW stablecoin** gained political momentum as a **domestic digital alternative** to dollar tokens. But **capital controls** across much of Asia prevent **offshore circulation**, leaving **Hong Kong’s HKD** as the region’s only **practically usable** base.

### Politics vs. economics
Proponents stress **monetary sovereignty** and reduced reliance on **USD tokens**. Yet post-**1997** safeguards against **capital flight** mean **internationalizing KRW** is **not** the goal — which also curtails a KRW stablecoin’s **global utility**.

Elsewhere, **TWD** is effectively **trapped onshore**, **RMB** is **partly convertible** and tightly managed. In practice, most local-currency stablecoins become **domestic tools** with **limited international use**.

### Why Hong Kong stands out
**Hong Kong** combines **SAR status**, a **USD peg** and **HKMA** licensing/supervision — enabling an **HKD stablecoin** with **clear issuance, reserve and oversight rules**, and **real usability** beyond Hong Kong. The **legal + FX** conditions line up.

### “Sandbox” or nothing
Without **special zones/sandboxes** that **mimic Hong Kong’s conditions** (freer flows, convertibility, reporting), **KRW and peers** remain **onshore-only**. That still helps **domestic payments**, but misses the **regional/global** use-case.

### About those dollar examples
**Re-dollarization** via **USDT** in some markets is politically **unpalatable**: it weakens sovereignty and shifts control of rails. Hence a KRW stablecoin is framed as **defensive**, but those same safeguards **limit** international utility.

---

## Conclusion
The **paradox** is clear: the tighter you protect the **capital account**, the less likely your currency can underpin a **globally usable** stablecoin. **Hong Kong** is the **exception**: **special status + FX regime + clear oversight**. For others, the pathway offshore is a **well-scoped sandbox** — or accepting a **domestic-first** role.
