On Western high streets, bank windows glow with percentages: 3%, 4%, 5% on deposits. In many cities, those numbers don’t glow — they go dark overnight. Today the rate is “safe,” tomorrow the account is under **moratorium**, the currency drifts, or the border turns back into a wall. In that world, asking why Bitcoin **doesn’t pay yield** feels like a textbook objection, not a real-life answer.  
*Image is illustrative.*

The story is simple. **Yield** means you **lend** to someone and trust you’ll be repaid with interest. In stable systems that “someone” is the state or a bank; trust comes easily. In fragile systems that “someone” often is **risk** itself: devaluation, withdrawal freezes, sudden rules. That’s why an asset that needs **no permission** and **no issuer** can feel liberating. Bitcoin doesn’t pay interest — it offers something else: **a base layer**. Scarcity by design, **self-custody**, and borderless settlement.

Picture a home safe. It doesn’t pay interest, but it’s **yours**. No one can pause it, relocate it, or “borrow it for a bit.” The moment you carry that safe out of your house so it can “work for you,” the story becomes **credit**: to whom did you hand it, what do you get back, and how do you unwind if it breaks? Bitcoin is the same: once you chase yield you enter the world of **intermediaries**, **collateral**, and **maturities**. That’s not bad — just a different game governed by finance, not by the protocol.

So why the fixation on yield in comfortable systems? Because we’re **measuring with the wrong ruler**. If the benchmark is a U.S. Treasury that pays like a metronome, Bitcoin will always look “inferior.” Talk instead to a shopkeeper under capital controls, to a family that survived a **currency haircut**, or to a freelancer whose payout was “temporarily” frozen. For them, “no yield” often reads as **“no middleman who can be coerced.”** That’s value.

If you **need income**, you don’t have to dismiss Bitcoin; you **place it correctly**. A foundation isn’t built to pay rent; it’s built so the house stands while the upper floors (bonds, deposits, business cash flows) do their job. With Bitcoin as the foundation, it’s easier to choose which floors to live on and whom to let in as a tenant — and on what terms.

### Our take (Metaadvisor)

In stable worlds, “Bitcoin has no yield” sounds reasonable — but **the measuring stick is wrong**. Bitcoin works as an **issuer-free savings base**; turning it into a bond is entering the **credit business**, and it should be managed as such. For households a **two-layer** setup is robust: a core in **self-custody** as quiet savings, and yield in assets built for yield — that you actually understand. For institutions it’s the same, just more complex: yield on BTC is a function of **intermediaries and protocols**; if you want it, you must manage **collateral, liquidity and regulatory** risks.  
With Bitcoin, “no yield” is **by design**, not a bug — and for many outside the financial capitals it’s a **feature**, not a flaw.
