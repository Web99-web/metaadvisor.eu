## What’s happening
Sora 2 went viral by letting anyone generate professional-looking videos in minutes — including scenes featuring deceased public figures. “Historical figures” are defined broadly, from kings and politicians to recently deceased celebrities. The living require consent; the dead are excepted.

Examples include **Henry VIII on a date**, **Princess Diana as a DJ**, and **Karl Marx at a mall**. The line between satire, entertainment, marketing and deception is blurring fast.

## Legal gray areas
- **Post-mortem publicity rights** — the commercial right to one’s likeness after death exists in some jurisdictions and not in others.  
- **False endorsement** — creating the impression that a person/estate endorses a product or message.  
- **Reputation** — the deceased often lack classic defamation protection; estates/relatives may act via trademark, consumer law or moral rights.  
- Bottom line: what’s legal in one country may be contentious in another.

## Ethics
- **Dignity & grief**: “reviving” the tragically deceased can harm families and the public.  
- **Revisionism**: AI videos can implant false memories and rewrite history.  
- **Propaganda**: historical leaders and activists are easily instrumentalized.

## How we got here
- **Zelig (1983)** — Woody Allen composited characters into historical footage (manual VFX).  
- **Forrest Gump (1994)** — Tom Hanks “meets” JFK, Lennon and others; manual VFX with licenses and rights cleared.  
- **2000s/2010s** — digital doubles and de-aging (*Benjamin Button*, *Irishman*, *Rogue One*).  
- **Today** — generative AI does this **in minutes, at scale, often without licenses**.

## Technical guardrails
Watermarks, deepfake detection and “synthetic media” labels exist — but are easily lost once videos are re-uploaded elsewhere.

## Our take (Metaadvisor)
- **Legal**: the “historical figures” carve-out isn’t a free pass — context and commercial benefit matter.  
- **Ethical**: the more recent and tragic the death, the higher the duty of care.  
- **Technical**: without interoperable labels and fast response, virality outruns regulation.  
- **Bottom line**: “can be made” ≠ “should be published.”
