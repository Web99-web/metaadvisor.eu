This morning it started almost unnoticed: some apps took forever to load, emails didn’t get through, and online shops showed empty carts. Soon it became clear: **Microsoft Azure** and **Amazon AWS** were experiencing major issues. **Google Cloud** and **Cloudflare** also showed disruptions – and when multiple core providers falter at once, it feels for many like the **internet itself is down**.

What looks like a small technical glitch quickly becomes a **chain reaction**: payment services wait for confirmations that never arrive; shops can’t issue receipts; logistics lose track of stock and routes. Users only see “slow internet” – in the background, queues and retries overwhelm systems, pushing them into degraded mode.

The hardest hit are companies with **no backup plan**. No cash till, no paper invoices, no defined emergency procedures – they grind to a halt. Those better prepared simply pull out their **offline runbook**: paper receipts, per-customer limits, daily logs and fallback communication channels.

At this point it’s worth revisiting our base article — **[“What if the internet went down worldwide”](/en/news/what-if-the-internet-went-down-worldwide)** — which explains why the net isn’t a central switch and why regional outages can still paralyze daily life. Today’s incident proves it: no global blackout, but enough to affect millions.

The cause lies in **over-reliance on a few providers**. Centralization brings efficiency and lower costs, but also **systemic risk**. The answer is not panic or full retreat, but **resilience**: multi-region setups, multiple providers, and clear offline processes.

**Our take (Metaadvisor)**  
Today’s outage isn’t the end of the world, but it is a **wake-up call**. Those with simple offline options keep working. Those who rely solely on “always online” stand still – with their customers waiting in line.
