## Tornado Cash: a trial that could redefine crypto privacy

The Tornado Cash case has evolved from a technical debate into a legal turning point for the entire Web3 ecosystem. At the center is Roman Storm, one of the three developers who launched the Ethereum mixer in 2019. His co-founders, Roman Semenov and Alexey Pertsev, are outside U.S. jurisdiction and face their own legal battles, while Storm stands before a U.S. federal court.

What began as a tool for digital privacy quickly became — according to U.S. authorities — a system exploited by some of the world’s most notorious cybercriminals, including the North Korean Lazarus Group. As a result, Tornado Cash has become the focal point of the most significant privacy-related court case in crypto history.

## How Tornado Cash actually worked

At its core, Tornado Cash functioned like a digital washing machine. A user deposited funds, the system mixed them with other deposits, and then returned “clean” funds with no visible connection to the original wallet. In a fully transparent blockchain world, this offered a rare layer of privacy.

However, the same mechanism made it attractive for illicit actors. According to the DOJ, the Lazarus Group laundered hundreds of millions of dollars through Tornado Cash — including assets stolen during the historic Ronin / Axie Infinity hack.

This is where the project stopped being a technical experiment and became a national security concern.

## The DOJ’s key argument: Tornado Cash was not decentralized

The defense claims the developers merely wrote open-source code. But prosecutors presented evidence that Storm and his partners were not passive contributors — they were operators.

The DOJ states that the developers:

- modified the official UI **over 250 times** between 2020 and 2022  
- had full control over hosting and domain access  
- actively adjusted functions that governed how users interacted with the mixer  

According to court transcripts:

> “At least 96% of Tornado Cash users accessed the service through the developer-controlled UI.”

From a regulatory perspective, this is not decentralization — it is operational control. And that is the cornerstone of the prosecution’s case.

## Why the “neutral code” argument failed

There is a difference between general-purpose software (like Linux) and software designed primarily to obscure financial flows. Tornado Cash clearly falls into the latter category. That is why the DOJ argues that “neutrality” cannot serve as a defense.

## Mixing vs Zero-Knowledge: the critical technological distinction

Tornado Cash relied on mixing — a method long associated with money laundering.

Modern privacy solutions work differently. They use **Zero-Knowledge (ZK) cryptography**, such as the **[Zero-Knowledge technology](https://metaadvisor.eu/news/zero-knowledge-zk-technology-explained/)**, which enables privacy without mixing funds and without centralized points of control.

Prominent ZK systems include Zcash, zkSync, StarkNet, Aztec, Scroll, Polygon zkEVM, Axiom and others.

### Why this matters

Zero-Knowledge systems are **decentralized**. No one can:

- change the rules  
- shut down access  
- decide who can or cannot use the protocol  
- influence transaction flow  

Even Mastercard, which uses ZK proofs for identity validation, does not control the ZK technology itself. It uses mathematical proofs that exist independently of any central authority.

## A case that shapes the future of Web3 privacy

The combination of UI control, frequent modifications, Lazarus-related laundering, and regulatory pressure transformed Tornado Cash into the most controversial crypto court case of the decade. The outcome of Roman Storm’s trial will become a global precedent influencing how privacy, open-source development and decentralization are interpreted in the years ahead.

## Our Commentary (Metaadvisor):

- The ruling may redefine the boundary between developer responsibility and open-source freedom.  
- Zero-Knowledge systems are likely to become the new privacy standard due to the absence of centralized control points.  
- Mixers like Tornado Cash will struggle to survive in the modern regulatory landscape.  
- The case raises a broader question: who is responsible when a tool can be used by both ordinary users and sophisticated criminal groups?
