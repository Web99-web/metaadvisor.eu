South Korea’s largest crypto exchange, **Upbit**, has suffered one of its most severe security incidents to date: around **36 million USD** in **SOL** and **SPL tokens** disappeared from its Solana hot wallet. The incident occurred on November 27 and immediately drew global attention due to the size of the breach and the importance of the Korean crypto market.

Upbit quickly froze deposits and withdrawals, moved funds to cold storage, and initiated an investigation with regulators and on-chain forensics teams. Early blockchain analysis points toward the North Korea–linked hacking group **Lazarus**, whose known tactics include rapid fund hopping, the use of mixers, and splitting transactions into smaller fragments to avoid detection.

### What exactly was stolen?
The attack focused on the Solana ecosystem, including:
- SOL  
- SPL tokens  
- additional Solana-based digital assets  

The total value of the stolen assets is estimated at **36–37 million USD**, roughly **54 billion KRW**.

## South Korea reacts immediately: the strictest AML package so far

Just hours after the Upbit hack, the South Korean government introduced a new, extremely stringent anti-money-laundering package (AML – *Anti-Money Laundering*).

The key change is the expansion of the **Travel Rule** — a regulation requiring crypto exchanges to share sender and recipient information — now extended to transactions **below 1 million won**, or approximately **680 USD**.

This means:
- Large transfers can no longer be split into smaller ones  
- Identity reporting is mandatory even for small crypto transactions  
- Korean exchanges must significantly upgrade their KYC/AML systems  

### Additional measures include:
- Blocking access to “high-risk” offshore crypto exchanges  
- Stricter financial audits for exchanges seeking **VASP** (*Virtual Asset Service Provider*) status  
- Banning individuals with drug-related or tax-related criminal records from becoming major VASP shareholders  

Regulators say the goal is to curb the use of crypto for tax evasion, drug trafficking, and cross-border illicit payment schemes.

## Why did South Korea respond so fast?

South Korea is one of the most heavily regulated and technologically advanced crypto markets in the world. The combination of:

- a major hack  
- a potentially state-sponsored attacker (Lazarus)  
- growing illegal crypto flows  

… triggered a swift and uncompromising political reaction.

## Our take (MetaAdvisor)

- The Upbit hack shows that even the most regulated exchanges remain vulnerable — especially in fast-finality ecosystems like Solana.  
- The suspicion of Lazarus underlines the increasing role of state-sponsored cyberattacks in the crypto world.  
- Expanding the Travel Rule to very small transactions could influence global regulation — other countries may follow.  
- Korean users will face stricter KYC requirements and reduced anonymity, likely boosting interest in decentralized alternatives.  
- Korea often sets regulatory precedents that later spread to Japan, Singapore, and even the EU.
