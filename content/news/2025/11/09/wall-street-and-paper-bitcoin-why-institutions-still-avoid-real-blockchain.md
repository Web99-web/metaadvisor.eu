Wall Street’s appetite for crypto is stronger than ever.  
Bitcoin ETFs like **BlackRock’s** have broken inflow records.  
**Fidelity** and **VanEck** launched their own spot products, while **Nasdaq** has signaled plans to expand its digital asset infrastructure.  
Yet despite this momentum, almost none of it happens **on the blockchain**.

Institutions now treat crypto as a **legitimate asset class**, but not as a **place to operate**.  
Most trading, settlement, and price discovery still occur on **private servers** and traditional rails, far from the transparency of public chains.

The reason is simple: current blockchains **don’t meet institutional performance standards**.  
Until they can offer predictable speed, data reliability, and operational resilience comparable to Wall Street systems, major players will continue to trade **off-chain**, limiting transparency, liquidity, and innovation.

---

### Wall Street and “Paper Bitcoin” — trading off the blockchain

While retail investors buy Bitcoin directly and wait for confirmations, institutions play by different rules.  
On Wall Street, Bitcoin often trades **without a single transaction touching the blockchain**.

When **BlackRock** or **Fidelity** receive new inflows into their ETF funds, their custodian — often **Coinbase Custody** — **doesn’t have to buy the Bitcoin immediately**.  
Instead, an obligation for the amount and price is recorded.  
The actual purchase may occur within a day or two, depending on market conditions and settlement processes.  
This *off-chain* bookkeeping allows flexibility in timing and reduces costs, but it doesn’t imply active speculation on price differences.  
ETFs and custodians are under strict regulatory oversight and cannot trade the fund’s assets for profit.

Thanks to T+1 or T+2 settlement rules (trade settled within one or two days), institutions can execute and finalize transfers without relying on blockchain speed.  

Unlike the 24/7 crypto market, **ETF trading follows the traditional schedule — weekdays only, during exchange hours**.  
If Bitcoin moves sharply over the weekend, ETF prices reflect those changes only on Monday, creating the so-called **ETF gap**.

---

### Why Wall Street still loves paper money

Despite the rise of crypto and promises of decentralization, large institutions **trust only what they can control**.  
Paper, databases, and private systems provide what blockchains still can’t fully guarantee:  
- **speed**,  
- **predictability**,  
- **legal protection**,  
- and the ability to **reverse transactions if something goes wrong**.

On the blockchain, there’s no “undo.”  
Once a transaction is sent, it’s permanent.  
For financial giants used to reversible systems, that’s simply too much risk.

So Wall Street today loves Bitcoin — but not its essence.  
They buy it, store it, and resell it — but **avoid the fully transparent, irreversible world that blockchain represents**.  
Ironically, the very system Bitcoin was meant to disrupt is now using it — **on its own terms**.
