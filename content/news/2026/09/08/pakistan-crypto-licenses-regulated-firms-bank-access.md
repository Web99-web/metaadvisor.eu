# Pakistan Introduces Crypto Licenses: Firms Without Applications Must Stop Operating, While Regulated Providers Gain Bank Access

**Pakistan has drawn a clear line for the crypto industry: companies that want to continue operating in the country must enter the regulatory system, but in return they gain access to something that has long been difficult for the crypto sector to obtain — the formal banking system.** Existing virtual asset service providers that were operating in Pakistan on March 5, 2026 or earlier had until **September 5** to submit an application for a No Objection Certificate, or NOC, or stop operating.

The deadline has now passed, and the Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority, or **PVARA**, is continuing to implement a system under which crypto exchanges, wallet operators, custodians and other virtual asset service providers can no longer legitimately operate outside the formal regulatory framework. Pakistan is not moving toward a crypto ban. Instead, the country is trying to shift the sector from a regulatory grey area into a system of licensing, supervision and banking access.

## Deadline Passed: NOC or Stop Operating

PVARA set a clear deadline for so-called transitional operators — companies that were already providing virtual asset services in Pakistan on March 5, 2026 or earlier. **By September 5, they had to submit an NOC application or stop providing covered services in the country.**

An NOC, or No Objection Certificate, is a preliminary regulatory approval that allows a company to enter the formal licensing process. Filing an application does not mean the company automatically receives a full operating license, but it allows the regulatory process to continue and, if the required conditions are met, opens the path toward a final VASP license.

PVARA states that all Virtual Asset Service Providers must hold the appropriate regulatory approval before offering services in Pakistan. Operating without the required authorization is therefore no longer treated as a regulatory grey area, but as a breach of the new legal framework.

## An NOC Is Only the First Step Toward a Full Crypto License

The path toward a full license consists of several stages. A company must first submit a business plan, corporate documents, information about owners and key personnel, as well as documentation related to financial capability, AML/CFT systems and technological infrastructure.

After the NOC is issued, additional regulatory obligations follow, including registration with Pakistan’s **Financial Monitoring Unit**, compliance with the relevant AML requirements and establishment of a local legal entity under Pakistan’s Companies Act.

Only after that does the company enter the final stage of obtaining a full **VASP license**. In other words, Pakistan is not introducing a simple registration process that allows companies to sign up once and continue operating as before. It is building a conventional multi-stage regulatory framework for virtual assets.

## Regulated Crypto Companies Gain Access to Banks

The most interesting part of Pakistan’s new model is not the licensing requirement itself, but what regulated companies receive in return. **The State Bank of Pakistan allows banks and other regulated financial institutions to open accounts for VASPs that have the appropriate PVARA approval.**

This significantly changes the relationship between the traditional financial system and the crypto industry. Pakistan restricted banks’ involvement with virtual currencies in 2018, while the new framework now allows formal banking infrastructure for companies that accept licensing, supervision and AML requirements.

Broader banking services related to virtual asset activity are available to **licensed VASPs**, while companies that have only received an NOC can obtain limited-purpose accounts needed to complete formalities and continue the licensing process.

Pakistan is therefore effectively offering a trade-off: **enter the regulatory system and gain access to banks.**

## Bank Access Comes With Strict Restrictions

Opening the banking system to crypto does not mean Pakistani banks can now freely trade Bitcoin, Ethereum or other digital assets. The State Bank of Pakistan has imposed clear limits.

Before opening an account, banks must verify the validity of the PVARA license, carry out risk assessments and continuously monitor the business relationship. Suspicious transactions must be reported to the relevant authorities under anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing rules.

Separate transactional accounts are required for client funds, and company assets must not be mixed with customer funds. Banks are also prohibited from using their own capital or customer deposits to invest in, trade or hold virtual assets.

In other words, the government is opening **banking infrastructure to crypto**, but it is not turning banks themselves into crypto traders.

## Binance and HTX Have Already Entered the Regulatory Process

Pakistan has already shown that the new framework is not merely theoretical. **PVARA has granted NOC approvals to major global crypto exchanges Binance and HTX**, allowing them to proceed to the next stages of the country’s regulatory process.

The NOC does not mean that Binance or HTX automatically received a full operating license. It is a preliminary regulatory clearance that allows an applicant to continue toward AML registration, local incorporation and, ultimately, a full VASP license.

That distinction is important, but so is the broader message. **Pakistan is not trying to push major international crypto companies out of the country. It is trying to bring them inside the domestic regulatory system.**

The involvement of Binance and HTX also gives the framework more significance than a purely domestic licensing exercise. If some of the world’s largest crypto platforms are willing to enter the process, Pakistan is effectively testing whether strict regulation and formal banking access can be combined in a way that remains commercially attractive to major global players.

## Regulation Covers Much More Than Crypto Exchanges

Pakistan’s regulatory framework is not limited to traditional exchanges where users buy and sell Bitcoin or other tokens. PVARA regulates a much broader range of virtual asset service providers.

These include **crypto exchanges, wallet providers, custody services, brokers, virtual asset management, token issuance and other activities related to the exchange, transfer, safekeeping or administration of digital assets**.

Companies must also meet requirements related to capital, governance, the suitability of directors and key personnel, cybersecurity, business continuity and protection of customer assets.

Particular emphasis is placed on **KYC, transaction monitoring, suspicious activity reporting and sanctions screening**, while PVARA requires regulated providers to keep certain transaction and customer records for at least ten years.

## Pakistan Moves From Restricted Banking Access Toward a Regulated Market

The broader picture may be even more important than the September 5 deadline itself. In a relatively short period, Pakistan has moved from a highly restrictive relationship between traditional financial institutions and crypto toward an attempt to create a formal and supervised market.

The new **Virtual Assets Act 2026** established PVARA as a specialized regulator for virtual assets. This was followed by more detailed rules covering licensing, AML/CFT, consumer protection, cybersecurity and various categories of virtual asset services.

New Pakistan Virtual Asset Services Regulations and Activity Specific Regulations were also introduced in August, bringing the regulatory framework closer to a fully operational system.

The result is not deregulation. Quite the opposite: Pakistan is introducing significantly more supervision than before. At the same time, however, crypto companies that meet the requirements are being offered a clearer legal status and access to the infrastructure of the traditional financial system.

## Why Bank Access Matters So Much

A crypto company can have millions of users, advanced technology and a global platform, but without access to banks it is extremely difficult to operate normally in a local market.

It needs accounts for operating expenses, salaries and taxes, as well as channels through which users can deposit or withdraw local currency. Without that infrastructure, users often rely on peer-to-peer markets, intermediaries or offshore structures.

Pakistan’s model attempts to move those flows into a **formal, supervised and traceable financial system**. For the government, this means better control of AML risks and more transparent transactions. For regulated companies, it means more stable operations and stronger links to the banking system.

In that sense, bank access may become a stronger incentive to enter the regulatory framework than the threat of penalties for unregulated activity alone.

## Pakistan Is Sending a Different Message From a Traditional Crypto Ban

At first glance, the rule **“register or stop operating”** may sound like another crackdown on crypto. The wider model, however, points in a different direction.

Pakistan is not saying that crypto companies are unwelcome. The message is that they are no longer welcome **outside the regulatory system**.

Companies that accept licensing, transparent ownership structures, AML/KYC controls, protection of customer assets and continuous supervision gain the ability to operate legally and access banks.

That makes Pakistan an interesting example of a broader global trend in which governments are increasingly moving away from a binary choice between a total ban and a completely unrestricted crypto market. Instead, they are building a third model: **crypto can stay, but it must operate under rules comparable to other regulated financial services.**

The fact that **Binance and HTX have already entered that process** makes the Pakistani experiment particularly interesting. It suggests that regulation does not necessarily have to mean excluding the largest crypto companies — if the framework also offers legal certainty and access to the financial infrastructure they need to operate.
