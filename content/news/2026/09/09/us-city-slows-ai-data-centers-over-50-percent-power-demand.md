# US city slows AI data centers over potential use of nearly 50% of its power capacity

**The US city of Plattsburgh in New York State is considering a one-year moratorium on new AI data centers, crypto mining operations and other energy-intensive computing facilities.**
The issue is not opposition to artificial intelligence itself, but a question that is becoming increasingly important as AI infrastructure expands: **how much of a community's limited electricity capacity should one private data center be allowed to consume, and what should the community receive in return?**
Plattsburgh has access to an electricity allocation of approximately **105 MW**, while one large data-center project could require around **50 MW**.
That means a single project could potentially consume almost **48% of the city's available power allocation**.

## The moratorium would cover AI, crypto and cloud facilities

The proposed **Local Law P-2 of 2026** is not limited to Bitcoin mining.
It would cover several types of high-energy computing facilities, including **AI computing, machine learning, cloud computing, cryptocurrency mining, blockchain validation, data centers, server farms and colocation facilities**.
The threshold is set at **300 kW of connected electrical capacity or more**.
If approved, the city would stop issuing new building, zoning, special-use, site-plan and occupancy approvals needed to establish, expand or convert such facilities for 12 months.
Existing lawful operations could continue operating, but significant expansion requiring new city approvals would be restricted during the moratorium.
The city says it needs time to evaluate the effects of these facilities on the **power grid, infrastructure, municipal finances, cooling requirements, noise, fire safety and surrounding communities**.

## One data center could require almost half of the city's available power

The most important number in this story is not the 300 kW threshold, but the potential **50 MW demand** of a large data-center project.
Plattsburgh Mayor **Wendell Hughes** has said the city has a total electricity allocation of approximately **105 MW**.
A 50 MW project could therefore consume almost **48% of that available capacity** on its own.
For local government, that raises a much broader question than simply whether another technology project can be connected to the grid.
If almost half of the available capacity is allocated to one data center, less room remains for future factories, businesses, housing projects, transport electrification and other major users.
Hughes has argued that the city needs to protect its future energy capacity before allowing new large consumers to take a significant share of that limited resource.

## Plattsburgh has already stopped Bitcoin miners once

This is not the first time Plattsburgh has found itself at the center of a debate over the enormous electricity demand of computing facilities.
In **2018, Plattsburgh became the first US city to introduce a moratorium on new commercial cryptocurrency mining operations**.
The original moratorium was designed to last **18 months**, although it could be lifted earlier once appropriate local rules were established.
Plattsburgh has access to relatively inexpensive hydroelectric power, which made the city particularly attractive to Bitcoin miners.
The problem emerged when large mining operations consumed enough electricity to push the city beyond its low-cost power allocation, forcing it to purchase additional electricity at higher prices.
Then-Mayor Colin Read openly said the moratorium was intended to protect other electricity customers.
Eight years later, Plattsburgh is debating the same underlying problem again, but this time the issue is no longer limited to Bitcoin.

## Bitcoin mining and AI are becoming the same energy problem

Technologically, crypto mining and AI data centers are very different, but from the perspective of the electricity grid the distinction becomes much smaller.
Both require **large amounts of continuous electricity, high-capacity grid connections, transformers, cooling systems and substantial energy infrastructure**.
That is why many companies that spent years building Bitcoin mining infrastructure are now expanding or redirecting part of their business toward **AI and high-performance computing**.
Mining companies already control something that has become extremely valuable to the AI industry: land, large grid connections, transformers, cooling infrastructure and experience operating facilities that consume tens or hundreds of megawatts.
Companies including IREN, Riot, HIVE, Cipher and TeraWulf have increasingly expanded their strategies toward AI and HPC operations.
The line between former Bitcoin mining infrastructure and new AI infrastructure is therefore becoming increasingly blurred.

## Part of the cost of data centers can end up on everyone's electricity bill

**Metaadvisor.eu already examined this issue in detail on April 30, 2026, in our article “Data Centers and Rising Electricity Prices: What’s Really Behind the AI Boom?”**
The central fact remains easy to overlook in the public debate around AI infrastructure: **part of the infrastructure cost created by large data centers can be transferred to other electricity customers**.
This is not only a theoretical or indirect effect.
If a new large consumer requires **new power plants, transmission lines, substations, transformers or grid connections**, and those costs are spread across the wider electricity system, they can appear directly on the **electricity bills of households and businesses**.
People and companies can therefore end up helping to finance AI infrastructure even though they have no direct connection to the data-center project itself.
That is why the question of **who pays for new energy and grid infrastructure** has become an increasingly important regulatory issue.
New York State launched a moratorium on new hyperscale data centers in 2026 while it develops new rules covering grid impacts, cost allocation and the effects on local communities.
Governor Kathy Hochul has specifically argued that New Yorkers should not be forced to pay for additional transmission and other electricity infrastructure required by large data centers.
Plattsburgh experienced a similar dynamic in 2018 when Bitcoin miners pushed the city beyond its inexpensive hydroelectric allocation and forced it to purchase additional electricity at higher prices.
The current situation is not identical because Mayor Hughes has said that existing high-energy users **are not currently raising residents' electricity bills**.
The purpose of the new moratorium is partly to prevent that situation from developing in the future.

## The city only recently tried to regulate these projects

What makes the situation particularly interesting is that Plattsburgh did not initially approach the industry with a complete ban.
In June 2026, the city adopted **Local Law 1-2026**, introducing new rules for energy-intensive facilities including cryptocurrency mining, blockchain validation and large data centers.
One of the main principles behind the law was that the use of limited municipal energy resources should provide an appropriate economic benefit to the city and its residents.
Plattsburgh therefore looks not only at how much electricity a project consumes, but also at how many local jobs, investments and other benefits it creates.
That matters particularly for data centers because enormous capital investment and electricity demand do not automatically produce a proportionally large number of permanent jobs.
Only a few months after introducing those rules, the city is now considering another step: a **complete 12-month pause on new approvals** while broader zoning changes are developed.

## New York is already slowing the largest data centers

The debate in Plattsburgh is not happening in isolation.
The State of **New York launched a one-year moratorium on new hyperscale data centers on July 14, 2026**, becoming the first US state to introduce such a measure.
During that period, the state plans to develop new rules covering electricity demand, grid infrastructure costs, water, environmental impacts and benefits for local communities.
The state measure focuses particularly on very large projects requiring around **50 MW or more**.
New York is also seeking a model under which large data centers would **pay more for their electricity or provide their own energy resources**, rather than automatically shifting the cost of their grid connections onto other consumers.
Plattsburgh is going considerably further at the local level because its proposal would cover specialized high-energy computing facilities beginning at just **300 kW** of connected capacity.

## The AI boom is increasingly becoming an energy story

The largest AI models require increasingly large data centers, and those data centers require increasingly large amounts of electricity.
The race to build AI infrastructure is therefore no longer only about processors, GPUs and software.
Access to **electricity, available grid capacity, transformers, transmission lines, cooling systems, water and land** is becoming just as important.
In some parts of the United States, the question is no longer simply whether a data center can be built, but whether enough grid capacity exists to connect it without requiring enormous additional investment.
Plattsburgh is particularly interesting because it encountered the same problem early through Bitcoin mining and is now seeing the same dynamic return with a much larger wave of AI infrastructure.
The city is effectively moving from regulating a particular technology toward regulating **the electricity demand of computing itself**, regardless of whether that computing is used to mine Bitcoin, train AI models, operate cloud services or perform other high-performance workloads.

## The question is not whether AI should exist, but how much power it should be allowed to take

The debate in Plattsburgh illustrates a problem that is likely to appear more frequently as AI infrastructure continues to expand.
Data centers can bring billions of dollars in investment, tax revenue and new jobs.
At the same time, they can consume an enormous share of available power capacity and require new grid infrastructure that somebody must pay for.
Plattsburgh is therefore not deciding whether artificial intelligence should exist, but **how much of a limited local resource one private project should be allowed to consume**.
When one project could require approximately 50 MW in a city with access to around 105 MW, the issue is no longer simply a business decision between an investor and a utility.
It becomes a question about the future development of the entire local community.
Plattsburgh asked Bitcoin miners the same question in 2018.
Now it is asking the AI industry.
