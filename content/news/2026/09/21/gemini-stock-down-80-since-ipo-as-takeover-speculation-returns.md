# Gemini stock down 80% since IPO as takeover speculation returns

**Shares of U.S. crypto platform Gemini are down about 80% since the company went public, cutting its market capitalization to roughly $753 million. Weaker exchange revenue, declining trading volumes and a substantial drop in assets held on the platform have revived questions about whether Gemini could eventually become a takeover target. At the same time, investors are increasingly looking beyond the struggling exchange business toward assets Gemini still controls: regulatory licenses, custody infrastructure and established customer relationships.**

Gemini was founded by Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss and spent years building a reputation as one of the better-known regulated crypto platforms in the United States. Today, however, investors are focusing less on how much Gemini is worth as a traditional exchange and more on how much value a potential buyer could extract from its existing regulatory and operational infrastructure.

## The stock has collapsed as the exchange business weakened

The decline in Gemini's share price has not happened in isolation. In the second quarter, the company reported a 38% year-over-year decline in exchange revenue, while spot trading volume fell 66% to about $3.8 billion. Assets on the platform dropped from roughly $18.2 billion to $8.4 billion.

Those figures help explain why the market is valuing the company much more cautiously than it did around the time of its public debut. When a platform best known for crypto trading sees both trading activity and customer assets fall sharply, investors tend to reduce the premium they are willing to pay for future growth.

## Total revenue did not collapse, but the business mix is changing

It is important to note that Gemini has not reported a collapse across the entire company. Total revenue in the second quarter rose 37% year over year to $45.5 million, driven largely by strong growth in services revenue. In other words, the problem is not that Gemini has no functioning business left. The issue is that its traditional exchange segment no longer looks like the clear growth engine it once was.

That shift matters for valuation. Investors appear less willing to assign a high multiple to a company whose most recognizable core business is weakening, even if other revenue streams are expanding.

## The real value may increasingly lie in Gemini's licenses

As the exchange business weakens, other parts of Gemini become more important. These include regulatory approvals, custody infrastructure, existing legal entities and established relationships with customers. In the crypto industry, building another trading interface or order book is not particularly difficult. Securing and maintaining regulatory approval in major jurisdictions is far more complicated.

Buying an already regulated platform can therefore be faster and more efficient than spending years applying for licenses from scratch. From that perspective, Gemini could remain strategically valuable even if its core exchange operation is no longer growing as strongly as it once did.

That regulatory position was strengthened again in early September, when Gemini received a Major Payment Institution license in Singapore. Such approvals do not solve the problem of lower trading activity, but they can increase the strategic value of the company's existing infrastructure.

## Hyperliquid is being mentioned, but the idea is neither confirmed nor simple

Hyperliquid has also appeared in discussions around possible strategic scenarios for Gemini. One investment thesis suggested that Gemini's licenses and regulatory framework could potentially serve as a useful gateway for products connected to the Hyperliquid ecosystem. That idea, however, requires significant caution.

First, there is currently no confirmation that Hyperliquid is actually negotiating to acquire Gemini or preparing a formal offer. Second, any such transaction would be structurally complicated because Hyperliquid operates as a decentralized trading protocol, following a DEX model, while Gemini is a centralized and regulated exchange, or CEX.

For that reason, the story should not be interpreted as an automatic bullish signal for HYPE or as evidence that an acquisition is close to happening. At this stage, it is simply speculation about which types of buyers might find Gemini's regulatory infrastructure strategically useful.

## Why takeover speculation is returning now

A steep decline in valuation almost inevitably brings takeover speculation back into focus, especially in an industry where regulatory access is becoming increasingly valuable. If a company was once worth around $4 billion and is now valued at roughly $753 million, potential buyers naturally begin comparing the cost of acquiring the business with the cost of building similar regulatory infrastructure themselves.

That does not mean Gemini will be sold. It does mean the market is no longer looking only at how much trading activity currently takes place on the platform. Investors are also asking how much value another owner could potentially extract from Gemini's licenses, infrastructure and existing market position.

## Gemini is becoming more of a regulatory story than a growth story

For years, Gemini was seen as one of the more institutionally oriented and regulation-focused names in crypto. After the sharp decline in its stock price, the company now looks much less like a traditional growth story.

Instead, its strategic value is increasingly shifting away from rapid trading growth and toward regulation and infrastructure. That is why the roughly 80% decline since the IPO matters beyond the headline number. It shows how dramatically the market's perception of Gemini has changed and why investors are now asking whether the company could be worth more to another owner than as a standalone exchange.
