# Poland Keeps Crypto Veto: President Nawrocki Warns Rules Stricter Than EU MiCA Could Drive Companies Out of Poland

**Poland's parliament has again failed to override the presidential veto on legislation governing the crypto-asset market**, but the most important part of the story is not simply another failed vote. President **Karol Nawrocki** is not opposed to regulating the crypto sector. On the contrary, he argues that regulation is necessary, but warns that Poland's proposal goes significantly further than the European **MiCA framework** and that excessive regulatory costs and broad supervisory powers could encourage crypto companies to move their operations to other EU member states.

That dispute over where European regulation ends and additional national regulation begins is now at the center of Poland's crypto policy. **MiCA already applies across the European Union**, including Poland, but Warsaw has still not passed the domestic legislation needed to designate a national supervisory authority and establish the detailed mechanisms for implementation. The result is an unusual situation in which the European regulatory framework exists, while Poland still lacks a fully functioning domestic institutional structure for applying it.

## Nawrocki: Regulation Yes, But Not MiCA Overregulation

Nawrocki has repeatedly stressed that he **supports regulation of the crypto-asset market and consumer protection**, but considers the government's proposal too far-reaching. During earlier disputes over the legislation, the president described the government's approach as a clear **overregulation of MiCA** and warned that such a system could hurt legitimate companies operating in Poland.

The presidential office has raised concerns about several parts of the law, including **supervisory costs, the regulator's powers and the possibility of blocking websites** operated by crypto service providers. Nawrocki's side argues that the goal should not be to create a system in which operating in Poland becomes more expensive and administratively burdensome than in other EU countries.

That matters because one of MiCA's central goals is to create a common European market for crypto companies. Once a company receives the relevant MiCA authorization in one member state, it can under certain conditions provide services across borders. If one country significantly increases the costs or regulatory risks of operating locally, companies have a practical incentive to seek authorization somewhere else in the EU.

## How Much Further Does Poland's Law Go Than MiCA?

The claim that Poland's proposed law amounts to overregulation is primarily **the position of President Nawrocki and his office**. The Polish government disputes that interpretation and argues that additional national mechanisms are necessary for the regulator to enforce MiCA effectively and protect consumers from fraud and other abuses.

One of the most controversial issues concerns the possibility of **blocking domains and websites linked to unauthorized or illegal crypto services**. The presidential side warns that such powers could be applied too broadly, while supporters of the legislation argue that MiCA itself provides for strong supervisory tools and that national law is mainly needed to establish procedures for using them.

Another major issue is the **cost of supervision**. Critics argue that high fees could make Poland less attractive than other EU jurisdictions. That raises a broader issue of regulatory competition: if the same crypto company can access the single European market from another member state, why would it choose a country where licensing is more expensive or complicated?

## Sejm Again Fails to Override the Presidential Veto

Poland's lower house, the Sejm, voted on Friday on whether to override the presidential veto. **A total of 241 lawmakers voted in favor of overriding it, 198 voted against and three abstained.** A three-fifths majority, or 266 votes, was required, leaving supporters of the law **25 votes short**.

The vote marked another failed attempt by the government to advance its model for regulating the crypto-asset market. Nawrocki has now **vetoed government crypto legislation three times**, arguing that key objections raised by his office have not been addressed.

The president has also put forward his own regulatory approach. His office argues that it would also implement MiCA, but without some of the additional requirements Nawrocki considers excessive. The political dispute is therefore no longer about **"regulation versus no regulation"**, but about **two different models of regulation**.

## Poland Now Has an Unusual MiCA Implementation Problem

The consequences of the political conflict are no longer theoretical. **Poland's MiCA transitional period ended on July 1, 2026**, and the Polish Financial Supervision Authority, KNF, has pointed out that because the national implementing law has not been adopted, the country still has no designated authority with full responsibility for supervising the crypto market.

That means Polish companies that previously operated under older domestic rules cannot simply continue under the former transitional regime. Continuing regulated operations requires MiCA authorization, yet **Poland currently has no competent authority able to issue such licenses to domestic companies**.

At the same time, crypto service providers that have obtained MiCA authorization in another EU member state can, subject to the European framework, provide cross-border services to customers in Poland. This gives additional weight to Nawrocki's competitiveness argument: while Warsaw remains locked in a political dispute over its national framework, companies can choose to establish their regulatory base elsewhere in the EU.

## Zondacrypto Deepens the Political Conflict

The regulatory dispute is unfolding alongside a major investigation connected to the crypto platform **Zondacrypto**, which the government of Prime Minister **Donald Tusk** is using as an argument for stronger supervisory powers. Polish prosecutors released new information on the investigation in early September and sought the detention of additional suspects linked to the case.

Tusk went further immediately before the Sejm vote. Citing information from the investigation and testimony from a key witness, he spoke about alleged political connections involving people linked to Zondacrypto and used the case as evidence that Poland urgently needs stronger oversight of the crypto market.

It is important to distinguish **political allegations from facts established by final court judgments**. The investigation remains ongoing, and the claims presented by Tusk are part of an active criminal case and political debate. That makes Zondacrypto a powerful argument for the government, but it also makes a calm discussion about the long-term design of crypto regulation more difficult.

## Nawrocki Says Zondacrypto Is Not an Argument for This Law

The president rejects attempts to link his veto to the problems surrounding Zondacrypto. Nawrocki argues that the legislation he blocked **would not have solved the specific Zondacrypto case** and that the government is using a major scandal as justification for imposing stricter rules on the entire crypto sector.

His position is that the state must investigate fraud and protect users, but that the response should not be to increase the burden on legitimate companies at the same time. Under this argument, effective investigation of suspicious activity and a well-designed regulatory framework are two different issues, and one should not be used to justify excessive regulation of the other.

The government sees the situation very differently. Tusk's side argues that Zondacrypto demonstrates the consequences of insufficient supervision and that without a clearly designated regulator and adequate powers, the state remains at a disadvantage when dealing with large crypto platforms.

## This Is No Longer Just a Polish Debate

Poland's case could become relevant for other EU member states because it raises a broader question: **how far should national governments go once the European Union has already established a common regulatory framework?**

MiCA was designed to reduce regulatory fragmentation across the EU and allow crypto companies to operate in a single market under common rules. However, member states still have to designate competent authorities, define penalties and procedures, and establish many of the practical details of enforcement.

If those national layers begin to differ significantly, a new form of regulatory competition could emerge. Companies will not compare only taxes and labor costs, but also **the cost of obtaining a MiCA license, the duration of the authorization process, supervisory fees, legal certainty and the powers of local regulators**.

For Poland, that is particularly relevant because it is a large market with a developed technology sector. If domestic companies conclude that obtaining MiCA authorization in another member state is easier, Poland could retain crypto users while losing companies, jobs, tax revenue and part of its fintech industry.

## What Comes After the Third Veto

Following the failed parliamentary vote, the presidential veto remains in force, but the regulatory problem has not been resolved. Poland still needs national legislation that designates the competent authority and establishes a functioning system for issuing MiCA authorizations.

The government can now try to introduce **another version of the law**, seek a compromise with the president or adopt parts of Nawrocki's own proposal. Since neither the government nor the president is advocating an entirely unregulated market, there is at least theoretically room for compromise.

The key question is how far both sides are prepared to move. Time also matters for the crypto industry: while the political dispute continues, **Polish companies do not have the same domestic path to a MiCA license as competitors in member states that have already established competent authorities**.

## Our Take

* **Polish President Karol Nawrocki is not opposed to crypto regulation.** His central argument is that the government's proposal overregulates the European MiCA framework.
* Nawrocki warns that **high regulatory costs and broad supervisory powers could push crypto companies to leave Poland and establish themselves in other EU member states**.
* The Sejm voted **241-198** in favor of overriding the veto, with three abstentions, but fell 25 votes short of the required three-fifths majority.
* This is already the **third presidential veto** of government attempts to regulate Poland's crypto-asset market.
* MiCA already applies in Poland, but after the end of the transitional period the country **still lacks a national supervisory authority able to issue MiCA authorizations to Polish companies**.
* At the same time, companies licensed in other EU member states can provide cross-border services to Polish customers under MiCA rules.
* Donald Tusk's government is using the **Zondacrypto investigation** as an argument for stronger oversight, while Nawrocki argues that problems in one specific case do not justify overregulating the entire sector.
* The key question for the rest of Europe is: **if MiCA is intended to provide a single EU framework, how much additional regulation can one member state impose before companies start moving to other jurisdictions?**
