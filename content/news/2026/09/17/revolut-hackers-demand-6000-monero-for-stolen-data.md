# Revolut attackers demand 6,000 Monero and threaten to sell customer data, while Revolut says it was not contacted directly

**The Revolut data incident has entered a new phase. A group calling itself "iamnotavillain" is demanding 6,000 Monero, worth roughly $3 million, and threatening to sell stolen customer data to other criminal groups if Revolut does not pay. Revolut, however, says the attackers have not contacted the company directly and that no negotiations are taking place.**

New details from Italy further change the picture of the incident. According to Italian news agency ANSA, requests for customer information apparently came through a compromised institutional PEC address linked to the Prefecture of Reggio Calabria and were presented as requests from the Polizia Postale. If confirmed, the attackers did not need to break into Revolut's database. Instead, they appear to have convinced the company to hand over selected customer records through what looked like an official government communication channel.

## ANSA: requests came through an Italian government PEC address

ANSA reports that the messages were sent through an institutional PEC address on the `interno.it` domain linked to the Prefecture of Reggio Calabria, while the requests appeared to come from the Polizia Postale. Such a channel would initially look credible, but Italian reporting points to inconsistencies that could have triggered additional verification.

The Polizia Postale has its own official PEC channels, and ANSA also reports that requests of this kind would normally involve additional legal documentation, such as an order or decree from a judicial authority. Italian investigators are now examining whether the Prefecture's own system, broader Interior Ministry infrastructure or an individual government account was compromised.

## Attackers apparently requested data on selected individuals

According to the Financial Times, the attackers claim they did not simply target Revolut's entire customer database. Instead, they say they used blockchain analysis to identify users with significant crypto holdings and then sent individual requests to Revolut for information on those specific people.

If that account is accurate, the operation was highly targeted: first identify a person who may hold significant crypto assets through blockchain data, then connect that identity with a Revolut account and use the compromised government channel to request their KYC and financial records. At least 680 accounts have reportedly been affected.

## Why was a request involving a Kazakh tennis player not checked more closely?

Among the publicly identified victims is Kazakh tennis player Alexander Shevchenko. The fact that he is not an Italian citizen does not mean Italian authorities could never legitimately request his data. A foreign national could clearly be connected to a lawful investigation in Italy.

However, a request involving such a specific foreign individual should make verification of the legal basis, jurisdiction and sender identity particularly important. Combined with the inconsistencies described by ANSA, including an apparent Polizia Postale request arriving through another institution's PEC address and potentially missing legal documentation, it raises the question of why Revolut did not contact the relevant Italian authority directly at an earlier stage.

## ANSA says the scheme lasted around five months

One of the most troubling new details is ANSA's report that the scheme may have lasted for approximately **five months**, with multiple requests spread across different periods. That would mean this was not one incorrectly assessed message but a method that appears to have been used repeatedly.

There is currently no evidence that the attackers had direct access to Revolut accounts for five months or monitored customer activity in real time. A more likely scenario is that they repeatedly submitted new requests for selected individuals and gradually collected information. If Revolut provided account statements and transaction histories alongside KYC documents, each successful request could have given the attackers a detailed retrospective view of a specific person's financial activity.

## Passports, KYC photos and transaction histories

Based on information published so far and notices sent to affected customers, the data handed over may have included names, dates of birth, home addresses, email addresses, phone numbers, copies of passports and driving licences, KYC photographs, IBANs, account statements and transaction histories.

The Financial Times reports that the group provided a short screen recording as evidence, apparently showing identification documents, KYC images and transaction histories belonging to individual users. Revolut says its internal systems were not compromised and customer funds were not affected.

The combination of these records is nevertheless highly sensitive. If a person has already been identified as holding significant crypto assets and attackers then obtain their home address, identity documents and financial history, the possible risks extend far beyond ordinary online phishing.

## Attackers now demand 6,000 Monero

The group "iamnotavillain" is now publicly demanding **6,000 XMR**, worth around $3 million, and threatening to sell the data to other criminal groups if Revolut does not pay.

The choice of Monero is unlikely to be accidental. Unlike transparent blockchains such as Bitcoin, XMR is designed to obscure key transaction information about the sender, recipient and amount, making financial flows significantly harder to trace.

Revolut, however, says the group has not contacted the company directly and that there are no negotiations. For now, this is therefore a publicly issued ultimatum from the attackers rather than confirmed direct communication between the two sides.

## Revolut is simultaneously applying for a Swiss banking licence

The timing is particularly sensitive for Revolut. On **September 16**, the same day the latest extortion demand became public, the company also confirmed that it had applied for a **Swiss banking licence** and announced plans to invest more than **150 million Swiss francs** in the market.

Revolut already has around 1.3 million customers in Switzerland. The attackers also claim that many of the people they targeted are located in Switzerland and France, although Revolut has not confirmed that geographic breakdown.

Two very different stories are therefore unfolding at the same time. Revolut is trying to expand its regulated banking presence in Switzerland while facing new questions about how it verifies and responds to government requests for sensitive customer information. The licence application is not directly connected to the data incident, but privacy controls, KYC security and procedures for government data requests are unlikely to be irrelevant to regulators.

## The key question is no longer only how the Italian email was compromised

Italian authorities are now investigating how the official PEC channel was compromised and who gained access to government communications infrastructure. For Revolut, however, an equally important question is how requests containing potential inconsistencies were able to pass internal checks for months.

If it is confirmed that the attackers selected individual users, repeatedly requested their KYC and transaction records and were able to use the same method for approximately five months, the incident starts to look less like a single error and more like a weakness in the process used to verify who is requesting the company's most sensitive customer data.

After discovering the incident, Revolut blocked the address used in the requests and notified the relevant government body, law enforcement, regulators and affected customers. Data already handed over, however, cannot be taken back.

* The attackers reportedly did not request the entire customer database, but instead used multiple individual requests to obtain data on selected people, claiming they identified crypto targets through blockchain analysis.
* **ANSA reports that the scheme may have continued for around five months, raising questions about why the sender's identity and legal authority were not independently verified earlier.**
* Kazakh tennis player Alexander Shevchenko is among the affected users. His nationality does not automatically make an Italian request implausible, but together with other irregularities it would make additional verification especially important.
* **There is no evidence that the attackers monitored Revolut accounts in real time for five months. Available information instead points to multiple separate requests targeting selected individuals over time.**
* Revolut says its internal systems and customer funds were not compromised, but KYC and financial records appear to have been handed over and cannot now be revoked.
* **The group is now publicly demanding 6,000 Monero, around $3 million, and threatening to sell the data while Revolut says it has not been contacted directly by the attackers.**
* On **September 16**, Revolut also applied for a Swiss banking licence and announced more than 150 million francs in planned investment, making questions about privacy and control procedures particularly sensitive from a regulatory perspective.
