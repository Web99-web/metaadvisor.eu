# Bank of England Warns of Rising Systemic Risk as Inflation Accelerates Again

**The Bank of England has issued a new official warning that the likelihood of interconnected vulnerabilities across the global financial system “crystallizing” at the same time has increased. The Financial Policy Committee highlighted the combination of the war with Iran and the energy shock, extremely high government bond yields and the rapid growth of debt linked to artificial intelligence. At the same time, new eurozone data show inflation accelerating sharply again, while global markets have so far remained surprisingly resilient.**

The BoE warning does not mean that a new financial crisis has already begun. Its message is different: several risks that could previously be viewed separately have now become large enough that, if they develop at the same time, they could trigger a much stronger reaction across financial markets.

## Bank of England sees growing interconnected risks

The Bank of England’s Financial Policy Committee warned on September 30 that the likelihood of interconnected financial-system vulnerabilities materializing has increased. Among the main risks, it cited renewed escalation in the conflict with Iran, the energy shock, high government bond yields and rapidly rising borrowing linked to AI investment.

The core problem is not necessarily any one of these factors on its own. High energy prices can push inflation higher, higher inflation can keep interest rates elevated for longer, high rates increase financing costs and can simultaneously reduce valuations across risk assets. If these pressures materialize together, market repricing could become significantly sharper.

## AI-related debt has risen to around $450 billion

The Bank of England specifically highlighted the rapid growth of debt-financed AI infrastructure. According to data cited by the BoE, global issuance of debt related to artificial intelligence has reached around $450 billion, roughly twice the level seen in 2025.

Large investments in data centers, chips, energy infrastructure and other parts of the AI ecosystem are increasingly being financed with debt. That does not automatically mean the AI sector is heading toward a crisis, but it does increase sensitivity to high interest rates and to any disappointment over future revenue.

If markets were to significantly lower expectations for returns on massive AI investment, high debt levels could amplify asset-price declines and spread pressure from equity markets into credit and bond markets.

## Inflation in France rises from 2.6% to 3.4%

The same day brought another inflation shock from the eurozone. Harmonized inflation in **France** rose in September **from 2.6% to 3.4%**, significantly more than expected. **Italy** recorded an even larger increase **from 3.2% to 4.1%**, while inflation in **Spain rose from 4.6% to 5.0%**.

In **Germany**, data from five major federal states also showed a strong acceleration in prices, suggesting that the same trend is spreading to the eurozone’s largest economy. At the time of the report, a final unified national inflation rate for Germany had not yet been published, so the direction of travel is more important than speculating about the final figure.

Energy is again the main driver behind the renewed rise. The war with Iran has lifted energy costs, while the strong U.S. dollar has made imported energy even more expensive for European buyers.

## The eurozone is moving back into a higher-for-longer problem

Eurozone inflation for September is estimated at around 3.6%, up from 3.2% in August. That moves inflation further away from the European Central Bank’s 2% target and increases pressure on the ECB to maintain tighter monetary policy.

Markets are now pricing in at least four additional ECB rate hikes over the next year. If the energy shock continues to feed into broader inflation, the eurozone could remain stuck for longer in a combination of high rates, expensive financing and weaker economic growth.

That is particularly uncomfortable at a time when long-term global yields are already at very elevated levels.

## U.S. yields ease slightly but remain extremely high

The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.293% the previous day, its highest level since June 2007. On September 30, it eased toward roughly 5.21%, giving markets some short-term relief but doing little to change the broader picture of very expensive long-term financing.

New York Fed President John Williams reduced some of the pressure by saying there was no urgency to raise rates again. After his remarks, the market-implied probability of an October Fed hike fell toward roughly 50%, after previously being close to 70%.

That is why the current setup does not look like a fresh accelerated selloff. Risks are rising, but immediate monetary pressure from the U.S. is slightly lower than it was a day earlier.

## Asia remains surprisingly resilient

Despite a very difficult month for global bonds, Asian equities were mostly higher on September 30. The Nikkei rose by around 1.9%, while Asian shares outside Japan were up about 0.3%. The main support once again came from AI and semiconductor stocks.

That is an important contrast. Bonds remain under pressure from rising inflation, increased government debt supply and high energy costs, while equity markets continue to receive strong support from the technology and AI investment cycle.

Japan stands out as one of the relatively stronger markets, while parts of Asia that are more exposed to high oil prices and a strong dollar are under greater pressure. The Indian rupee, for example, remains among the weaker Asian currencies because of the combination of expensive oil imports and high global yields.

## Equities are still ignoring part of the warning from bond markets

One of the most interesting features of the current market is the divergence between bonds and equities. Global bonds are ending one of their most difficult months in years, while stocks continue to hold up relatively well.

AI and semiconductor momentum remain an important support. That is exactly why the BoE warning about AI-related debt carries additional weight: the same sector helping to keep equity markets near elevated levels is becoming increasingly dependent on enormous capital investment and growing debt financing.

If expected AI revenues continue to justify that investment, the cycle can continue. If there is a major disappointment, the combination of high valuations and rising debt could amplify a correction.

## Oil remains above $100

Energy risk has not disappeared either. Brent crude continues to trade around $102 to $103 per barrel, without a new major spike but still high enough to maintain inflation pressure.

Much of the current market story is connected through energy. High oil prices push inflation higher in Europe, complicate ECB policy, increase pressure on countries dependent on imported energy and help keep global bond yields elevated.

Without a meaningful decline in energy prices, it is difficult to see higher-for-longer risk disappearing quickly.

## The problem is not one risk, but how they connect

The BoE message therefore goes beyond a warning about AI debt or high yields. The real issue is the possibility that several market vulnerabilities begin reinforcing each other at the same time.

An energy shock can raise inflation. Higher inflation can force the ECB and other central banks to raise rates further or delay rate cuts. Higher yields increase the cost of government and corporate financing. At the same time, they can pressure valuations in the most expensive parts of the market, including AI and technology companies going through a historically large investment cycle.

If equity markets begin reacting to the same risks that bond markets have already been pricing for months, the correction could become much faster.

## Our view

- **The most important new development today comes from the Bank of England.** This is not a market comment or media interpretation, but an official Financial Policy Committee warning that the probability of interconnected financial risks materializing together has increased.
- **AI is increasingly becoming a credit story as well.** Around $450 billion in AI-related debt issuance shows that the investment boom is no longer only about equity valuations, but increasingly about financing markets too.
- **European inflation is accelerating again very quickly.** France rose from 2.6% to 3.4%, Italy from 3.2% to 4.1%, and Spain from 4.6% to 5.0%, while German regional data point in the same direction.
- **The ECB is moving back into a very uncomfortable higher-for-longer environment.** The latest energy shock is making inflation harder to bring down at a time when financing is already expensive.
- **The U.S. is giving a slightly more positive signal today.** The 10-year yield has pulled back from 5.293%, while Williams’ comments have reduced expectations for an immediate Fed hike.
- **Asia is not yet confirming a new global risk-off move.** The Nikkei is around 1.9% higher, while AI and semiconductor stocks continue to absorb part of the macro pressure.
- **The biggest risk is not any single number, but the interaction between all of them.** Energy, inflation, interest rates, bond yields, AI valuations and debt are increasingly becoming parts of the same cross-market problem.
