# White Hats Move 3,832 NFTs for 0 ETH After Possible Magic Eden Security Flaw

**A single wallet moved 3,832 NFTs from hundreds of other wallets after concerns emerged over a possible security flaw linked to Magic Eden. The transactions appeared as sales for 0 ETH, but Yuga Labs' 0xQuit says this was not a theft. According to him, it was a white-hat operation that temporarily moved exposed NFTs to safety.**

NFT trader Cirrus was among the first to publicly flag the unusual activity after noticing that one wallet was moving thousands of NFTs from a large number of addresses. Users who had previously used Magic Eden were advised to check and revoke NFT permissions they no longer needed as a precaution.

Shortly afterward, 0xQuit, the pseudonymous vice president of blockchain at Yuga Labs, said he controlled the wallet receiving the NFTs and that the assets were safe. According to him, the NFTs will be returned to their owners once they are no longer at risk.

## 3,832 NFTs moved from hundreds of wallets

According to the information currently available, the same wallet moved a total of 3,832 NFTs from hundreds of different addresses. What drew particular attention was how the transactions appeared on-chain: they looked like sales connected to Magic Eden, but at a price of 0 ETH.

That pattern immediately raised the question of whether someone had found a way to use existing permissions that NFT holders had previously granted to smart contracts. At the time of publication, Magic Eden had not publicly explained the exact cause of the incident or confirmed the full scope of the possible vulnerability.

## 0xQuit says it was a white-hat rescue

After the initial concern, 0xQuit said the activity was part of a white-hat operation. According to his account, the NFTs were not stolen but moved to prevent a malicious attacker from exploiting the vulnerability first.

From the outside, such an operation can look almost identical to a real attack because the blockchain shows a large-scale movement of assets without fresh individual authorization from every owner. The key difference is who controls the destination wallet, why the assets were moved, and whether they are returned to their original owners.

0xQuit has been involved in similar efforts before. In June, he helped recover 68 NFTs after an exploit hit Flooring Protocol, with the assets later held for return to affected users.

## Why did the transactions appear as 0 ETH?

On NFT marketplaces, users often grant a smart contract permission in advance to manage certain NFTs so that a later sale can be completed without requiring a separate approval for every technical step. Those permissions can remain active even after a user stops using a particular platform.

If a smart contract with such permission contains a vulnerability, or if a way is found to abuse it, an old approval can become a security risk. That is why users were advised after the incident to review existing NFT permissions and revoke those they no longer need.

It has not yet been confirmed whether an old permission was what enabled all 3,832 NFTs to be moved. However, this is one of the main scenarios that needs to be clarified once Magic Eden publishes a technical explanation.

## Magic Eden had already shut down its EVM marketplace

The case is particularly interesting because Magic Eden had already ended support for its EVM NFT marketplace earlier this year. EVM stands for Ethereum Virtual Machine, the execution standard used for smart contracts on Ethereum and many compatible blockchains.

Shutting down a marketplace or its user interface does not necessarily mean that the underlying smart contracts or previously granted permissions automatically disappear. That is why this incident raises a broader question about how long old permissions can remain active after a user has stopped using an application entirely.

This matters beyond Magic Eden. A large number of decentralized applications rely on smart-contract approvals, meaning the same security principle applies to other NFT marketplaces, DeFi protocols and EVM applications.

## Magic Eden has not yet published a technical explanation

At the time of writing, Magic Eden had not published a full technical report explaining exactly what happened, which component was affected or how such a large number of NFTs could be moved.

It has also not been publicly confirmed whether the white-hat operation was coordinated with Magic Eden in advance or whether 0xQuit acted independently after the vulnerability was discovered.

That makes it important to distinguish between what is visible on the blockchain and the claims made by participants, which still need to be supported by a full technical analysis.

## Old wallet permissions can remain active for years

The incident highlights a problem that many crypto users underestimate. Once a wallet grants a smart contract access to tokens or NFTs, that permission does not necessarily disappear simply because the user closes the website, stops using the marketplace or the service shuts down part of its product.

Users can therefore keep active permissions for months or even years toward applications they no longer use, without necessarily being aware of it.

Regularly reviewing wallet approvals can be an important part of protecting digital assets, especially for wallets that have been connected over time to many DeFi, NFT and other Web3 applications.

## Phishing risk also rises after an incident

A security incident does not necessarily end with the exploit itself or a white-hat intervention. Once the story spreads, fake websites, messages and accounts often appear offering supposed NFT recovery, wallet checks or urgent permission revocation.

Users should therefore be especially cautious with messages asking them to connect a wallet or sign new transactions.

If the NFTs were genuinely moved for protection and are going to be returned to their owners, the legitimate return process should be clearly explained through the official channels of the parties involved.

## A white-hat operation can look almost identical to an attack

This case illustrates one of the unusual features of blockchain security. A white-hat researcher who discovers a serious vulnerability may sometimes need to act before the team behind a project has time to fix the problem.

If digital assets are directly at risk, the fastest way to protect them may be to move them to a secure address.

On-chain, that intervention can look almost identical to an exploit. Thousands of NFTs leave their owners' wallets, arrive at a single address and the original owners temporarily lose control over them.

Only afterward does it become clear whether the event was a theft or a rescue.

## The key question is how access was possible at all

The figure of 3,832 NFTs attracts attention, but the more important long-term question is how it was possible to move such a large amount of digital property from hundreds of different wallets in the first place.

If old smart-contract permissions are confirmed as the cause, the incident would become another example of why stopping the use of a Web3 application does not automatically end its technical access to a wallet.

If the problem instead involved another part of Magic Eden's infrastructure, it will be important to determine how many users were actually exposed and whether additional wallets could have been affected.
