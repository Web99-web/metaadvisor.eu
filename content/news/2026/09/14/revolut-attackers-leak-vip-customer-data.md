# Revolut attackers leak VIP customer data: Shevchenko, Römer and Karpelès among named victims

**Revolut's security incident has taken a far more serious turn. After it was confirmed that the fintech handed sensitive customer data to someone impersonating a government authority, the attackers have reportedly begun publishing some of that information and are threatening to release more data every day until Revolut pays.**

Among the first publicly named individuals whose data was reportedly released are professional tennis player Alexander Shevchenko and Felix Römer, CEO of online crypto casino Gamdom. The leaked material reportedly includes identity documents and selfies used for identity verification. The attackers are also said to have posted a message on Telegram promising to release more and more information every day until “Revolut pays.”

## Shevchenko and Römer among the first named victims

The latest development gives the first concrete indication of what the customer data handed over by Revolut can look like once it is published online. Reports indicate that identity documents and verification selfies associated with Alexander Shevchenko and Felix Römer have appeared among the leaked material.

Shevchenko is a professional tennis player, while Römer runs Gamdom, an online gambling platform with a strong connection to cryptocurrency. The fact that people from professional sport and the crypto industry are among the first publicly named victims raises the question of whether the attackers deliberately targeted customers they believed might have substantial assets. Blockchain investigator ZachXBT had already suggested that high-net-worth users may have been targeted, although Revolut has not confirmed such a selection strategy.

## Mark Karpelès had already confirmed he was affected

Even before the reported publication of customer documents began, one prominent affected user had already identified himself publicly. Mark Karpelès, the former CEO of the collapsed cryptocurrency exchange Mt. Gox, shared parts of Revolut's security notification and said he received a message titled “Urgent security update about your Revolut account” on September 11 at 21:59 UTC.

His disclosure helped bring the incident to much wider attention within the crypto community and international media. An important distinction remains, however: Karpelès confirmed that he was affected by the original Revolut incident, but there is currently no reliable confirmation that his personal documents have also been publicly released alongside those associated with Shevchenko and Römer.

## Attackers are now trying to extort Revolut

The first phase of the incident was already unusual. Rather than breaking into Revolut's infrastructure, the attackers apparently convinced the fintech that it was handing information to a legitimate government authority by using an email address associated with a real government domain. The case now appears to be evolving into a more conventional form of extortion in which genuine customer data is being used as leverage.

Messages attributed to the attackers claim that the group intends to publish new information every day until Revolut pays. Reddit discussions have also circulated claims that customer data is being released “one by one” through Telegram and that the group does not intend to stop until it receives payment. Those claims should still be distinguished from officially confirmed facts, as Revolut has not publicly confirmed every person whose documents are alleged to have been released or announced a response to any payment demand.

## Hungarian Reddit claims Bitcoin may have been the filter

One particularly interesting discussion appeared on Hungarian Reddit, where users claimed that the attackers requested complete KYC information from Revolut for customers connected to known Bitcoin deposit or withdrawal addresses. The same discussion also includes claims that some affected users have already received extortion messages through Telegram.

There is currently no independent confirmation that Bitcoin activity was actually used as a criterion for selecting targets. If that claim were proven true, however, it would add an entirely new dimension to the incident: rather than requesting random Revolut accounts, the attackers may have used blockchain activity to identify potentially valuable targets. Revolut's original notices to affected users did confirm that the exposed information could include full transaction histories, including records of Bitcoin transactions.

## Leaked verification selfies raise another uncomfortable question

The reported publication of selfies used for identity verification is particularly troubling. A facial image combined with an identity document, home address, telephone number and financial information gives an attacker far more than an ordinary list of personal details.

The incident also raises a broader question: how many selfies and recordings of our faces do we leave with different apps and online platforms? We have previously reported on Meta asking users to submit selfies or video recordings of their faces during certain account verification procedures, including situations in which the account may still be disabled afterward. The Revolut case shows why this is not merely a theoretical privacy question. Every financial or technology platform that stores these highly sensitive files over long periods is also holding material that could eventually leave its systems if something goes wrong.

## The risk is no longer theoretical

Revolut previously said that potentially exposed information could include full names, dates of birth, occupations, home addresses, email addresses, telephone numbers, copies of passports and driving licences, verification selfies, IBANs, bank statements, payout information and complete transaction histories.

That combination of information can be extremely valuable for identity theft, targeted phishing attacks, social engineering and attempts to take over other accounts. For customers connected to cryptocurrency, another risk emerges if a real identity, home address and facial photograph can be linked to Bitcoin activity. At that point, the potential consequences are no longer limited to online fraud.

This is also why the possibility that wealthier users were deliberately targeted is particularly serious. If attackers selected victims based on perceived wealth or blockchain activity, the leaked information could become useful not only for digital scams but for much more targeted forms of coercion.

## Revolut still will not say how many customers were affected

Revolut continues to describe the number of affected customers only as “limited.” The company has not disclosed an exact number, identified the government agency whose domain was abused or revealed which country that agency belongs to. It also remains unclear whether the customers whose data was handed over came from one country or several markets.

Revolut says its own systems and customer funds were not compromised and that identified affected customers were contacted directly. That means users who have not received a security notification currently have no specific reason to believe their information was included in this particular incident. As the situation develops, however, one of the most important questions will be how many complete customer profiles the attackers actually possess.

## The story has changed completely in just days

When the incident first became public, the central question was how Revolut could hand over passports, verification selfies and financial records because a request appeared to come from a legitimate government authority. The question is now much more serious: how much data do the attackers hold, who exactly did they target, and how much of it are they prepared to publish?

If Shevchenko and Römer are only the beginning and the attackers really possess information belonging to a larger group of specifically selected customers, the pressure on Revolut may only be starting. It will also be important to establish whether the claims circulating on Hungarian Reddit are accurate and whether Bitcoin activity was genuinely used to identify potentially valuable targets.

One thing, however, is no longer merely theoretical. The data Revolut handed to an unauthorized party can no longer be described only as “exposed.” According to the latest reports, at least some of it is now being actively published and used as leverage against the company.
