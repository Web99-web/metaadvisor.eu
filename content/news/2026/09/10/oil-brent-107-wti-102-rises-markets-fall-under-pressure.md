# Oil Above $107 While Almost Everything Else Falls: What Is Happening Across Markets?

**Brent surged above $107 a barrel on September 10 and WTI moved above $102, while Wall Street, technology and AI stocks, cryptocurrencies, gold, silver and platinum all came under pressure. The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield moved close to 5%, and markets are again seriously pricing in the possibility of higher interest rates. The current picture no longer looks like a simple rotation between sectors – capital is being pulled from a broad range of risk assets, while energy remains one of the few clearly positive areas.**

The most important link between almost all of today’s market moves is oil. Higher oil prices raise energy, transportation and production costs, revive inflation expectations and reduce the Federal Reserve’s room to lower interest rates.

At the same time, U.S. Treasury yields are climbing. That increases competition for stocks, crypto and gold because investors can once again earn very high nominal returns from comparatively safe U.S. government debt.

## Oil: Brent Above $107, WTI Above $102

The energy market is moving in the opposite direction from most other major asset classes. Brent climbed to around **$107.63 a barrel**, gaining about **6.3%** on the day, while U.S. WTI reached roughly **$102.48**, up around **6.7%**.

The move is tied to growing concern about physical oil supply and transport routes in the Middle East. Tanker attacks and risks to traffic through key maritime routes are once again adding a substantial geopolitical premium to crude prices.

At the same time, the search for alternative supplies is intensifying. Chinese refiners are turning to additional volumes from West Africa, Canada and South America, increasing competition for available barrels.

Unlike many financial shocks in which crude rises mainly on speculative fear, the market is now also pricing in a genuine risk of constrained supply.

## Energy Companies: One of the Few Sectors Attracting Capital

Higher oil prices directly improve revenue expectations for major energy producers. Energy companies have therefore been outperforming much of the broader market.

Exxon Mobil previously gained around **2.2%**, while Chevron rose roughly **1.9%**, with the energy sector among the few positive areas of the S&P 500.

For companies producing oil and gas, crude above $100 can translate into materially stronger cash flows and margins. What becomes a cost problem for transport, industry and consumers simultaneously becomes a source of additional earnings for the energy sector.

## Wall Street: S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow in the Red

The U.S. equity market is under renewed pressure. The **S&P 500 fell around 0.58% to 7,592**, while the **Nasdaq Composite lost roughly 0.64% and finished near 26,086**. The **Dow Jones fell around 0.60%**, or more than 300 points.

The S&P 500 is therefore continuing a weaker stretch of trading and moving further away from the record levels reached in August.

The problem is not geopolitics alone. Investors are simultaneously dealing with oil above $100, renewed inflation pressure, government bond yields near multi-year highs and the possibility that the Federal Reserve may not lower rates as quickly as markets previously expected.

## Tech, AI and Semiconductors: Micron, Nvidia and AMD Under Pressure

Technology stocks, especially companies linked to artificial intelligence and semiconductors, are highly sensitive to a sharp rise in yields.

Micron was down roughly **4.7%** during the session, AMD around **3.2%**, and Nvidia about **2.3%**. Taiwan Semiconductor was roughly **1.4% lower**, despite strong operating performance.

Highly valued growth companies are particularly sensitive to interest rates. A significant part of their market value depends on earnings expected many years into the future. As discount rates rise, the present value of those future earnings falls.

A U.S. government bond offering a yield close to 5% therefore becomes a serious competitor to equities where investors must accept considerably more risk.

## U.S. Bonds: 10-Year Yield Approaches 5%

The yield on the U.S. 10-year Treasury climbed to around **4.94–4.95%**, one of the highest levels seen in recent years.

It is important to distinguish between bond yield and bond price. When yields rise, the price of existing bonds falls. The bond market is therefore not a conventional “green” area either – bond prices are falling, while new buyers are receiving increasingly attractive yields.

Pressure is also visible further out on the yield curve. Long-term U.S. government debt is being affected by inflation expectations, high public debt, large fiscal deficits and the continued need for heavy Treasury issuance.

For equities, 5% is an especially important psychological threshold. The higher the relatively safe return rises, the greater the return investors demand from riskier assets.

## Inflation Is Becoming the Main Problem Again

The U.S. Producer Price Index has again shown pressure on prices. The additional rise in crude increases the likelihood that higher energy costs will gradually feed through into transportation, production, logistics and ultimately consumer prices.

Markets have therefore increased expectations that the Fed could tighten monetary policy again.

That represents a major change from the period when almost the entire market was focused on one question: when the next cycle of rate cuts would begin.

If energy remains expensive, the Fed faces an uncomfortable combination – inflation rising again at a time when high interest rates are already slowing parts of the economy.

## Gold Falls Despite Geopolitical Risk

One of the most unusual market moves is happening in gold. In a classic geopolitical shock, investors would normally be expected to seek protection in the precious metal.

Instead, gold fell by around **1%** toward approximately **$4,356 an ounce**, while U.S. futures lost roughly **1.2%**.

The reason is strong competition from high bond yields. Gold pays no interest, so a U.S. Treasury yield close to 5% raises the opportunity cost of holding bullion.

A stronger dollar is adding further pressure. A rising U.S. currency traditionally makes it more difficult for dollar-denominated commodities to advance.

## Silver, Platinum and Palladium Fall Much Harder

Losses are significantly larger across the other precious metals.

**Silver fell around 4.6% to roughly $64.19**, **platinum dropped about 5.5% to approximately $1,791**, while **palladium lost around 5.1% and moved toward $1,284**.

These metals face an additional challenge because they depend not only on investment demand but also on industrial demand.

If investors expect a combination of expensive energy, higher interest rates and slower economic growth, industrial metals can simultaneously lose financial demand and face weaker expectations for future industrial consumption.

That is why the current divergence is so clear: energy commodities are rising sharply, while precious and industrial metals are falling.

## Crypto Market Loses Almost 4% of Its Value

Crypto is also following the global risk-off pattern.

Total cryptocurrency market capitalization fell by around **3.9% in 24 hours**, to roughly **$2.65 trillion**.

Bitcoin traded near **$77,300**, down approximately **1.2%**. Ethereum was around **$2,467** and comparatively stable, while Solana fell roughly **2.2% to around $100**.

BNB traded near **$715**, down around **3%**, while XRP dropped roughly **3.2% to about $1.36**.

Bitcoin dominance moved close to **58.5%**, indicating that altcoins are once again absorbing a larger share of the selling pressure.

It is also notable that Bitcoin is not attracting a major safe-haven bid despite geopolitical uncertainty. In the current market environment, investors appear to be treating it primarily as a risk asset again.

## Dollar Supported by Higher Rate Expectations

The U.S. dollar is strengthening again as expectations of higher American interest rates increase the appeal of dollar-denominated assets.

If investors can earn nearly 5% on U.S. government debt, demand increases for the dollars needed to buy those securities.

A strong dollar also makes conditions more difficult for commodities and emerging markets. Much of global oil and commodity trade is priced in dollars, while many governments and companies also carry dollar-denominated debt.

The combination of a stronger dollar and expensive energy is therefore especially difficult for energy importers.

## Industry, Transport and Consumers Pay the Price of Expensive Oil

Oil above $100 is not only a financial-market problem. Higher crude prices gradually move through the entire economy.

Costs rise across road and air transport, logistics, shipping, plastics, petrochemicals, agriculture and large parts of manufacturing.

Companies then have two options: accept lower profit margins or pass part of the higher costs on to consumers.

If they pass them on, inflation rises further.

If they cannot pass them on, corporate profitability falls.

Either way, the combination of expensive energy and high interest rates is negative for a large part of the equity market.

## What Is Rising – and What Is Falling?

The September 10 market picture is unusual because only a limited number of major asset classes are clearly moving higher.

**Oil is rising:** Brent around $107.63, +6.3%; WTI around $102.48, +6.7%.

**Energy companies are showing relative strength:** Exxon Mobil and Chevron are among the companies benefiting from higher energy prices.

**Bond yields are rising:** The U.S. 10-year yield is approaching 5%, although this simultaneously means bond prices are falling.

**The dollar is strengthening:** Supported by expectations of higher U.S. interest rates.

On the other side is a much longer list.

**S&P 500:** around -0.6%.

**Nasdaq:** around -0.6%.

**Dow Jones:** around -0.6%.

This no longer looks like a simple rotation from technology into metals or from crypto into gold. Capital is currently being pulled from a broad range of risk assets, while the clearest strength is concentrated in energy.

## The Next Major Test Will Be U.S. Inflation

The key data point for the next market move will be U.S. consumer inflation.

If CPI confirms that price pressure is accelerating again, markets could further increase expectations for higher interest rates. In that scenario, a 5% yield on the U.S. 10-year Treasury could stop being merely a psychological threshold and become a more persistent market benchmark.

That would create additional pressure on technology stocks, AI companies, crypto and precious metals.

If inflation comes in weaker than expected, markets could quickly scale back expectations for further rate increases. The sectors that have been sold most aggressively could then have the greatest room for a rebound.

For now, however, the market message is relatively clear: **oil is one of the main sources of the renewed inflation shock, which is why it can rise sharply while almost everything around it comes under pressure.**
