# SEC Clarifies Token Buybacks and the Line Between Functional and Non-Functional Crypto Networks

**The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission has issued new regulatory guidance on token buybacks, the development of crypto networks and the application of the Howey test. On September 25, the Division of Corporation Finance said that in an already functional crypto system, merely announcing a buyback program for a non-security token does not constitute a promise of essential managerial efforts. At the same time, it drew an important distinction between such networks and projects that are not yet functional and present future development or buybacks to token buyers as a potential source of returns.**

The new clarification is part of the SEC's crypto asset FAQ and addresses circumstances in which the activities of an issuer or other participants may constitute so-called *essential managerial efforts*, one of the elements considered when applying the Howey test.

SEC staff does not provide a universal answer for all tokens, nor does it say that a buyback automatically determines the regulatory status of a crypto asset. Instead, the focus is on the state of the system, its actual functions and the way future activities and potential economic benefits are presented to buyers.

## Buybacks on a functional network

The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance directly addresses programs in which tokens are repurchased for treasury management, supply reduction, protocol-funded burns or other economic purposes.

In an already functional crypto system, merely announcing a buyback program for a non-security token does not, according to SEC staff, constitute a promise of *essential managerial efforts*.

That wording is important because it shows that the existence of a buyback program alone is not enough to determine whether an investment contract exists. The regulatory analysis depends on the broader context in which the token and network operate.

The SEC also explicitly leaves open the possibility of a different conclusion when the system is not yet functional.

## When a buyback may have a different meaning

If a crypto system is not yet functional, the regulatory analysis can look very different.

SEC staff gives an example in which an issuer promises that a future buyback program will generate yield or return for token holders. In that situation, the buyback may form part of a broader promise of an economic outcome that depends on the future activities of the issuer or development team.

In other words, the buyback mechanism itself is not the only relevant factor. The circumstances in which it is presented also matter.

The distinction between an existing functional network and a project whose product still needs to be built therefore becomes central to understanding the SEC's new clarification.

## The key word is functional

The new clarification on buybacks is part of a broader SEC approach to what happens after a crypto system becomes functional.

SEC staff says that activities such as securing, maintaining, improving or expanding a functional crypto system do not necessarily constitute *essential managerial efforts* by themselves.

That means continued network development after launch is not automatically equivalent to a situation in which token buyers depend on a team that still has to build the product for which the token was sold.

In a non-functional project, the future work of the development team may carry much greater significance because the product, network or expected functionality does not yet exist without that work.

## Maintaining a network is not the same as promising a future product

The SEC therefore draws an important distinction between continuing to develop an existing system and performing work without which the system would not yet be able to deliver its basic function.

If a network is already operational, security upgrades, technical improvements and further expansion do not automatically mean that token holders depend on critical future efforts by a single team.

The situation is different when a token is sold before the system can perform the function for which it was presented to buyers.

In that case, promises that a team will still need to build the network, develop the product, attract users or create an economic model may carry much greater regulatory significance.

## How the buyback is presented to buyers also matters

The SEC's clarification does not reduce the regulatory analysis to the technical fact that a project buys back its own token.

What potential buyers are told about that process also matters.

A buyback that forms part of treasury management, supply reduction or an already existing economic model of a functional network is not the same as a promise that future work by the team and future buybacks will generate yield or returns for buyers.

This again emphasizes that context, system functionality and the way potential economic benefits are presented play an important role in the regulatory analysis.

## The new clarification is not a new SEC rule

The legal status of the document is important. The SEC explicitly states in the FAQ that the answers reflect the views of the staff of the Division of Corporation Finance.

They are not rules, regulations or official decisions of the Commission, do not have the force of law and do not create new legal obligations.

The FAQ also does not determine the regulatory status of any individual token.

Even so, such clarifications have practical importance because they give projects, lawyers and market participants a more concrete view of how SEC staff currently analyzes certain structures and facts.

## Why the clarification matters for DeFi

Token buybacks have become an increasingly common part of DeFi tokenomics in recent years.

Protocols may use part of their revenue to buy back their own tokens. Depending on the project, those tokens may then be removed from circulating supply, held in a treasury, staked or used in other ways within the protocol economy.

That is why the SEC's clarification is relevant to the broader industry, but it does not mean that all such projects automatically have the same regulatory status.

Each model still needs to be considered individually, including the functionality of the system, the way the buyback is funded, the role of the token and the way potential economic benefits are presented to users.

## Examples of projects with token buyback models

One of the better-known examples is Hyperliquid. Its Assistance Fund uses part of the revenue from trading fees to buy HYPE on the market, removing the purchased tokens from circulating supply.

CoinGecko estimates that about $644.6 million was spent on HYPE buybacks during 2025.

The SEC does not mention Hyperliquid or HYPE in the new FAQ. The clarification therefore cannot be interpreted as a regulatory decision on HYPE or as approval of Hyperliquid's model. Hyperliquid is mentioned here only as a market example of a functional crypto system that uses a token buyback mechanism.

Other projects also use or have used buyback models. These include Jupiter with JUP, Pump.fun with PUMP, Aave with AAVE, GMX, Raydium with RAY and dYdX with DYDX.

Their models are not identical. In some projects, the repurchased tokens are removed from supply, while in others they are held in a treasury, staked or used in another way within the protocol.

The SEC does not identify any of these projects as subjects of its analysis in the FAQ. They are included here only as market examples of different types of token buyback models.

## Buybacks are becoming an important part of token economics

The growing use of buyback models shows that the tokenomics of many projects are becoming increasingly connected to the actual use of a protocol and the revenue it generates.

Rather than having a token exist almost entirely separately from the economic activity of the network, some projects now use revenue to repurchase their own tokens.

That can reduce circulating supply, change the way economic value is distributed within a protocol or connect product usage more directly with token economics.

That is why the SEC's clarification has broader significance. Buybacks are no longer a marginal practice used by only a few projects, but a model that appears across a growing number of functional crypto systems.

## Our view

- **The most important word in the SEC's new clarification is "functional."** Staff clearly distinguishes between a system that already works and a project that has yet to achieve its promised functionality.
- **The SEC did not say that token buybacks are generally outside securities law.** The clarification applies to specific circumstances and expressly starts from a non-security token in a functional crypto system.
- **In a functional system, merely announcing a buyback does not constitute a promise of essential managerial efforts.** That is a much narrower and more precise statement than saying that any token with a buyback mechanism is automatically not a security.
- **The analysis can be different for a non-functional network.** Especially if a future buyback is presented as a way to generate yield or return for token holders.
- **SEC staff distinguishes maintenance and improvement of an existing network from the work required to make a product functional in the first place.**
- **The FAQ is not a new SEC rule or a decision of the Commission.** It reflects the views of the staff of the Division of Corporation Finance and does not have the force of law.
- **HYPE, JUP, PUMP, AAVE, GMX, RAY and DYDX are not subjects of this SEC FAQ.** They are market examples of projects that use different forms of buyback models.
- **The clarification still matters for DeFi.** Buyback models have become common enough that SEC staff is now directly explaining how it views them in the context of functional crypto systems.
