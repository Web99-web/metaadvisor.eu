# China Regulates the Industry Behind Influencers From September 1: What Are MCN Agencies and How Do They Work?

China is introducing new national rules from September 1 for companies that sit behind a large part of the country’s influencer economy. The focus is not only on individual influencers, but on so-called MCN agencies — Multi-Channel Networks — that can discover creators, help shape their online identity, produce content, manage accounts, organize marketing and turn audience reach into sales.

Chinese regulators use a broader term for providers of multi-channel internet content distribution services. The official definition covers planning, production, distribution, marketing, promotion and representation of public online accounts. In other words, the state is beginning to regulate the business infrastructure that often remains invisible behind the person seen on screen.

## What Is an MCN and How Does It Work?

A large Chinese MCN can simultaneously operate as a talent agency, production studio, marketing company, social media management service and e-commerce operator. An agency can find someone with little or no audience, determine which niche may suit them, help build an online persona and then provide scriptwriters, camera crews, editors, studio facilities and teams that analyze content performance.

If a format works, the same model can be expanded across multiple accounts and platforms such as **Douyin**, **Kuaishou** or **Xiaohongshu**. That means an influencer who appears to be one person creating content every day may actually have an entire team behind them deciding what to produce, when to publish, how to attract traffic and how to monetize it.

## An Industry Worth More Than 60 Billion Yuan, or Around $9.5 Billion

This is no longer a small business. According to data from the China Association of Performing Arts and Douyin Live, China had around **29,000 MCN organizations** by May 2025. The **market was worth 63.6 billion yuan, or around $9.5 billion, with expectations that it would exceed 70 billion yuan, or roughly $10.4 billion**.

MCN agencies can make money in several ways. They may take a share of creator revenue, charge brands for campaigns and promotions, earn commissions on products sold during livestreams, produce content for companies and offer marketing or account-management services. Some go even further by entering supply chains, developing their own products or running entire e-commerce operations.

In live commerce, the influencer may therefore be only the visible face of a much larger sales system. Behind the camera, teams negotiate with manufacturers, set prices and commissions, prepare sales scripts, organize studios and monitor viewers and sales in real time.

## What Changes on September 1?

The new rules create a clearer chain of responsibility between creators, MCNs, platforms and regulators. An MCN must be a registered business entity, appoint people responsible for content and verify the identity of creators with whom it signs contracts. Existing agencies have 30 days from the start of the rules to align their registered business activities.

Platforms also receive greater responsibilities. They must formally register MCN organizations operating through their systems, provide special management accounts for contracted creators and make it clearer on profiles which MCN organization a creator belongs to.

That matters because audiences often do not know whether they are watching a genuinely independent influencer or someone backed by a company involved in content production, marketing and monetization.

## What if an Influencer Really Works Completely Alone?

Then the situation is different. A person who manages their own account, produces their own content and does not have an MCN providing these services **does not automatically become an MCN organization**.

The new rules primarily regulate businesses that provide other creators with services such as planning, production, distribution, promotion or representation. An independent influencer does not suddenly need to create an agency simply because they have a large number of followers.

They still have to comply with Chinese laws, platform rules, advertising requirements and e-commerce regulations that apply to their content and business activities. The difference is that there is no additional MCN between the creator and the platform that now also carries part of the responsibility.

## Fake Personas, Followers and Sales Figures Under Scrutiny

The new rules specifically prohibit fabricated backgrounds, events or personas when they are used for false or misleading marketing. This is particularly relevant in an industry where a creator’s online identity is not always entirely spontaneous, but may be carefully designed.

The issue is not fiction or acting itself. The problem begins when a fictional life story is presented as reality in order to build trust and sell products.

Regulators are also targeting artificially inflated follower counts, views, clicks, ratings, votes and even displayed spending figures. Fake traffic cannot be created manually or through technical means, and large-scale posting of nearly identical content to manipulate distribution is also targeted.

For brands that pay influencers based on reach, engagement or sales performance, this is one of the most important parts of the new rules.

## Stricter Rules for Livestream Sales and Minors

MCNs involved in live commerce must have clear procedures for selecting products, checking compliance, correcting problems and responding to violations. Regulators are therefore trying to connect influencer marketing more directly with responsibility toward consumers rather than treating livestreaming as entertainment alone.

Special restrictions apply to minors. MCNs cannot provide certain livestream publishing services to children under 16, while creators aged 16 to 18 require identity verification and consent from a parent or guardian.

## Platforms Can Act Against an Entire MCN

One of the biggest changes is that a problem involving one creator does not necessarily have to end with a penalty against that individual account. If an MCN systematically enables or organizes problematic content, platforms can warn the agency, restrict functions and monetization, close accounts or remove the entire organization from the platform.

An MCN must also respond when one of its contracted creators violates the law, platform rules or contractual terms. Measures may include warnings, temporary suspension of services or termination of the relationship.

China is effectively saying that a professional influencer is not always simply an individual publishing content. If a company manages that person’s business and earns money from it, that company must also take responsibility.

## Our Take

* **China’s influencer business has grown into a major industry with around 29,000 MCN organizations and a market worth 63.6 billion yuan, or roughly $9.5 billion, with expectations that it would exceed 70 billion yuan, or about $10.4 billion.**
* **An MCN can handle almost everything behind an influencer: from finding creators and producing content to marketing, sales and live commerce.**
* **Agencies earn money from creator revenue, promotions, brand campaigns, sales commissions, production and e-commerce services.**
* **An influencer who genuinely works alone is not automatically an MCN**, but still remains subject to other rules covering content, advertising and online sales.
* **The new rules specifically target fake personas, artificial followers and views, manipulated sales figures and traffic manipulation.**
* **Audiences should gain greater visibility into when an MCN organization stands behind an influencer.**
* **The biggest change is responsibility:** when a company manages creators, content and the money generated by that content, it can no longer remain completely invisible behind the influencer.
