# Austria and 12 other countries call for a pause on new EU rules

**Thirteen European Union member states are calling on Brussels to place stronger limits on new regulatory obligations and carry out a fundamental review of existing legislation. Austria, Czechia, Denmark, Germany, Estonia, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia and Slovakia have signed a joint non-paper proposing a broad clean-up of European regulation and a period focused on implementing and consolidating existing rules.**

The initiative, led by Austria, calls for a more systematic assessment of how new EU rules affect businesses, innovation and competitiveness, while also examining whether existing rules are still necessary, should be changed or create administrative burdens without delivering sufficient added value.

At the heart of the proposal is a simple question: does every new economic or social problem require another new regulation, or should Europe first examine how many of the rules already in place are still necessary?

## Europe already acknowledges that administrative burden has become a problem

The call from the 13 countries comes at a time when the European Commission itself is increasingly linking administrative burden to Europe’s competitiveness. The Commission wants to reduce administrative burdens by at least 25% for all businesses by 2029 and by at least 35% for small and medium-sized enterprises.

The target is to reduce administrative costs by €37.5 billion by the end of the Commission’s mandate. According to current Commission figures, simplification measures already proposed are expected to deliver around €17 billion to €18 billion in annual administrative savings.

That raises an important question: **if tens of billions of euros can be saved simply by removing or simplifying administrative obligations, how much did businesses previously spend adapting to those same rules?**

## The cost is not only tax, every new adjustment has a price

When business burdens are discussed, the debate often focuses on tax rates, contributions and fees. But there is another type of cost that is much harder to see in official statistics. **An institution creates a new obligation, but the cost of implementing it is usually borne by the person or business required to comply.**

Every change to a law or tax rule requires time and adjustment. A business owner has to find out what has changed, determine whether the new rule applies to their business, change procedures, accounting software, price lists, contracts, forms or reporting systems. Sometimes an accountant, tax adviser, lawyer or IT specialist has to be brought in. Sometimes a new software version or an additional upgrade has to be purchased simply because the regulation changed.

For a large company with its own legal, tax and IT departments, such a change may be just another project. For the owner of a small business, the same change can mean hours, days or even weeks spent away from customers, product development or sales, simply trying to understand a new version of the rules.

## When interpreting new and frequently changing rules becomes a business of its own, it is a warning sign for the business climate

In countries where tax and business regulations change very frequently, demand grows for professional interpretation of almost every major change. That creates another cost which businesses increasingly have to include in their normal operating expenses.

Consulting services, specialist seminars, webinars, tax newsletters, specialised portals and training courses on new rules become an increasingly important part of the business infrastructure. This is not a criticism of tax advisers, accountants or lawyers. They become necessary precisely because the regulatory system can become so complex that it is difficult to follow without professional assistance.

The problem is also that such consulting and specialist seminars are often expensive. Businesses therefore do not pay for new regulation only through the time required to adapt, new software or changes to business processes. In some cases, they must also spend hundreds of euros on a seminar or professional consultation simply to find out reliably what the new rules actually require them to do.

**When almost every new regulatory change creates demand for additional, often expensive seminars, consulting and professional interpretation simply so that a business owner can understand what they are required to do, it shows just how complex a regulatory system can become.**

An individual change may be justified. The problem is the cumulative effect. If different parts of the business regulatory system change year after year, and sometimes several times within only a few years, businesses are no longer adapting occasionally. Regulatory adaptation itself becomes a permanent business function.

## Small businesses do not have a regulatory affairs department

Large companies may have tax departments, compliance teams, lawyers, accountants and IT specialists. When a rule changes, the work can be distributed among employees who are paid specifically to handle it.

A small business owner is often the director, salesperson, administrator, purchaser and the person speaking to the accountant all at once. Every hour spent studying a new obligation is an hour taken away from the activity the business actually depends on.

This is precisely why the European Commission places particular emphasis on small and medium-sized enterprises. Its target of reducing their administrative burden by at least 35% is higher than the general target of 25%. The Commission explicitly says that simplification should allow smaller businesses to spend less time on bureaucracy and more time developing their business.

Regulation does not have to be unnecessary in itself for its quantity, complexity and frequency of change to become an economic problem.

## New rules, followed by new rules to simplify the old ones

In recent years, the European Commission has launched a whole series of so-called Omnibus packages aimed at simplifying, amending or consolidating existing European regulations. By mid-2026, it had presented 12 such proposals across different sectors.

The paradox is obvious: institutions are creating new rules while simultaneously spending increasing amounts of time and resources simplifying rules that already exist.

The Commission also uses the “one in, one out” principle in its better regulation approach. Under this principle, new administrative burdens introduced by one initiative should be offset by removing administrative burdens of roughly equal value in the same policy area.

The very need for such a mechanism shows that regulatory costs are no longer a secondary issue. They have become part of the wider debate about Europe’s competitiveness.

## When regulatory risk is joined by fear of penalties

If rules keep changing, the entrepreneurial focus can gradually shift from the question “How do I grow my business?” to “What has changed again, and what do I now have to do to remain compliant?”

The situation becomes even more difficult in systems where frequent regulatory changes are accompanied by high penalties for irregularities.

Penalties have a clear purpose when rules are broken, but proportionality also matters for the business climate. A system in which a relatively minor administrative irregularity can result in a very large fine or a temporary shutdown creates a particularly significant risk for small businesses.

The economic consequences of such a sanction can become greater than the original irregularity itself. If a business is unable to operate for a period of time, the owner is not the only one who loses. Revenue, orders, wages, tax receipts and other public revenues that would otherwise have been generated are also lost.

That is why the number of penalties imposed or their total value alone is not a sufficient measure of the quality of a regulatory system. Another important measure is whether the rules are clear, stable and understandable enough for businesses to apply them correctly.

**The goal of effective supervision should be a high level of compliance, not a business environment in which fear of making a mistake becomes more important than developing the business.**

## A year without new rules no longer sounds so unusual

The Austrian proposal does not call for a complete halt to European legislation. The signatories want the impact of new rules on competitiveness and innovation to be assessed more systematically before they are introduced, while the existing body of EU law would be reviewed sector by sector.

Rules on product safety, consumer protection, competition, the environment, finance or data protection serve specific purposes. But there is a difference between regulation and permanent regulatory change.

A business environment needs a certain level of predictability. Entrepreneurs need to be able to plan investments, hire employees, develop products or open new locations with a reasonable expectation that the regulatory framework will remain stable enough to plan around.

Sometimes the economy does not need another regulatory change. Sometimes it simply needs time to work under the rules that already exist.

## Our take
* **Entrepreneurs need more than lower taxes, they also need stability.** The ability to plan over a longer period reduces both costs and uncertainty.
* **Every regulatory change has a cost.** A new form, reporting requirement or technical obligation can together create significant costs for software, accountants, advisers, training and working time.
* **The cost of adaptation is usually borne by the party required to implement the rule.** That is why the cost of implementation should be an important part of assessing any new regulation.
* **Small businesses do not have compliance departments.** Time spent studying new rules is time taken directly away from customers, sales, product development and growth.
* **When interpreting new and frequently changing regulations becomes a business of its own, it is a warning sign for the business climate.** The problem becomes even greater when entrepreneurs have to pay for expensive seminars and professional advice simply to understand reliably what a new obligation requires.
* **Penalties should be proportionate to the violation.** Clear rules and a high level of voluntary compliance are important indicators of the quality of a regulatory system.
* **The European Commission itself estimates that simplifying existing rules could generate administrative savings worth tens of billions of euros.** That shows that regulatory burden is not merely a subjective feeling among entrepreneurs, but a measurable economic cost.
* **The proposal for a year of implementation and consolidation therefore raises a useful question:** before adopting the next new rule, should Europe first examine whether all existing rules are still necessary, how much they cost to implement and whether they can be simplified?
