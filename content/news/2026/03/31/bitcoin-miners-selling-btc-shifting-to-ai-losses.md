# Bitcoin miners are selling BTC and shifting to AI

The Bitcoin mining industry is entering a phase that increasingly resembles a **true industrial stress test**, where it is no longer just about the price of Bitcoin, but a complex combination of market forces, energy costs, and geopolitics. What was until recently considered a stable and long-term profitable model — mining — is now facing a reality where the math simply no longer works in favor of miners.

According to available data, the average cost of producing one Bitcoin has reached around **$88,000**, while the market price is hovering near **$69,000**. This gap means miners are losing nearly **$19,000 per BTC mined**, representing roughly a **21% loss per block**. Such a situation is not sustainable in the long run and is already reshaping the behavior of the entire industry.

## Energy, war, and global pressure

The main driver behind this situation is not just the drop in Bitcoin’s price, but primarily the **surge in energy costs**, fueled by geopolitical tensions. Oil prices above $100 directly translate into higher electricity costs — and electricity is the largest expense in Bitcoin mining.

A key pressure point is the **Strait of Hormuz**, through which around 20% of the world’s oil and gas supply flows. Any disruption or instability in this region has immediate global consequences for energy prices. On top of that, political threats and the risk of attacks on energy infrastructure add another layer of uncertainty for mining operations worldwide.

## Network under pressure: first signs of strain

The impact is already visible within the Bitcoin network itself. Mining difficulty has dropped by **7.76%**, one of the largest adjustments in 2026. This indicates that some miners are shutting down operations because they can no longer cover their costs.

The total hashrate has fallen to around **920 EH/s (exahash per second)**, significantly below the record levels seen in 2025. At the same time, average block times have stretched beyond **12 minutes**, compared to the network’s 10-minute target. While the system remains functional, these signals clearly show that the network is under stress.

## Why miners are moving into AI

In this environment, an increasing number of mining companies are making a strategic shift: **diversifying into artificial intelligence (AI)**. This is not driven by hype, but by economics. AI infrastructure currently offers more stable revenue streams, longer-term contracts, and less exposure to market volatility.

Mining companies already possess the critical infrastructure required for this transition: large data centers, advanced cooling systems, and access to energy. Instead of shutting down equipment, they are repurposing it for AI workloads such as model training and data processing.

This is a pivotal moment, as it shows how the boundaries between the **crypto industry and the AI sector are increasingly merging**. Infrastructure once dedicated to mining is now becoming the backbone of a new digital economy.

## Selling BTC as a necessity, not a choice

To cover losses and finance the transition, many miners are being forced to **sell Bitcoin**, adding additional pressure to the market. This selling is not driven by panic, but by operational necessity — survival comes first.

At the same time, a reduction in active miners could decrease the amount of newly mined BTC entering the market in the long term, creating a dynamic balance between short-term selling pressure and long-term supply constraints.

## A new phase for the industry

Bitcoin mining is entering a phase where simply “holding and waiting” is no longer enough. The industry is becoming more sophisticated, more connected to global events, and increasingly integrated with other technological sectors.

This transformation highlights that crypto is no longer an isolated ecosystem — it is becoming part of a broader digital infrastructure that connects energy, geopolitics, and artificial intelligence.
