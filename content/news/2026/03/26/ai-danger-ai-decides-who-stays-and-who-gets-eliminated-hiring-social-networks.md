# AI danger – AI decides who stays and who gets eliminated — from hiring to social networks

## When AI selects the “ideal candidate” — who gets crossed out before that and why?

In one company, an AI system, as part of a standard hiring process, created a shortlist of candidates. At first glance, everything looked perfect: applications were clean, precise, and almost fully aligned with the job description. The candidates appeared to perfectly match the required profile. However, it quickly became clear that there was a problem the algorithm could not recognize. All candidates looked almost identical. The differences were minimal, and the impression was as if these were not real people, but variations of the same template.

At that point, an HR professional decided to review the applications that AI had excluded from the shortlist. Among them was a candidate whose CV did not match the expected structure. He did not use the same keywords, it was not “polished,” but it clearly stood out — and that is exactly why the algorithm rejected him. Unlike the system, the experienced eye of the HR professional noticed what AI missed. She reviewed the CV and found no real reason to reject it. On the contrary — the candidate seemed interesting precisely because of that difference. She invited him for an interview. Today, he is one of the best members of the team.

This case raises an increasingly important question: what exactly is AI filtering — and how many strong candidates are we losing because of it?

## While employers use AI to eliminate, candidates use it to create “perfect” CVs

The paradox of modern hiring is becoming increasingly visible — and increasingly dangerous. On one side, employers use AI to filter applications, reject “unwanted” candidates, and select the “most relevant” ones. On the other side, candidates use the same technology to adapt their applications to those exact systems. Tools analyze job postings, detect keywords, and generate CVs that almost perfectly match the required criteria.

The result is a situation where both sides are using the same technology — but with completely opposite goals. People are increasingly afraid of making mistakes, they want to appear perfect, and therefore use AI to “polish” their applications to the maximum.

CVs today are technically better than ever. They are precise, structured, and optimized to pass through systems. However, at the same time, they are becoming increasingly similar to one another. The language is safe, neutral, and “correct,” but without personal tone, without risk, and without a real story about the person.

In such a system, a candidate who stands out is often eliminated for not fitting the pattern, while a candidate who perfectly fits the pattern becomes just another one in the crowd.

In other words: if you are different — you are filtered out. If you are the same — you disappear in the mass.

## AI is eliminating people on social networks too — Facebook and AI moderation

What is happening in hiring is not an isolated case. The same pattern is increasingly visible on social networks, especially on Facebook. Since 2025, according to numerous user experiences, AI moderation has removed a massive number of profiles — including those that were 10, 15, or even 20 years old. People who spent years building networks, contacts, groups, and business pages suddenly disappeared. – Permanently! – Without clear explanation. Without real human support. Without any possibility to speak to an actual person.

They were permanently removed because **AI (not a human)** decided that a **human**, who spent 15–20 years living and working within that social network, no longer belongs there. It is a bitter form of social irony and a clear paradox.

In these situations, users do not just lose a profile. They lose Messenger, contacts, business pages, and communities they have built for years. For many, this also means losing communication, business, and an entire digital life.

Unlike hiring, where the consequence may be a missed opportunity, here the consequence can be complete disappearance from the digital world. People are suddenly cut off from a network they were part of for years. They can no longer be part of local groups, communities, or even receive simple notifications. AI has decided they no longer have access.

## From assistance to control

When these two examples are placed side by side, it becomes clear that this is not just technological progress, but a highly questionable shift in how decisions are made.

AI does not just assist — it increasingly decides. It standardizes how we present ourselves and simultaneously determines whether we move forward or not.

The problem is not only in system errors. The real problem is that the system does not understand context. AI struggles to recognize nuance — personality, potential, intention — exactly the elements that make the difference between average and exceptional.

## How many people are we already losing?

Hiring and social networks may seem like separate systems, but they are connected by the same principle: decisions that were once made by humans are increasingly being delegated to AI systems that search for matching — not for personality, thinking, ideas, or understanding.

This leads to a question that is becoming harder and harder to ignore: are we truly improving processes — or are we narrowing them?

We are slowly entering an *Ex Machina* scenario — where the system no longer serves humans, but the other way around.

And even more importantly:

Who is actually in control? Humans or AI?

How many strong candidates and real people are already disappearing from the system simply because they did not fit the pattern? And not just any pattern — but an AI pattern. Or an AI “boss”?

## Our take (MetaAdvisor)

This is no longer just a technological topic — it is a question of control. AI has become a filter that people must pass through, whether they are applying for jobs or simply trying to remain part of the digital world.

If systems continue making decisions without real human oversight, we risk a scenario where being different becomes a mistake, and matching becomes the only measure of value.

That is not progress — it is a narrowing of space for real people.
