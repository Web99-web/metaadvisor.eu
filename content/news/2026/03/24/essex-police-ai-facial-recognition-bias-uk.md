# Essex police pause AI facial recognition cameras over bias concerns  

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being integrated into security systems, but at the same time it raises questions that go beyond technology itself. Essex police have decided to temporarily suspend the use of facial recognition systems after a study revealed potential differences in how the system identifies individuals.

The technology in question is “live facial recognition” (LFR) — a system that scans the faces of passersby in real time and compares them against databases. These cameras are deployed in public spaces, often from police vans or fixed locations, with the goal of quickly identifying individuals on watchlists.

## AI that sees — but not equally for everyone  

The study involved 188 participants who walked past active cameras under real-world conditions. The results showed that the system successfully identified around half of the individuals on the watchlist, with a very low rate of false identifications.

However, deeper analysis revealed something more important. The system was more successful at identifying men than women, and even more pronounced differences appeared across ethnic groups. Data showed that the AI was significantly more likely to correctly identify Black participants than participants from other groups.

## The issue is not just accuracy — but fairness  

At first glance, such results might appear as improved efficiency. But in the context of policing, this difference means that certain groups could be more frequently stopped, checked or placed under surveillance.

In other words, AI does not need to be inaccurate to be problematic — it only needs to be uneven. That is why the UK regulator, the Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO), has warned that police forces must implement clear safeguards to mitigate these risks.

## Expansion of the technology — and rising risks  

Despite these concerns, plans to expand the technology continue. The UK Home Office has already announced a significant increase in the number of mobile facial recognition units, with the goal of making them available to all police forces.

This means such systems could soon become a standard tool in everyday policing. At the same time, the need for clear rules and oversight is becoming increasingly urgent.

## AI bias: a broader issue beyond a single study  

This case is not isolated. Bias in AI systems has already been documented across multiple sectors — from hiring to financial services and healthcare.

The reason lies in how AI learns. If the underlying data reflects existing social imbalances, the system can reinforce them — and in the context of policing, that can have very real consequences for individuals.

## Conclusion  

The decision by Essex police highlights how thin the line is between innovation and risk. AI can be a powerful tool — but only if it is used with a clear understanding of its limitations.

The key question is no longer just how well AI can see — but how fairly it can decide.
