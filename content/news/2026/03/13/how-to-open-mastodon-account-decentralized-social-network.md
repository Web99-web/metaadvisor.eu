# How we opened a Mastodon account: a guide to the decentralized social network

In recent years there has been growing discussion about **decentralized social networks**. An increasing number of users have started looking for alternatives to large centralized platforms such as Facebook, X, or TikTok, especially after numerous debates about **algorithmic moderation, account bans, and content control**.

One of the best-known decentralized networks is **Mastodon**, a project that in recent years has become the center of a broader internet ecosystem known as the **Fediverse**.

Unlike traditional social networks, Mastodon is not a single platform run by one company. Instead, it is a network of many interconnected **servers**, or communities, that together form a decentralized social network.

In this article we show **how we opened a Mastodon account step by step**, where the registration actually begins, how **servers** work, and why the concept of Mastodon can initially feel confusing to many users.

---

# 1. Where the registration actually begins

One of the first things that may confuse new users is the fact that registering on Mastodon **does not start directly on mastodon.social**.

The entry point into the Mastodon network is the official project website:

https://joinmastodon.org/

This page acts as a **central portal** that explains how Mastodon works and allows users to choose the **server** where they want to open their account.

Only after selecting a server and completing registration does the user arrive at a specific instance, for example:

https://mastodon.social/

In other words, **joinmastodon.org is the gateway into the network**, while individual servers are the places where user accounts actually exist.

---

# What exactly is a **server (like a Facebook group)**

The easiest way to understand Mastodon is to compare it to **Facebook groups**.

At first glance a **server** on Mastodon can look very similar to a Facebook group. Each server represents its own community with its own administrators, rules, and moderation.

In other words, **a server is a community of users**.

Some servers are large and general, while others are specialized around certain topics. There are servers dedicated to:

- technology  
- open-source projects  
- art  
- journalism  
- local communities  

The major difference from Facebook is that all these **servers are interconnected**.

This means users on one server can **follow and communicate with users on other servers**. The entire network of servers together forms what is called the **Fediverse**.

Because of this, Mastodon functions more like a **network of communities**, rather than a single centralized platform.

---

# 2. Choosing a server

After a user starts the registration process via joinmastodon.org, they reach a step where they must choose the **server** on which the account will be created.

On the Mastodon homepage two options are often presented:

- **Join mastodon.social**
- **Pick another server**

The **Join mastodon.social** option leads to one of the largest and best-known servers in the network. For that reason it is often the easiest choice for beginners who want to open an account quickly.

The **Pick another server** option allows users to browse other servers in the network, which may be focused on specific topics or communities.

In practice this means users can choose between **large global servers or smaller thematic communities**.

---

# 3. Accepting the server rules

After choosing a server, a page with the community rules opens.

Each **server** has its own rules because it is run by its own moderation community. These rules often include bans on hate speech, bans on violent content, or bans on harassing other users.

It is also common that **sensitive content must be marked with a warning (content warning)**.

This step is very similar to rules that exist in Facebook groups. Server administrators have the right to moderate content and enforce community rules.

To continue the registration process you must accept the rules by clicking **Accept**.

---

# 4. Registering the account

The next step is entering the basic information required to create an account.

This includes:

- username  
- email address  
- password  

After this Mastodon sends a **confirmation email for the registration**.

One interesting thing we noticed during registration is that the confirmation email can sometimes land in the **Spam folder**, so it is worth checking there if the message does not arrive immediately.

---

# 5. Email confirmation and first login

After clicking the link in the email message the account becomes active and it is possible to access the Mastodon profile.

At that point Mastodon often suggests a few users you can start following so that your feed fills with content more quickly.

The system also offers options to set up:

- a profile picture  
- a header image  
- a short profile description  

There is also the option **Make my profile discoverable**, which allows the profile to appear more easily in searches and recommendations.

---

# 6. The first post

On Mastodon posts are sometimes called **toots**, although most users simply use the word post.

For our first post we decided to greet the **Fediverse community** and explain why we joined Mastodon.

Very quickly after posting we received **our first like and first follower**, which shows that Mastodon feeds and hashtags allow new profiles to be discovered relatively quickly.

Unlike large social networks that rely heavily on algorithms, Mastodon relies more on:

- hashtags  
- following users  
- communities within servers  

Posts are often organized **chronologically**, which means users have more control over the content they see in their feed.

---

# How Mastodon actually works

Mastodon is part of a broader network known as the **Fediverse**, which includes multiple decentralized platforms that can communicate with each other.

This means users on one server can communicate with users on other servers, even if they are registered on completely different instances.

This model strongly resembles how **email** works. A user can have an email address on different services, yet they can all communicate with each other.

This idea of decentralization is exactly what many people find appealing because it reduces dependence on a single large platform.

---

# Conclusion

At first glance Mastodon may seem somewhat confusing because the concept of **servers** is unfamiliar to users of traditional social networks.

However, once you understand that **servers are interconnected communities**, the entire system becomes much clearer.

Decentralized social networks like Mastodon represent an interesting alternative model of online communication that does not depend on a single centralized platform.

How the Fediverse will evolve in the future remains to be seen, but interest in decentralized networks has clearly been growing in recent years.
