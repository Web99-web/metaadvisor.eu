# Digital euro enters the real world

The European Central Bank (ECB) has made a decisive move — the digital euro is no longer just an idea, but a project entering the phase of real-world implementation. The focus is no longer on whether this currency should exist, but on how it will function in everyday life across Europe.

Most importantly, the ECB is now actively seeking experts to define how the digital euro will work on **ATMs, card payment terminals and in stores across the entire eurozone**. The goal is clear — to allow users to **pay with the digital euro at checkout or withdraw funds from cash machines**, just as they do today with cash or cards.

## From theory to infrastructure

This is the moment the project moves from concept to infrastructure. The ECB has already completed its technical and preparatory work, and according to President **Christine Lagarde**, it is now up to political institutions to take the final decision. The project is currently under review by the **European Council and the European Parliament**, meaning it is entering its final phase before potential approval.

The key focus now is how the digital euro will function in real-world scenarios — including **offline payments**, integration with existing devices, and compatibility with current payment standards. This means the digital euro will not replace existing systems overnight, but will instead **be integrated into the current payment infrastructure**.

## Who is already involved — and why this is an important signal

The ECB is no longer operating at the level of concept — it has already opened applications for experts who will take part in building the digital euro. Through its Rulebook Development Group (RDG), the central bank is actively seeking industry professionals who will define how the digital euro will function in practice — from ATMs and point-of-sale terminals to the entire payment acceptance system.

This includes technical specifications for devices, communication standards, support for offline payments, and the integration of the digital euro into existing infrastructure across the eurozone.

Another part of the work focuses on certification and approval — defining who can participate in the system and under what conditions. Banks, fintech companies and payment service providers will need to go through testing and approval processes to support digital euro usage.

👉 In other words: the ECB is no longer planning — **it is building a system and actively looking for the people who will build it.**

## Who is already involved

The ECB has opened two major workstreams within its Rulebook Development Group (RDG). The first focuses on technical implementation — how ATMs and point-of-sale terminals will process digital euro payments, how devices will communicate, and how users will pay in stores across the eurozone.

The second workstream focuses on certification and approval processes — defining who can participate in the ecosystem and under what conditions. This includes **banks, fintech companies and payment service providers**, all of which will need to go through testing and approval processes to support digital euro payments.

Alongside the ECB initiative, a private alternative is already emerging. **A group of 12 European banks — including BBVA, ING and BNP Paribas — is developing a euro-pegged stablecoin under the Qivalis project**, with a planned launch in the second half of 2026. The goal is to enable blockchain-based payments without relying on dollar-backed tokens.

## The bigger picture: control over payments

This is no longer just about technology — it is about control. Today, most digital payments in Europe run through private networks such as Visa and Mastercard, while the global crypto ecosystem is dominated by dollar-based stablecoins.

The digital euro represents Europe’s attempt to regain control over its financial system and reduce dependence on external players. In that sense, the digital euro is not just a new currency — it is a **strategic project for Europe’s financial sovereignty**.

## When is the digital euro coming

If political institutions approve the project, the ECB has already signaled a potential rollout around **2029**. This means we are currently in a critical phase — the phase where the rules and infrastructure that will shape the future of payments in Europe are being defined.

## What this means for users

For citizens, the digital euro could mean faster, simpler and cheaper payments across the eurozone. One of the most important features is the ability to make **offline transactions**, bringing the digital euro closer to cash while maintaining the benefits of a digital system.

For banks and the financial sector, this represents a potential turning point. If users begin holding funds directly in digital euro wallets, it could significantly impact deposit structures and liquidity across the system.

## Conclusion

The digital euro is entering a phase where it is no longer theoretical, but becomes real infrastructure. The inclusion of ATMs, card terminals and retail payments shows that the ECB is preparing for everyday usage across the eurozone.

The key question is no longer whether the digital euro will exist, but how quickly it will become part of daily life.
