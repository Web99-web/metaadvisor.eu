# Sora is shutting down: we waited for it in Europe — but it will never arrive

Sora, one of the most well-known AI tools for generating video from text, is disappearing before it ever reached most users. OpenAI has decided to shut down the application and commercial layer of the project, bringing one of the most viral AI stories of recent years to an end.

For many users in Europe, this news carries additional weight. Sora was never truly available on the EU market, despite massive interest and expectations. In other words, a tool that was supposed to represent the future of video production never actually reached a large part of the world. As Metaadvisor.eu highlights, this says as much about the industry as it does about the product.

## From viral hype to a quiet decision

When Sora was first introduced, the internet was flooded with examples of AI-generated videos that looked almost cinematic. From realistic scenes to creative animations, it seemed that the boundary between real and generated content had completely disappeared.

But behind that impressive showcase was a different reality. Sora was never widely available, instead being used within limited circles — selected testers, partners, and creators. Even then, it became clear that there is a gap between what technology can demonstrate and what it can deliver as a scalable product.

## Expensive AI: the hidden problem

One of the main reasons behind the shutdown lies in cost. Video generation requires enormous computing power, far beyond what is needed for text or image models. Every second of video involves processing multiple frames, simulating motion, and maintaining scene consistency.

In practice, this means that scaling Sora to a mass audience would require extremely expensive infrastructure. At a time when AI companies are increasingly focused on sustainable business models, such projects quickly become financially questionable.

On top of that, monetization remains unclear. How much are users willing to pay for video generation? And can that price realistically cover the costs? These are the questions that often determine the fate of AI products.

## Europe: a market that never got Sora

Unlike the United States, where some users had limited access, Europe was largely left out. The reason was not a lack of interest, but a combination of regulatory and legal barriers.

The European Union has strict rules around privacy and artificial intelligence, including GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) and the AI Act. In such an environment, a tool capable of generating realistic people and scenes carries significant legal risk.

This means that even if Sora had not been shut down, its launch in Europe would likely have been slower, more limited, and heavily regulated.

## Deepfake and legal risks

Sora also raised serious concerns around deepfake technology. The ability to generate realistic-looking videos that are not real introduces potential misuse — from misinformation to manipulation of public opinion.

As a result, regulators and industry players are increasingly focused on controlling such technologies. The film and media industries have also expressed concern over the potential imitation of styles, characters, and content.

In this environment, developing video AI is not just a technical challenge, but a legal responsibility.

## Strategic shift: where OpenAI is heading

The shutdown of Sora fits into a broader strategic shift. OpenAI is increasingly focusing on tools with clear use cases and sustainable business models. This includes AI agents, automation, coding tools, and enterprise solutions.

Video AI, while impressive, does not currently deliver the same return on investment as other areas of artificial intelligence. This does not mean it will disappear, but rather that it will evolve more slowly and under stricter control.

## Conclusion

Sora did not disappear because it failed as a technology — but because it was not sustainable in its current form. It was ahead of the market, regulation, and business models needed to support it.

For users in Europe, the story has an additional layer. Sora is not a tool we lost — it is a tool we never actually got. As Metaadvisor.eu notes, this is a defining moment for how AI products will be built and delivered going forward.

## Our take (Metaadvisor)

- Sora shows that the AI hype cycle is entering a phase of real-world sustainability.  
- Video AI is technologically impressive, but currently difficult to sustain financially and legally.  
- Europe often lags behind in access to new AI tools due to regulation.  
- OpenAI is clearly shifting toward more profitable and practical AI segments.  
- Sora may have been too early — but video AI will return in a different form.
