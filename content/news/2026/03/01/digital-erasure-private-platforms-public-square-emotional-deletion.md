# Digital Erasure: When Private Platforms Become the Public Square and Emotionally Delete a Person

*The image is symbolic.*

In recent times, the same pattern is being heard across many social platforms: long-term users suddenly disappear. Without public explanation. Without warning. Without the opportunity to say goodbye or preserve their work. People who spent years participating in discussions, building reputation, sharing knowledge, humor, and personal stories — vanish overnight.

They do not leave voluntarily.  
They do not announce their departure.  
They do not say goodbye.  

They are simply no longer there, because they have been digitally erased.

People begin to protest and express disbelief. On one social platform, during a program discussion, users talked about exactly this — one or more individuals who disappeared overnight. After years of work, posts, debates, and community interaction. The entire group was in disbelief. In shock. They realized that the algorithm had simply nullified them. Those who remained were emotionally shaken. They wrote that they could not believe someone who had been part of their daily life for years was deleted without a word. The sense of grief and disbelief was so intense it felt tangible — as if someone had died and disappeared from real life.

This was not a cold discussion about terms of service. It was shock. And pain. The experience felt powerful — as if they had been digitally killed.

This is not an isolated case nor the personal perception of a single individual. More and more communities are noticing the same pattern. Active users who spent years building presence and relationships disappear without clear and public explanation. And the community is left without answers.

## The Digital Public Square and the Post-Covid Reality

Ten or fifteen years ago, the online space did not carry the same weight. Social networks were an addition to life. If someone disappeared from a forum or platform, there were other channels, other spaces, other meeting points. There were not as many online jobs or online stores either.

Then came Covid.

Lockdowns, isolation, bans on gatherings. The digital world became the only window to the world. People worked through screens, communicated, celebrated birthdays, debated politics, shared fear, grief, and hope. Many became more active than ever before because the online space was the only place where they could exist together.

Some lost real-life friendships during that period. At the same time, new ones were formed — digital ones. Some lost physical jobs but found online work, becoming even more tied to social networks. Relationships were built daily through comments, messages, shared discussions, and collaborative projects.

For younger generations, the digital world is not an alternative. It is the primary social space. Their friends are there. Their identity is there. Their sense of belonging is there.

In such a world, deleting a profile is not a technical act. It is a social rupture. It is isolation. It is social stigma. It can also mean the inability to work.

## A Shock That Is Not Just a Metaphor

When someone who has been active for years suddenly loses access, the reaction is not mere disappointment. People describe physical reactions: indescribable sadness, crying, a sense of darkness closing in, tightness in the chest, difficulty breathing, insomnia, disorientation. An inability to think rationally.

Only one question echoes: why? Why all my effort? Why can years of work disappear because of one or two posts the algorithm did not understand? Why without explanation? And why is there no one to appeal to after you are deleted?

On top of that, there is no warning. The user is simply deactivated without a single word of explanation.  
No deadline to save contacts.  
No opportunity to download archives.  
No space to notify people you have communicated with for years.

It feels like a digital execution.

For the person who is deleted, it can mean the loss of audience, the loss of reputation, the loss of income, and — most painfully — the loss of an identity built over years. Overnight, the individual experiences an existential, physical, and psychological shock.

## Digital Identity Is Real Identity

Digital identity today is not abstract. It is real. Someone may have been the funniest member of the community under an alias. Someone may have been an analyst whose insights were quoted. Someone may have been the voice of reason or the person who connected people.

An alias does not make something less real. The history of literature and journalism is full of pseudonyms. People wrote under different names for security reasons, because of their profession, social circumstances, or simply because they did not want public recognition.

Digital identity can represent professional capital, emotional value, and social belonging.

When that identity is erased, the network of relationships is erased as well.

## The Emptiness — and the Fear — Felt by Those Who Remain

Social platforms consistently underestimate the feelings of those who remain after a long-term member is erased. Within communities, people write that it feels as if a friend has disappeared. They do not know what happened. They cannot send a message. They cannot check whether the person is safe. They cannot reach them. The silence is not procedural — it is personal. This is not just a “profile disappearance.” It is emptiness.

Digital communities are not an illusion. People connect through ideas, humor, shared values, and interests — sometimes for years. They build routines around each other’s presence. When someone vanishes without a word, the community experiences it as an abrupt rupture of relationships. The emotional impact of deleting a long-term active member does not affect just one person; it reverberates through an entire active community, a digital “family,” a group that suddenly feels destabilized.

But alongside grief, another emotion quietly spreads: fear. Members begin asking themselves what exactly triggered the algorithm. Was it a word? A link? A political opinion? A sarcastic remark? If someone who wrote thousands of acceptable posts can suddenly be erased, then what is truly safe? What am I allowed to say now? Can a link I share lead to suspension? Can irony be misread? Can context disappear inside code?

This uncertainty changes behavior. People begin to calculate every sentence. They soften their opinions. They avoid controversial topics. They hesitate before posting. Some stop speaking altogether. The digital public square, once a place of open participation, slowly transforms into a space shaped by caution.

When participation itself begins to feel risky, self-censorship becomes a survival instinct. And that quiet, spreading fear may be one of the most profound and least discussed consequences of opaque algorithmic moderation.

## The Silent Are Not at Risk. The Active Are.

There is another uncomfortable reality: those who remain silent are rarely affected. Observers who do not participate.

Under scrutiny are the active users. Those who comment. Those who debate. Those who have opinions or knowledge. Those who analyze markets or politics. Those who expose irregularities.

Visibility carries risk. Silence is low-risk.

This creates the perception that the system punishes engagement.

The paradox becomes even stronger when users simultaneously see that platforms continue to host profiles publishing extremely violent content — recordings of fights, assaults, weapons — while a satirical comment, slang, or critical analysis may be interpreted as a more serious violation.

Without clear and transparent criteria, such disproportionality appears illogical and unjust.

## Private Platform — But Public Function

Yes, social networks are private companies. They have the right to define their terms of use.

But when a platform becomes the main infrastructure of public discourse — the place where political debates, financial analysis, and social activism unfold — it assumes a public function.

When a private space becomes a public square, the question of responsibility arises.

If an algorithmic decision can physically and emotionally destabilize a real person, then this is no longer merely a technical issue. It is a societal issue.

## The Digital Services Act and the Legal Dimension

The European Union, through the Digital Services Act, seeks to increase transparency and introduce the obligation to justify content removal decisions. Platforms are expected to provide appeal mechanisms and proportional measures.

This is recognition that digital identity holds real value and that suspension decisions cannot remain entirely opaque.

When digital space has become an infrastructural component of society, exclusion from that space carries consequences beyond technical terms of service.

## Conclusion: Between Algorithm and Human Being

This is not about abolishing moderation. Hate speech and fraud must have consequences.

But when an algorithmic decision can emotionally destabilize a real person and simultaneously shock an entire community, we are no longer speaking only about technology. We are speaking about people.

In a post-Covid world, the digital public square has become a real living space. Deleting a profile can mean social stigmatization and loss of belonging.

If platforms have become the public square, then digital rules must also carry digital responsibility.
