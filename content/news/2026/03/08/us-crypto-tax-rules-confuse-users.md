# New U.S. crypto tax rules confuse users

The cryptocurrency market in the United States is entering a new phase of regulation. **New tax reporting rules for digital assets are being implemented**, and according to major crypto exchange **Coinbase**, the system may create significant complications for millions of everyday users.

Under new guidelines introduced by the **IRS (Internal Revenue Service — the U.S. federal tax authority)**, crypto trading platforms must begin reporting detailed information about users’ digital asset transactions. The new reporting framework revolves around the **1099-DA form**, a document specifically designed to bring cryptocurrency taxation closer to the reporting standards used in traditional financial markets.

The goal of the regulation is straightforward: improve transparency and ensure that gains from cryptocurrency trading are properly reported. However, the reality of the crypto ecosystem is far more complex than the traditional stock market, where investors typically execute only a limited number of trades each year.

Crypto users frequently interact with wallets, decentralized applications, token swaps, and multiple blockchain networks. Each of these interactions can generate a transaction that may have tax implications — and that complexity is at the heart of Coinbase’s concerns.

## The new 1099-DA reporting form

At the center of the new regulatory framework is **Form 1099-DA**, which crypto platforms must send both to the IRS and to their customers. The document is intended to function similarly to existing tax forms used for reporting stock trading activity.

Coinbase has already begun distributing **millions of these forms to U.S. crypto users**. However, according to **Lawrence Zlatkin**, Coinbase’s Vice President of Tax, the system introduces practical complications that could disproportionately affect retail investors.

One key issue is that exchanges will initially report only the **gross proceeds from the sale of digital assets**, rather than the full context of each trade. Specifically, platforms will not always provide the **cost basis** — the original purchase price of the cryptocurrency.

This missing piece of information creates an important gap in the reporting process.

## Users must calculate gains and losses themselves

If a user sells cryptocurrency for $10,000, the IRS will receive information confirming that the sale occurred. What the tax authority may not know is how much the asset originally cost.

If the Bitcoin was purchased for $8,000, the user realized a $2,000 profit. If it was purchased for $12,000, the sale would actually represent a loss. Without the cost basis data, the IRS cannot determine the actual gain or loss.

As a result, **the responsibility for calculating the correct taxable outcome falls on the user**.

This means individuals must track their own transaction history, often across multiple platforms and wallets, in order to calculate their net gains or losses accurately. For professional traders using portfolio tracking software, this process may be manageable. For casual investors who occasionally buy or sell small amounts of crypto, however, it can quickly become complicated.

## Stablecoins and gas fees add additional complexity

Another area of concern highlighted by Coinbase involves transactions that do not necessarily reflect meaningful financial changes but must still be reported.

One example is **stablecoins** — digital currencies such as USDC or USDT that are designed to maintain a stable value tied to the U.S. dollar. Because their price rarely fluctuates significantly, many stablecoin transactions produce little or no actual gain or loss.

Nevertheless, these transactions still appear in the reporting data.

In addition, there are **gas fees**, the small network fees required to process transactions on blockchains such as Ethereum. Although these fees are often minimal, they can accumulate over time and appear as additional entries within a user’s transaction history.

When combined with decentralized finance activities, token swaps, and transfers between wallets, the result can be **dozens or even hundreds of taxable transaction records** for a single user.

## Crypto regulation enters a new stage

The situation highlights the broader challenge regulators face when attempting to integrate cryptocurrency into traditional financial and tax systems.

Traditional financial markets have developed standardized reporting structures over many decades. The cryptocurrency ecosystem, by contrast, evolves rapidly and includes a wide variety of new technologies — from decentralized exchanges and staking mechanisms to complex DeFi protocols.

Regulators in the United States are attempting to introduce clearer oversight and reduce tax evasion risks. However, industry participants warn that overly complicated reporting frameworks could discourage participation from smaller retail investors.

## Coinbase calls for a more practical approach

According to Coinbase representatives, tax enforcement efforts should focus more heavily on **large transactions and institutional market participants**, rather than imposing administrative burdens on millions of small retail users.

For someone trading modest amounts — perhaps $50 or $100 at a time — the administrative work required to calculate and report gains may exceed the actual tax owed.

This raises a broader question for policymakers: how can governments design a tax framework that ensures compliance without creating unnecessary complexity for everyday participants?

## What happens next

The new reporting system is still in its early stages, and the upcoming tax seasons will provide the first real test of how the framework functions in practice.

Many analysts expect the rules to evolve over time, particularly as regulators gain more insight into issues such as cost basis tracking, micro-transactions, and blockchain network fees.

One thing, however, is already clear: **cryptocurrency is increasingly moving into the realm of formal financial regulation**.

As digital assets become a larger part of the global financial system, governments around the world are working to integrate them into existing legal and taxation frameworks.
