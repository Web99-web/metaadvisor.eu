# Bitcoin strategy of large investors: why some never sell BTC but borrow against it

In the world of Bitcoin, a strategy often described as **“buy, borrow, never sell”** is increasingly being discussed. The idea is simple, but for many people it still sounds unusual: instead of selling Bitcoin when they need money, investors use it as **loan collateral** and obtain liquidity while keeping their BTC position.

For long-term investors, this model is attractive because it allows them to continue participating in potential Bitcoin price growth while still gaining access to cash. As **Metaadvisor.eu** notes, this strategy did not originate in the crypto world. Wealthy investors have used a similar model for decades in traditional finance by borrowing against stocks, real estate, or other valuable assets instead of selling them.

That is why more and more people are beginning to see Bitcoin not only as a speculative asset, but as **a new form of digital collateral**.

## How “borrow against BTC” actually works

In basic terms, an investor first buys and holds Bitcoin. If they later need money, they do not necessarily have to sell their BTC. Instead, they can deposit it as collateral on a lending platform and receive a loan in fiat currency, stablecoins, or other assets.

In practice, it may look like this: someone owns **1 BTC**, whose market value is €70,000. That Bitcoin is deposited as collateral, and the platform may approve a loan based on part of that value — for example €10,000 or €20,000, depending on platform rules and collateral ratios.

The Bitcoin remains locked as security during the loan period, but the investor still retains economic exposure to that asset. If the price of Bitcoin rises, they still benefit from that increase even though they did not sell it.

This is why the strategy is mainly used by people who believe BTC could be worth significantly more in the long term.

## Why some investors do not want to sell Bitcoin

One of the main reasons is **taxation**. In many countries, selling Bitcoin may trigger capital gains tax. If someone bought BTC at a much lower price and sells it today, the difference between the purchase and selling price may become taxable profit.

If someone instead takes a loan against BTC, no sale has occurred and therefore **no taxable event is usually created**. That is one reason why this model is often discussed outside crypto as well, particularly among wealthy investors.

Another important reason is **maintaining exposure to price growth**. If an investor sells Bitcoin to obtain cash, they no longer benefit if BTC later rises significantly. If they borrow against it instead, they keep their position and may still participate in future price increases.

The third reason is flexibility. If Bitcoin’s price increases in the meantime, the collateral becomes more valuable, allowing the investor to refinance the loan or take another one under better conditions.

## Where this can actually be done

For most ordinary people, this is often the most confusing part of the story. These transactions are not done in a regular wallet, but on **lending platforms** that accept Bitcoin as collateral.

On centralized platforms, these are typically services such as **Nexo**, **Ledn**, or similar lending companies that offer loans backed by BTC. The user transfers Bitcoin to the platform, it locks the BTC as collateral, and a loan is issued.

There are also decentralized options, but they are technically more complex and often use not “pure” BTC but a tokenized version such as **WBTC (Wrapped Bitcoin)** that is used within DeFi protocols.

After the collapse of platforms like **Celsius, BlockFi, and Genesis**, many investors became far more cautious about models where a platform directly holds their BTC. As a result, newer models such as **multisig escrow lending** have appeared, where the platform does not have full control over the collateral.

This shows that the market is still searching for safer ways to use Bitcoin as financial collateral.

## Platforms where borrowing against Bitcoin is possible

Although the concept of borrowing against BTC sounds simple, most people are interested in a very practical question: **where can this actually be done?**

Today there are several models — from centralized platforms to decentralized DeFi protocols.

### Nexo

**Nexo** is one of the largest crypto lending platforms in Europe. Users can deposit Bitcoin as collateral and receive loans in euros, dollars, or stablecoins. The platform is particularly popular among investors who want quick liquidity without selling BTC.

### Ledn

**Ledn** is a platform focused almost exclusively on Bitcoin. Many long-term BTC investors use Ledn for **Bitcoin-backed loans**, meaning loans secured by Bitcoin.

### Binance Loans

The crypto exchange **Binance** also allows users to take loans using cryptocurrencies as collateral, including Bitcoin. Users can borrow stablecoins or other cryptocurrencies while their BTC remains locked as collateral.

### Aave (DeFi)

Within decentralized finance (**DeFi**), it is possible to borrow using **Wrapped Bitcoin (WBTC)** as collateral. One of the best-known protocols for this is **Aave**, where users can deposit WBTC and borrow stablecoins such as USDC or DAI.

## Newer model: safer Bitcoin loans

After the collapse of large crypto lending platforms such as **Celsius**, **BlockFi**, and **Genesis**, many investors became much more cautious about models where a platform directly holds their Bitcoin.

As a result, new projects have emerged that attempt to reduce the risk.

### Firefish

**Firefish** uses a model in which Bitcoin is held in a **multisig escrow wallet**, meaning the platform does not have full control over users’ funds.

### Debifi

A similar approach is used by **Debifi**, where loans are arranged between investors and lenders using Bitcoin as collateral without requiring a single centralized company to hold full control of the BTC.

## How much can actually be borrowed

This depends on the so-called **LTV (Loan-to-Value)** ratio, which represents the relationship between the loan and the value of the collateral. If a platform allows a maximum LTV of 50%, it means that with collateral worth €70,000 you could theoretically obtain up to €35,000 in loans.

However, serious investors rarely go to the maximum. Many use **very low LTV ratios, often between 10% and 20%**, leaving a much larger safety buffer if the price of Bitcoin drops.

This is an important difference between aggressive speculation and a more conservative approach. If someone takes too large a loan relative to the collateral, even a normal drop in BTC price may lead to **liquidation**, meaning forced sale of the collateral to cover the debt.

That is why this strategy is often romanticized. On paper it may look elegant, but in reality it requires discipline, understanding of risk, and caution.

## Who uses this approach

One of the most well-known public examples is **Michael Saylor** and his company Strategy, formerly known as MicroStrategy. Although the company uses more complex financial instruments such as bonds and corporate debt, the core logic is similar: **Bitcoin is not sold lightly**, but treated as a strategic long-term asset.

A similar philosophy is used by some hedge funds, venture capital funds, and wealthy private investors who view Bitcoin as a long-term reserve asset.

As **Metaadvisor** often points out, this is no longer only about trading but about a shift in how digital assets fit into the financial system. If Bitcoin becomes widely accepted as collateral, it could change how people think about savings, loans, and financial independence.

## Risks that many underestimate

The biggest risk is obviously **a decline in Bitcoin’s price**. If the loan is too high relative to the collateral, an investor may lose part or even all of their BTC through liquidation.

Another risk is **counterparty risk**, meaning the risk of the platform itself. If the company holding the collateral runs into problems, users may find themselves in a very unpleasant situation. This happened to many users during the crypto crisis of 2022.

The third risk is psychological. Once investors start using their assets as collateral, it is easy to fall into a pattern of constant refinancing and increasing leverage. In good market conditions this may look smart, but in bad market cycles it can become very dangerous.

That is why this strategy is not “free money”, but a sophisticated financial tool that can only be useful when used carefully.

## Could Bitcoin become a new form of digital collateral?

This may be the most important question. If more investors, funds, and fintech platforms begin to treat Bitcoin as an asset that can be borrowed against, BTC could gain a new role in global finance.

In that scenario, Bitcoin would not only be a trading or savings instrument, but also a **foundation for credit infrastructure**. Just as loans today are taken against real estate or stocks, borrowing against Bitcoin could become much more widespread and standardized in the future.

For now, this model is still in development and carries significant risks. However, the trend is clear: as the market matures, more sophisticated ways of using Bitcoin within the broader financial system are emerging.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

- **The “buy, borrow, never sell” strategy shows that Bitcoin is increasingly entering the logic of traditional finance.**
- **The biggest advantage of this model is the ability to obtain liquidity without selling BTC and without an immediate taxable event.**
- **The biggest risk remains liquidation of collateral if Bitcoin’s price drops sharply.**
- **For ordinary users, it is crucial to understand where and how this works and what risks the platform itself carries.**
- **If Bitcoin eventually becomes widely accepted as standard digital collateral, it could represent one of the larger shifts in the financial system in the coming years.**
