# Gemini: Facebook AI banned up to 20 million accounts in early 2026

If the estimates are correct, the scale of **AI moderation** on Facebook at the beginning of 2026 is astonishing. According to an analysis presented by Google’s AI system **Gemini**, in the first two months of 2026 alone, **up to 20 million Facebook accounts may have been banned or suspended**. When that figure is translated into the speed at which algorithms operate, the scale becomes even more dramatic: around **330,000 bans per day**, nearly **14,000 per hour**, and approximately **230 accounts every minute**. In other words — at the start of 2026, Facebook’s algorithms are banning **almost four accounts every second**.

An even more shocking part of the estimate concerns so-called **false positive cases**, situations in which an algorithm wrongly identifies a legitimate user as someone violating platform rules. Estimates suggest that **more than 1.5 million users may have been wrongfully banned in just two months**, which means that potentially **25,000 people per day** lost their accounts because of an algorithmic mistake. In other words, nearly **17 users every minute** may have lost their Facebook profiles because of an automated artificial intelligence decision.

## The stories that started spreading across the internet

Over the past few months, and especially at the start of 2026, unusual stories began appearing on forums and in tech communities. Long-time Facebook users — some with profiles that were ten or even fifteen years old — started reporting that their accounts were simply being shut down without warning and without any clear explanation. In many cases, the only message they received was a short automated notice saying their account had violated the platform’s rules.

A particularly large number of such experiences began showing up on forums such as **Reddit**, where users described almost identical situations: the account disappears overnight, the appeal process is handled by an automated system, and the responses they receive also come from AI. Human support in these cases practically does not exist. Because of the growing number of such stories, we decided to check what the available data and artificial intelligence systems say about this phenomenon. Since Google indexes a large part of the internet, it seemed logical to assume that its AI system **Gemini** might have a broader overview of this trend.

We asked a simple question: **how many Facebook accounts were banned in early 2026?**

The answer given by Google’s AI system **Gemini** was surprisingly dramatic. According to that estimate, **between 18 and 20 million Facebook accounts may have been suspended or permanently deleted in the first two months of 2026**.

Gemini lists several key estimates for the January–February 2026 period:

- **Total suspended or deleted accounts:** approximately **18–20 million**
- **Accounts removed بسبب age restrictions (under 16):** around **550,000** cases (according to Meta’s data)
- **Wrongfully banned profiles (“AI glitch” or false positive):** estimates speak of **more than 1.5 million cases worldwide**

In other words, even if only part of these estimates is correct, we are talking about **millions of user accounts removed in just two months**, with **hundreds of thousands or even more than a million people potentially becoming collateral damage of automated moderation systems.**

## What 20 million banned accounts actually means

When people hear about millions of removed accounts, many instinctively assume those are bots, spam profiles or scammers. Meta often talks about exactly that in its official reports — fake account removals and the fight against spam networks. But the stories appearing on forums suggest that among those accounts there are often **real people** as well.

If even part of the estimates is correct, those profiles could include **someone’s friends, colleagues or family members, but also marketing agencies, small business owners and administrators of business pages** who used Facebook to run their operations or stay in touch with clients. When such an account disappears, access disappears too — to contacts, business pages, advertising campaigns, private photos and years of digital history. For some users, that means losing **an entire digital identity**, an archive of life built on the platform over many years.

## When the algorithm gets it wrong: real examples of **false positive bans**

One of the most controversial issues appearing in discussions about Facebook’s content moderation is the so-called **false positive case**. This refers to situations in which an artificial intelligence algorithm **misinterprets content** and automatically labels a user as someone violating platform rules. In such cases, an account can be **suspended or permanently shut down without any real human review**, while the appeal process is often handled again by — another algorithm.

Users in such situations find themselves in a kind of digital limbo: the account is gone, and **contact with a real person is almost impossible**. That is exactly why more and more people are beginning to publicly share examples where bans are clearly the result of a system error.

### **Example 1: Family photos labeled as “Child Sexual Exploitation”**

**Many ordinary users were banned because of completely harmless family photos.** Numerous cases have been documented on forums and social media in which parents posted photos of their children on the beach or during bath time, after which the algorithm labeled that content as **Child Sexual Exploitation (CSE)**.

The AI system often reacts to **the detection of bare skin or certain poses**, without understanding the context of a family moment. In some cases, that label leads to an **immediate and permanent suspension of the account**, with no human review at all. In the process, users do not just lose access to the platform — they also lose **thousands of photos, messages and contacts** that had been stored on their profiles for years.

### **Example 2: A historical photo declared “drug promotion”**

Another often-cited example concerns a user who posted **a photo of an old 19th-century advertisement for cough syrup**. At the time, some medicines contained ingredients such as cocaine, which is a well-known historical fact today. But the algorithm recognized the word **“cocaine”** and automatically marked the post as **drug promotion**, after which the user’s account was permanently suspended.

Cases like this show how much difficulty algorithms can have with **understanding historical context, irony or educational content**.

### **Example 3: “Heroine” becomes “heroin”**

In one well-known case, a page promoting female heroes and using the word **“Heroine”** in its posts was also suspended. The AI system interpreted the word as **“heroin”**, the drug, and automatically flagged the content as the promotion of illegal substances.

These kinds of mistakes are especially problematic because they happen due to **language nuances**, which algorithms often misread.

{{< support2 >}}

### **Example 4: AI “hallucinations” in comments**

There are also cases in which users say their comments **did not mention drugs, loans or financial fraud in any way**, yet the algorithm still concluded that the posts were promoting such activities. In those situations, users often receive a notice saying they violated rules related to **financial scams or illegal trade**, even though their posts contain no such elements.

Critics describe such situations as **“AI hallucinations”**, because the system sees something in the content that was **neither written nor implied**.

### **Example 5: VPN use labeled as a “cybersecurity threat”**

Another example concerns users who rely on **VPNs to protect their privacy**. Automated security systems can interpret such behavior as **an attempt to hide identity or a possible hacking attack**, after which the account is locked for alleged **“cybersecurity” reasons**.

But the question of identity on the internet has another dimension as well. Internet history shows that many important disclosures entered the public sphere precisely thanks to **anonymous or pseudonymous profiles**. Over the years, many users published information about major scandals, including cases such as **Jeffrey Epstein and Prince Andrew**, precisely because they feared for their own safety, their jobs, or the safety of their families.

There are still thousands of such profiles today — from people trying to expose **corruption and abuse of power**, to citizens living under repressive regimes where openly criticizing authorities can lead to serious consequences. Because of this, critics warn that **automatically treating anonymity as suspicious behavior can have much broader consequences for freedom of expression on the internet.**
Critics of such decisions ask a logical question: **if someone uses a VPN precisely to protect their privacy, why should that user be treated as a potential security threat?**

## Tens of thousands of small businesses vanished overnight

The problem does not affect only private users. **Reports from Reddit and technology forums suggest that tens of thousands of small business accounts disappeared in the first two months of 2026.** In many cases, these were small entrepreneurs, marketing agencies or freelancers who used Facebook to run advertising and communicate with clients.

When such an account disappears, access to business pages, advertising campaigns and customer communication disappears as well. For many small companies, Facebook is not just a social network but **their main sales channel**, so a ban can also mean an **immediate loss of income**.

Because of such situations, **in 2026 more and more people are beginning to join class-action lawsuits** against Meta, claiming that this amounts to **an arbitrary disruption of digital infrastructure on which their businesses depend**.

## The main problem: a ban without any real chance to defend yourself

It is important to emphasize that most users do not have a problem with content moderation on social networks. The vast majority of people agree that platforms must remove illegal content, spam, scams or organized crime. The problem begins the moment **the algorithm makes a mistake**, while the user has **no real possibility to appeal or gain access to human support**.

Users who lost their accounts usually complain about two key things.

The first is the **complete impossibility of contacting a human being**. Since AI systems make decisions about bans, appeals are often also handled by algorithms. That creates a **vicious circle of automation**, in which the user receives generic responses without any genuine review of the case.

The second is the **loss of digital history**. Many users lose access to profiles that are **10, 15 or even 20 years old**, along with photos, contacts, business pages and private messages. For some people, Facebook is not just a social network but **a digital archive of life**, and its disappearance can mean losing a large part of their personal and business history.

In other words, the problem is not that the platform moderates content — the problem arises when **a long-standing account with thousands of photos and millions of posts can be permanently shut down because of a few mistaken flags**, without any chance for the user to explain the situation to a real person.

## The EU begins to respond: Digital Services Act

Unlike many other parts of the world, the **European Union has in recent years been trying to introduce stricter rules for major technology platforms**. Facebook, or rather Meta, is classified in the EU as a **“Very Large Online Platform” (VLOP)**, which means it is subject to additional oversight under the **Digital Services Act (DSA)** framework.

One of the key ideas behind that law is that **users must have the right to a transparent appeal process** if a platform removes their content or blocks their account.

If a user believes their profile was unfairly banned, there are several options in Europe.

### Direct complaint to the European Commission

EU citizens can report systemic problems with platform moderation directly to the European Commission through the official channel:

**CNECT-DSA-COMPLAINTS@ec.europa.eu**

Such complaints do not solve individual cases within a few days, but they serve as **evidence for regulators** that platforms may not be respecting their obligations under the DSA. If it is established that a moderation system seriously violates the rules, the European Commission can impose fines of up to **6% of a company’s global annual turnover**.

For technology giants like Meta, that could mean **fines worth billions of euros**.

---

## Appeals Centre Europe – a new institution for disputes

Another new development in Europe is the creation of **independent bodies for resolving disputes between users and major platforms**, which is also part of the DSA framework.

One such body is **Appeals Centre Europe**, where users can initiate proceedings if they believe a platform decision was unfair.

In such proceedings:

- the dispute is handled by **an independent body outside Meta**
- the process is **free for users or subject only to a symbolic fee**
- if the user wins the case, **Meta must restore the account and cover the costs of the procedure**

According to reports from **March 2026**, such bodies overturn **more than 75% of disputed platform decisions**, which raises an additional question about how reliable AI systems really are in content moderation.

---
## Class actions are already being prepared in Europe

At the same time, **organized legal initiatives against Meta** are beginning to appear in Europe as well.

According to information from legal and technology forums, **law firms in Germany and the Netherlands are already gathering users** who lost access to their business or personal accounts because of automated bans. Such cases are increasingly being turned into **collective legal actions (class-action lawsuits)**, arguing that Meta **through arbitrary algorithmic decisions cut off digital services on which many users’ businesses and income depend**.

One important legal precedent comes from Germany. In one case, a court in Düsseldorf concluded that a platform **cannot permanently block a user account without giving the user a real opportunity to respond before a human being**. Such rulings are increasingly being used as arguments in disputes against major technology platforms.

## Digital infrastructure of modern life

All of these discussions open a broader question: **what happens when a private platform becomes key infrastructure for communication, work and the social lives of billions of people?**

Facebook is now used by more than **three billion people worldwide**, and for many small companies and entrepreneurs the platform represents the main channel of communication with customers. When an algorithm makes a mistake and permanently shuts down an account, the consequences can be far greater than the simple loss of a social media profile.

In such situations, **contacts, business pages, marketing campaigns and years of digital content** disappear. That is precisely why more and more experts are warning that the issue of content moderation is no longer just a technical question of algorithms, but also **a matter of citizens’ digital rights**.

If the estimates are correct and if up to **20 million accounts really were banned at the start of 2026**, with a possible **1.5 million wrongful suspensions**, then the statistics are no longer speaking only about content moderation.

They are speaking about the fact that among those profiles **there may also be our friends, colleagues or business partners** — people who lost access to their digital identity overnight.

And that is precisely why more and more regulators in Europe are trying to ask the question that will become crucial in the years ahead:

**Who actually controls the digital lives of billions of people — algorithms or society?**

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

No reasonable person is against moderating content on large platforms. Social networks must remove illegal content, scams, spam and violence. The problem begins the moment **the algorithm makes a mistake**, while the user **has no possibility to explain their situation to a real person**.

Over the past few years, Meta has gradually removed almost all public customer support channels. Active email contacts practically no longer exist, and when a user loses access to an account there is often **no way left to contact the platform at all**. In such a situation, the user remains outside the system without any possibility of requesting a human review of the decision.

At the same time, the broader social context is completely different from what it was ten or fifteen years ago. During the COVID-19 pandemic, millions of people began connecting even more intensely online. Many gained **friends, business contacts and communities around the world** through social media, and in many cases Facebook remained **the only communication channel** with those people.

Because of this, many experts today believe that platforms such as Facebook are no longer just private technology companies, but **a global digital public square** where billions of people communicate every day.

**The key question, therefore, is not whether content should be moderated — but how to protect users when the algorithm gets it wrong.**

The most common criticisms users express today can be reduced to several basic problems:

- **the complete disappearance of publicly available email contacts for Meta**, which often leaves users with no way to even reach the company  
- **the impossibility of speaking to human support** after a permanent account suspension  
- **the loss of decades of digital history** — photos, contacts, messages and business pages  
- the fact that Facebook has become for many people **the only channel of communication with friends and business partners around the world**

If a platform on which **three billion people communicate** becomes key infrastructure of modern society, then a logical question also arises:

**should users in such a system have at least a basic right to defend themselves when the algorithm makes a mistake?**

That is exactly why regulators in Europe, through the **Digital Services Act**, are increasingly trying to introduce rules that would ensure algorithmic decisions can be reviewed — and that users have the right to a **real, human review of disputed bans.**

**In a world where a handful of technology platforms control the communication of billions of people, the issue of algorithmic bans is no longer just technical — it is about how many people lose access to their own digital lives. Today, that is both a social and a political question.**
