# A single user sued Meta and YouTube for addictive design — and won $6 million

## A verdict that could change how we view social networks

A court in the United States has delivered a ruling that could have far-reaching consequences for the entire technology industry. Meta and YouTube were found responsible for designing their platforms in ways that encourage addictive behavior, and the jury decided they must pay a total of $6 million in damages. It is particularly significant that Meta carries around 70% of the responsibility, while the remaining 30% is attributed to Google, specifically YouTube.

What makes this ruling exceptional is not only the amount of compensation, but the fact that it was not driven by a class action or a government institution. At the center of the case is a single user, now around twenty years old, who began using these platforms as a child. That relationship — an individual versus technological giants — gives additional weight to the case and raises the question of how accountable such systems truly are to their users.

## Platforms are not neutral — they are designed

During the trial, the focus was not only on the content users consume, but on the structure of the platforms themselves. The court recognized what has been discussed for years: elements such as infinite scrolling, autoplay videos, notifications, and algorithmic recommendations are not accidental. They are intentionally designed to hold user attention and extend the time spent on the platform.

Such an approach has a clear business logic — more time on the platform means more data, more advertising, and higher revenue. However, in this case, the court concluded that such design can also have serious consequences, especially for younger users who are more vulnerable to developing compulsive behavioral patterns.

## From “post more” to “spam”: where the platform contradicts itself

At the same time, platforms do not just retain users — they actively push them toward more engagement. Facebook page administrators are regularly shown weekly goals and recommendations: post more videos, increase interactions, respond to comments, stay active. The system rewards activity and creates the impression that constant posting is the key to visibility and success.

But this is where one of the biggest contradictions of the entire system emerges. Facebook has spent years encouraging users to create and manage groups and pages, often in large numbers within a single account. These individuals have built communities for free — from tourism and travel to cars, real estate, and local initiatives — moderating discussions, removing fake profiles, and protecting other users from scams.

In many cases, they were the ones maintaining the quality of content and the safety of the community — without any compensation from Facebook.

And then the shift happens. If those same administrators try to promote their own pages or content within their own groups, the system may suddenly label them as spammers. They can moderate others for free, but their own posts may be classified as networking or spam. The boundary between “desired activity” and “rule violation” remains unclear, and decisions are made automatically.

The contradiction is obvious: what was encouraged as engagement yesterday can be punished as abuse today — within the same system that built and rewarded that behavior for years.

## One click — and everything is gone — the platform deletes them — forever

In practice, this means that users who have spent years building their presence on a platform can suddenly lose everything. Profiles are removed, pages disappear, and groups that users have developed over years stop functioning. At the same time, in some cases, Meta automatically disables the entire user account without clear explanation — Facebook together with Messenger and Instagram — causing contacts, communication, and business relationships built over years within the system to vanish.

The problem is further worsened by the fact that many users do not have backups of their contacts. In practice, this means that some relationships existed exclusively within the Meta ecosystem, without any private contact details exchanged outside the platform. When the account disappears, so does the possibility of reconnecting. In many cases, there is no clear explanation and no real possibility of appeal or contact with a responsible person.

Such experiences are increasingly described as digital erasure. A system that has encouraged you for years to build a network, invest time, and develop relationships can remove you from its own ecosystem in a second. This aspect of the story is only now coming into focus and could become central in future legal proceedings.

## The pandemic as an accelerator of addiction

An important context that is often overlooked in these discussions is the COVID-19 pandemic period. During lockdowns, millions of people worldwide spent significantly more time online than ever before. Social networks, video platforms, and digital tools became almost the only means of communication, information, and maintaining contact with others.

During that period, the boundary between “normal use” and excessive dependence became even more blurred. What started as a necessary adaptation to circumstances turned into a lasting habit for many. Platforms recorded record levels of engagement during this time, while simultaneously further refining their algorithms and user retention mechanisms.

## The consequences appear later

For users who intensively used platforms during those years, the consequences were not always immediately visible. However, with time, it is becoming clearer that the behavioral patterns formed during that period have persisted even after returning to “normal” life.

Increasingly, issues such as reduced concentration, shorter attention spans, and difficulty focusing on learning are being reported, especially among children and young people who spent hours daily on digital platforms during that time. Such examples are no longer exceptions, but experiences shared by many families, further strengthening concerns about the long-term impact of these systems.

In the context of this ruling, this raises additional questions about responsibility. If platforms intensified their engagement and retention mechanisms during a period of increased user vulnerability, can their impact really be viewed separately from the broader social context in which they operated?

## This is just the beginning — more than 1000 lawsuits are waiting

But returning to the story of the young woman who sued them. This ruling is not an isolated case. On the contrary, it is a so-called test case within a much larger wave of lawsuits. More than 1000 similar cases have already been consolidated and are waiting for their turn in court. If further rulings confirm similar conclusions, technology companies could face serious financial and regulatory consequences.

That is why more and more analysts are drawing parallels with lawsuits against the tobacco industry. At the time, individual cases initially appeared limited, but over time they led to major changes, new regulations, and multi-billion-dollar settlements. Today’s situation in the technology sector shows similar patterns.

## How one person can sue Big Tech

Another important aspect of this case concerns how the lawsuit was financed. In the United States, such cases are often handled through a model in which lawyers work without upfront payment, taking a percentage of the compensation instead. This means individuals do not need significant initial funds to initiate legal action against large corporations.

This model is precisely what made it possible for this case to reach court. At the same time, it opens the door for other users to consider legal action, especially if they can prove concrete damages such as loss of income, contacts, or business opportunities.

## What this means for the future

Meta and Google have already announced an appeal, which means the legal battle is not over. Nevertheless, the ruling marks an important shift. For the first time, it has been clearly established that liability can arise not only from content, but also from the way a platform is designed.

This changes the perspective. The discussion is no longer just about what users do on platforms, but about how platforms shape user behavior. And that is a question that will become increasingly important in the years ahead.

## Our view (MetaAdvisor)

This ruling may appear to be a victory for users, but in reality, it is only the beginning of a much larger story. Platforms designed to capture attention are now, for the first time, seriously facing questions about responsibility for the consequences of that design. At the same time, another issue is coming into focus — automated decision-making and the possibility that a system can remove users from their own digital environment without human oversight.

If this trend continues, we may be witnessing one of the largest legal transformations in the technology industry, comparable to the changes that once impacted the tobacco sector.
