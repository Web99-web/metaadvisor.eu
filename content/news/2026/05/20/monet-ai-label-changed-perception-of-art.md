# When Monet Becomes “AI Slop”: A Viral Experiment Showed How Strongly a Label Changes Our Judgment of Art

A viral experiment on X showed how much our view of art can change the moment the label “Made with AI” appears next to an image. The anonymous conceptual artist SHL0MS posted an image from Monet’s **Water Lilies** series and wrote that he had just generated an image in Monet’s style using artificial intelligence. He then asked people to explain, in as much detail as possible, why the image was inferior to a real Monet.

The reactions were exactly what the experiment was meant to provoke. Many commenters began confidently analyzing the supposed weaknesses of the image: composition, colors, depth, reflections, “lack of soul” and artistic unconvincingness. There was only one problem: the image was not AI-generated. It was a real Monet painting. In other words, people were not necessarily judging only what they saw, but also what they thought they were looking at.

## The Label Changed the View Before the Analysis Even Began

This case is not interesting only because people made a mistake. People make mistakes every day, especially on social media. What is interesting is that the mistake was almost prepared in advance by one small piece of information: “this is AI”. As soon as the image received an AI label, part of the audience began searching for proof that it was bad, artificial, shallow or without value.

This is a very important moment in the debate about generative art. For a long time, we have heard claims that people can “always recognize AI”. But this experiment shows that reality is more complex. Sometimes people do not recognize AI. Sometimes they do not even recognize Monet, if they are told before looking that they are seeing AI.

In psychology, this could be connected to confirmation bias. Once we accept a certain assumption, we begin looking for signs that confirm what we already think. If someone believes that AI art is necessarily cold, empty and without authentic intention, then they will look for exactly those qualities in the image. Even if the image was created decades before modern AI generators.

## “AI Slop” as a New Internet Label

The phrase “AI slop” has been used more and more in recent months for content that people experience as generic, cheap, produced too quickly or without real human intention. The problem is that this label has started to be used even before any serious checking. Sometimes it is enough for an image to look “too smooth”, a text “too neat” or a composition “too strange” for part of the audience to immediately conclude that it is AI content.

This does not happen only with art. Something similar is already happening with texts, photographs, LinkedIn posts, comments, illustrations and even personal stories. People sometimes accuse real authors of using AI only because a text is clearly structured, a photograph is unusually lit or a style is different from what they expected. AI is therefore becoming not only a technology, but also a suspicion that sticks to content.

In Monet’s case, the absurdity is especially strong because he is one of the most famous painters of modern art. Monet’s paintings are not always “clean”, symmetrical or photographically precise. His impressionist style is often based on light, atmosphere, reflections, an unfinished impression and the visual vibration of a moment. Exactly what someone may declare an “AI mistake” may be part of the artistic language Monet used.

## What Are We Actually Judging: the Image or the Story Around the Image?

This experiment raises an uncomfortable question: do we judge a work of art according to what we see, or according to the story we receive before looking? If someone tells us the image is Monet, we may search for genius in it. If someone tells us it is AI, we may search for emptiness and mistakes in the very same brushstrokes.

That does not mean there is no difference between human and AI art. There is. Questions of authorship, intention, process, originality, training data and copyright are very serious. Many artists criticize, with good reason, the way AI models were trained on other people’s works without clear consent and compensation. But this case shows that the perception of the audience itself is not always a reliable judge.

When AI art is discussed, the debate often collapses into two extreme camps. One side claims that AI art is worthless because it has no soul, human labor or real intention. The other side claims that every resistance to AI is only fear of new technology. The truth is probably somewhere in between. AI can produce visually impressive images, but that does not solve questions of authorship and ethics. On the other hand, people can make very confident aesthetic judgments that fall apart as soon as the context changes.

## Art Is Increasingly Becoming a Question of Trust

In the digital age, an image is no longer just an image. It comes with metadata, description, label, author, platform, reputation, comments and algorithmic context. The way it is presented often shapes the way it will be understood. That is why the question of verifying the origin of content will become increasingly important in the future.

If a real Monet can be declared bad AI simply because someone labeled it that way, then the problem is not only in AI tools. The problem is also in our trust, our speed of reaction and our need to immediately take a side. Social networks reward fast, sharp and confident comments. They do not necessarily reward checking, patience and doubt about our own first impression.

This is especially important for media, artists and authors. In a world where content can be generated, copied, labeled, wrongly attributed and spread virally within minutes, it will become harder for audiences to distinguish between real, generated, edited and wrongly contextualized material. That is why labels such as “Made with AI” will be useful, but also dangerous if they turn into an automatic signal for contempt.

## The AI Debate Needs Less Panic and More Precision

This case does not prove that AI art is equal to Monet. That would be the wrong conclusion. It also does not prove that all critics of AI are wrong. Many problems around AI art remain open: copyright, compensation for artists, mass production of content, abuse, deepfakes and the flooding of the internet with generic visuals.

But the experiment shows something else very clearly: if people believe in advance that something is AI, they can begin seeing flaws even in the work of a great artist. This is a warning against superficial judgment. It is not enough to ask only “is this AI?”. We also need to ask who the author is, what the context is, how the work was created, why it was labeled in a certain way and whether we may have already made a judgment before we truly looked.

For Metaadvisor, this story is important because it shows that AI does not only change the tools for creating content. AI also changes the way we look at content. Even when AI is not actually present, the very thought of AI can change our interpretation, emotion and value judgment.

## Our Take (Metaadvisor)

* **The viral Monet experiment shows how strongly a label can change perception**, even when it is a real work by a famous artist.
* **The audience is not always a reliable detector of AI content**, especially when it already expects to see mistakes in advance.
* **Criticism of AI art has valid reasons**, but it must be precise and must not become an automatic rejection of everything that carries an AI label.
* **The biggest problem may not be only generative technology**, but also the speed with which people on social media draw confident conclusions without checking.
* **The future of art and media will increasingly depend on trust, context and transparent labeling of content.**
