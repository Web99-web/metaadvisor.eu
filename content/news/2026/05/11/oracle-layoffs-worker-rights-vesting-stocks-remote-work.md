# Oracle layoffs take a darker turn as former employees claim they lost millions through the vesting system

The Oracle layoffs themselves happened back on March 31, but the real controversy may only be starting now.

When news of the mass layoffs spread across social media on April 1, many people initially thought the reports were some kind of April Fool’s joke. But over the past few days, new details have emerged about former employees trying to negotiate better severance terms and protect parts of their stock-based compensation packages.

While the public mostly focused on reports that somewhere between 20,000 and 30,000 workers may have been affected, American media outlets are now paying closer attention to what happened after the layoffs — including internal pressure campaigns, online petitions and growing frustration surrounding RSU packages and the vesting system.

According to several reports, groups of former Oracle employees organized through internal communities and petitions in an attempt to pressure the company into offering better terms. So far, Oracle reportedly does not appear willing to make major concessions.

## The real issue is not only the layoffs — but lost RSU packages and vesting rights

For many former employees, the biggest issue is not the severance package itself.

The real problem involves lost RSU packages and the “vesting” system (future rights to company stock over time).

In large technology companies, employees are often compensated not only with salary but also through:
- RSU packages (Restricted Stock Units)
- stock options
- and long-term equity compensation.

The catch is that those shares do not immediately become the employee’s property.

Instead, they are distributed gradually through a multi-year process known as “vesting” (future rights to company stock over time), where employees must remain at the company long enough for the shares to officially become theirs.

In practice, that often means:
- part of the shares after the first year
- and the remaining portions over the next three or four years.

If an employee leaves the company or gets laid off before a major vesting date (future rights to company stock over time), the unvested portion is automatically lost.

That is why many former Oracle employees now claim they did not just lose their jobs — but also millions of dollars in future compensation they had spent years working toward.

## Some employees were reportedly only months away from major vesting dates

According to reports from TechCrunch and other American media outlets, some workers were allegedly only a few months away from major vesting dates (future rights to company stock over time).

One long-time employee reportedly lost around $1 million worth of stock that would have vested only four months later.

That is one of the reasons emotions around the layoffs have become so intense.

For many people in Silicon Valley, RSU packages and the vesting system (future rights to company stock over time) are often just as important — or even more important — than the monthly salary itself. Some employees intentionally remain at companies for a few additional months simply to reach a vesting milestone.

As a result, a growing number of people are now asking:
👉 were the layoffs timed just before major vesting dates (future rights to company stock over time)?

## Remote work may now be revealing its darker side

One of the most interesting aspects of the story involves remote work — a model that for years was promoted as the future of flexible and modern employment.

Some former Oracle employees claim they were officially classified as “remote workers” in states with weaker labor protections.

In the United States, worker protections depend heavily on the state where employees are officially registered. States like California and New York tend to have significantly stronger labor protections regarding mass layoffs and WARN Act enforcement.

The WARN Act (Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act) is a US law that in certain situations requires:
- earlier notice of mass layoffs
- longer warning periods
- or additional compensation for workers.

Some former employees now claim that because they were classified as remote workers, they may have lost access to parts of those protections.

And this is where the modern remote work paradox begins to emerge.

For years, remote work was promoted as:
- freedom
- flexibility
- and the future of employment.

But critics are now increasingly warning that the same model could also:
- make workers easier to replace
- simplify outsourcing
- and potentially help corporations bypass certain labor protections.

## AI infrastructure and data centers are becoming the real priority

At the same time, Oracle is aggressively investing billions of dollars into:
- AI infrastructure
- cloud operations
- and large-scale data centers.

The company is attempting to compete with Microsoft, Google and Amazon in the rapidly escalating AI race that is currently reshaping the global technology industry.

That is why some analysts no longer see this as a normal layoff cycle.

Instead, more people are beginning to ask:
👉 is the AI boom being financed by the very employees who helped build these companies in the first place?

## The tech industry may be entering a completely new phase

For more than a decade, Silicon Valley became associated with:
- massive salaries
- stock compensation packages
- flexible work
- and a sense of long-term stability.

But the AI boom and the infrastructure race are beginning to change those rules.

Companies are now pouring tens of billions of dollars into:
- AI models
- GPU infrastructure
- data centers
- and automation.

At the same time, pressure to reduce costs and improve efficiency continues to grow.

That is why some analysts believe the Oracle story could become an early sign of a much larger shift in how technology companies treat workers in the AI era.
