# MiCA Demands Discipline From Crypto Platforms, but Who Demands Efficiency From Bureaucrats?

The European Union introduced the MiCA regulation to bring order to the crypto market, protect users and force crypto platforms to operate under clearer rules. The idea sounds logical: if an exchange, wallet service or other crypto-asset service provider wants to operate in the EU, it must have the appropriate authorization, documentation, procedures, supervision and compliance.

But the case of Estonian Kriptomat raises an uncomfortable question that is often pushed under the carpet in regulation: why are private companies required to respect deadlines and strict obligations, while the efficiency of administration and the speed of processing applications still depend on the bureaucrats of each individual country?

Kriptomat has announced that it will gradually shut down its business by 30 June 2026, explaining that customer funds are safe and available, and that users can withdraw their funds or move to Kraken. The reason given is not a hack, insolvency or loss of assets, but regulatory uncertainty around MiCA authorization. It is especially important that the explanations mention that the timeline and unclear requirements of the process were not compatible with a sustainable and transparent continuation of the business.

## Why Is the Timeline So Important?

For a crypto platform, a license is not just a formality. Business plans, banking relationships, employees, customer support, marketing, technology investments and user trust all depend on it. If management does not know whether the license will be approved in a few months, in a year or perhaps not in time at all, it is difficult to continue operating responsibly.

That is exactly why this news is more important than the fate of one platform. It raises the question of the efficiency of European regulation in practice. The EU has defined what crypto companies must comply with, but the market can now see that the problem can also arise on the other side — in the speed and predictability of the administration itself.

## Kraken, Ireland and the Question of Equal Conditions

It is especially interesting that Kraken received its MiCA license from the Central Bank of Ireland and that this authorization enables it to provide regulated crypto services in all 30 countries of the European Economic Area. In other words, one regulator in one member state can open the door to the entire European market.

That is not a problem in itself. “Passporting” is one of the ideas of the single market. The problem arises if the speed, clarity and efficiency of licensing are not equal in all countries. In that case, the EU formally has a common framework, but the actual fate of an individual platform depends on the country in which it is waiting for the regulator.

If one regulator can carry out the process quickly and clearly, while another has no predictable timeline, an imbalance is created. Companies do not compete only with product, security and customer support, but also with regulatory luck — meaning where their application is being processed.

## Regulation Must Not Be a One-Way Obligation

MiCA demands seriousness from crypto platforms. That is understandable. After the collapse of platforms such as Celsius, BlockFi, Voyager and FTX, European regulators want to show that users must not be left unprotected. For years, the crypto industry had too many gray areas, too many promises and too little accountability.

But if discipline is demanded from platforms, the same logic must also be applied to the administration. If a company has a deadline, documentation and costs, then the body deciding on the application should also have a clear deadline, a transparent process and accountability for delays.

Otherwise, a very dangerous imbalance is created: a private company can be punished if it fails to meet its obligations, but the bureaucratic system faces no real consequence if the process drags on. For the company, this can mean the loss of reputation, market, users, employees and investments. For the administration, it is often just another file in the queue.

## Administrative Inefficiency Always Leaves a Bad Impression

One should not claim without evidence that the Kriptomat case involved intentional delay, favoritism toward large players or corruption. That would be irresponsible. But one should also not pretend that unclear and slow processes have no consequences.

When the administration has no clear deadline and a company has to live with the consequences of waiting, suspicion naturally arises. Administrative inefficiency always leaves room for a bad impression. In the best case, it looks like sluggishness. In the worst case, it can look like pressure, deliberate stalling or a system that favors those with enough money and legal teams to survive the wait.

This is especially sensitive in the crypto sector, where trust is hard to build and easy to lose. If users see smaller platforms giving up while large players receive licenses and take over the market, the question arises by itself: does the system protect users, or does it also create a market in which only the largest survive?

## Large Players Survive Bureaucracy More Easily

Large exchanges have lawyers, compliance teams, capital, regulatory advisers and the ability to work in several jurisdictions at the same time. If one process takes longer, they can redirect their strategy, use another license or wait without panic.

Smaller platforms do not have that luxury. For them, every month of uncertainty means cost. Salaries must be paid, systems maintained, users informed, banking partners reassured, and the market cannot grow normally while the regulatory future hangs in the air.

That is why MiCA, although designed as a framework for safety and order, can in practice accelerate market consolidation. Not because this is necessarily the official goal, but because the administrative burden and uncertainty naturally hit smaller players harder.

## If the EU Prescribes Obligations, It Should Also Prescribe Administrative Responsibility

The biggest question is not whether crypto should be regulated. It should. Users need protection, platforms need clear rules, and the market cannot function only on promises and marketing.

The question is why responsibility is not applied symmetrically. If the EU tells crypto platforms: “you must comply by a certain date,” why does it not also tell regulators: “you must process applications within a certain deadline”?

Without that, MiCA becomes a system in which one side has obligations and the other has power. And power without a clear deadline is always problematic, especially when it decides the survival of private companies.

ESMA says it is working with national competent authorities on a coordinated approach to authorizations of crypto-asset service providers during the MiCA transitional phase, which shows that the EU itself is aware of the problem of different implementation across member states. But a “coordinated approach” is not the same as a real guarantee of fast and predictable processing of applications.

## Our View (Metaadvisor)

* Bureaucrats of all countries, unite. While you sit in your chairs with secure monthly salaries and stretch out your own obligations, those on whom you impose rules cannot work, plan or develop their business normally.
* If discipline, deadlines, compliance and accountability are demanded from crypto platforms, then the same must also be demanded from those who approve licenses.
* We are not claiming that this is about intentional delay or favoritism toward large players, but the result can look very similar: smaller players shut down, users move to the large ones, and the market becomes more concentrated.
* If the EU wanted to bring order to the crypto market, it should have defined the obligations of regulators just as clearly.
* Regulation without clear deadlines is not legal certainty. It is pressure. And when only the largest can survive that pressure most easily, one has to ask whether the system truly protects users — or mainly creates a market in which small players no longer stand a chance.
