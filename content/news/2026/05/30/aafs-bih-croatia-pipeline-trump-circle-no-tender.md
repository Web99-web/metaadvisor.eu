# AAFS from the US to build BiH–Croatia pipeline: Trump’s circle and a billion-dollar deal without a tender

The Southern Interconnection project between Bosnia and Herzegovina and Croatia should, at least on paper, deliver something that has been considered strategically important for years: a new gas route that would give BiH an alternative to Russian gas and connect it to the Croatian network and the LNG terminal on Krk. But according to The Guardian, the story is no longer only about energy. At the center of attention is the US company AAFS Infrastructure and Energy, which has received a key role in a project worth around one billion dollars — apparently without a public tender.

That immediately changes the tone of the entire story. It is not disputed that BiH wants safer and more diversified access to energy, nor that Europe has been trying for years to reduce its dependence on Russian gas. What is disputed is how a relatively unknown company reached such a large and politically sensitive deal, and why the process moved forward without open competition.

## Who is AAFS and why did the story attract attention

The Guardian reports that AAFS Infrastructure and Energy is a US company connected to figures from Donald Trump’s political circle. That connection has naturally increased media interest, but the more important question is the company’s business track record. When a company without widely known experience in projects of this size receives a role in a billion-dollar infrastructure project, it is logical for the public to ask for more answers.

According to The Guardian, AAFS did not attract attention because it is a globally known energy giant, but precisely because it is a company that the wider public had barely heard of. Under normal circumstances, a project of this scale would include a detailed comparison of multiple bidders, a review of references, financial capacity, technical experience and clearly defined conditions. When that process is missing, the impression remains that the decision was made through a political-business route, not through open market competition.

## What is this company actually supposed to do

Here it is important to distinguish between an ordinary construction contractor and a company that receives a broader development role. According to The Guardian, AAFS is not only mentioned as a company that might participate in the project, but as a key partner for its development and implementation. In practice, that may include project organization, financial structuring, coordination, development activities and a broader investor role.

In other words, this is not only about putting pipes into the ground. It is about positioning a private company inside a project that has long-term consequences for the region’s energy security. That is why the way the deal is awarded is just as important as the project itself. If we are talking about strategic infrastructure, then transparency standards must be higher, not lower.

According to The Guardian, the pipeline itself is valued in a confidential AAFS proposal at around **300 million euros**, while an additional **900 million euros would relate to three power plants**. That means the “more than one billion” figure is not only about the pipeline, but about a broader package of energy infrastructure projects. The Guardian reports that the financing would come through equity and debt, meaning private capital and borrowing, rather than directly from the BiH state budget — but details about investment funds or expected returns have not been provided.

## Why a public tender matters even when the project is good

This is the key part of the whole story. It is possible that the idea of the Southern Interconnection makes sense. It is also possible that BiH really does need an alternative route for gas supply. But even if the goal is good, that does not mean the procedure can be non-transparent. Quite the opposite: the larger and more important the project, the greater the public’s right to know how the partner was selected.

The Guardian emphasizes that there was no public tender, and that detail gives the story weight. In a public tender, it should be clear who could apply, under which conditions, which references were required and why one bidder was the best choice. Without that, open questions remain. Was AAFS really the best candidate? Did anyone else even get a chance? Who assessed that this particular company deserved such an important position?

Because the project must connect to the Croatian gas network and the LNG terminal on Krk, Croatia is not just an observer. BiH and Croatia already signed an interstate agreement on the Southern Interconnection at the end of April 2026, but construction itself still depends on implementation contracts, permits, financing and the final model for managing the project.

## BiH and Croatia cooperation on building the Southern Interconnection pipeline

This is not only a “BiH project”. The pipeline would have to connect to the Croatian gas network, meaning the route toward the LNG terminal on Krk, so part of the infrastructure and the interstate agreement with Croatia are important elements of the story.

According to available sources, Croatia and BiH already signed an interstate agreement on April 28/29, 2026, on the Southern Gas Interconnection. So they are not merely waiting for a general “contract with Croatia” — that political and interstate step has already been taken. What remains are concrete implementation contracts, regulation, financing, permits, the route, operational models and possible agreements with AAFS.

## Energy, but also public trust

In the Balkans, large infrastructure projects are almost never only a technical issue. They regularly mix economics, politics, geostrategic interests and private business circles. That is why every pipeline story quickly becomes a story about influence, connections and control over future flows of money.

In this case, the project can be defended with the argument of energy security. BiH has been vulnerable for years when it comes to gas supply, and a connection with Croatia and the LNG terminal on Krk could open a new option. But The Guardian’s story shows that another layer appears at the same time: who profits, who gets control over the project and whether the public interest is sufficiently protected.

## Why this story matters beyond BiH

This topic is not important only for BiH or Croatia. It shows how large energy deals look today in a region that remains politically sensitive and energy-vulnerable. When strategic projects are awarded without a tender, it does not only trigger media attention, but also long-term doubts about institutions. People then stop discussing only gas and start discussing trust in the entire system.

That is why The Guardian’s story is important from a broader perspective as well. It does not claim that the project itself makes no sense, but it raises the question of procedure. And that is often exactly what decides whether the public sees a project as a development step or as another example of big deals being awarded behind closed doors.

## Can a good project be compromised by a bad procedure

Yes, and very easily. If citizens get the impression that a company was chosen because of political connections rather than quality and experience, even a strategically useful project loses legitimacy. That is why one simple thing matters in stories like this: transparency should not be an add-on, but the foundation.

If AAFS really is the best choice, then that should be possible to show through documents, criteria, references and a clear explanation of the process. If that is missing, room for doubt remains. And when a project is worth around one billion dollars, that room is not small.

For Metaadvisor, this story is a reminder that energy security and transparency must go together. Metaadvisor.eu follows these cases because they show how infrastructure, geopolitics and private business interests often overlap in ways that are not always visible at first glance. A pipeline can be strategically important, but the public still has the right to understand who builds it, who finances it and who benefits from it.

- The Guardian’s story raises a legitimate question about how the US company AAFS received such an important role without a public tender.
- The idea of a BiH–Croatia pipeline may make sense from an energy perspective, but that does not remove the need for a transparent procedure.
- The biggest problem is not necessarily the project itself, but the impression that the public does not have full insight into the criteria for choosing the partner.
- In a deal worth around one billion dollars, references, financial structure and the method of awarding the project should be fully clear.
- Stories like this remind us that trust in large infrastructure projects is not built only through strategic goals, but also through fair procedure.
