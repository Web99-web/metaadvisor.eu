# Sell in May and go away: does the old market saying still matter in 2026, in the age of AI markets?

“Sell in May and go away” is one of the best-known sayings in stock market history. In simple terms, it suggests that investors should sell their stocks in May, stay away from the market during the summer months and return in the autumn. The idea behind the phrase is straightforward: historically, the period from May to October has often been weaker for equities than the period from November to April.

But in 2026, this old market rule raises a much more interesting question. Does it still make sense in a world where markets are increasingly driven by artificial intelligence, large technology companies, semiconductors, data centers and expectations that AI could reshape almost every industry?

## An old market rule in a new investment environment

At first glance, “Sell in May” sounds like advice from an old trading handbook. During the summer, many investors are on holiday, trading volumes can be lower and markets sometimes become more nervous. In that kind of environment, even smaller news events can trigger larger price moves. That is why, for decades, some investors believed it might be wiser to step aside in May and return after the summer, when large market participants become more active again.

The problem with such sayings is that they sound simpler than markets really are. The stock market does not automatically follow a calendar. It is shaped by economic data, corporate profits, interest rates, inflation, investor psychology, global events and expectations. In 2026, one especially powerful factor has been added to that mix: artificial intelligence.

## The AI rally is changing the rules of the game

The 2026 market does not look like a quiet, slow and sleepy summer market. On the contrary, US stocks have shown strength even in a period when the old rule would suggest more caution. A large part of that optimism comes from the AI sector. Investors are no longer watching only traditional economic indicators, inflation and interest rate expectations. They are also asking how much money companies can make from artificial intelligence.

At the center of that story are chips, cloud infrastructure, software, data centers and everything that allows large AI models to expand. One earnings report from a major technology company can change the mood of the entire market. One sentence about demand for AI chips can push major indexes higher. One disappointment can immediately raise the question of whether some stock prices have already gone too far.

That is why “Sell in May and go away” in 2026 is not a simple instruction to sell. It is more of a reminder to think about risk. The old saying can remind investors that markets do not rise in a straight line and that strong optimism is often followed by corrections. But it cannot replace a real investment strategy.

## History matters, but it is not a guarantee

Historically, the saying has some basis. Many studies and long-term market observations show that the months from November to April have often delivered better average returns than the period from May to October. But an average is not a guarantee. The market does not know that it has to fall just because May has arrived. Some summers bring weaker trading and corrections, while others become the beginning of a new rally.

This is especially important for retail investors. If someone mechanically sells every May and waits until autumn, they may miss powerful market moves. The problem is not only selling. The bigger problem is knowing when to return. If the market keeps rising, it is psychologically difficult to buy back at higher prices. If the market falls, it is equally difficult to know whether the decline is over or only beginning.

On paper, “get out and come back later” sounds simple. In reality, it is market timing — the attempt to identify the right moment to enter and exit the market. That is one of the hardest parts of investing. Even professionals often get it wrong. Retail investors, who may react more emotionally, are even more vulnerable. The common problem is not only selling too early, but also losing the courage to return later.

## The AI market can be both an opportunity and a risk

In 2026, there is an additional complication: the AI rally. Many stocks connected to artificial intelligence have already risen strongly. That does not automatically mean they are overvalued, but it does mean expectations are high. When expectations are high, markets rarely forgive disappointment. A company can grow and still see its stock fall if that growth is not strong enough compared with what investors expected.

This often happens in sectors where a lot of future growth is already priced in. Artificial intelligence may be a real technological revolution, while the share price of some companies can still be too high at a certain moment. In other words, the AI market can be both a strong reason for further growth and a reason for caution.

On one hand, artificial intelligence is not just a short-term trend. Large companies are genuinely investing huge amounts of money into infrastructure. Demand for chips, servers, cloud capacity, data centers and energy solutions is real. AI is increasingly being built into business processes, search, programming, marketing, medicine, finance and customer support. This gives the market a real story, not just empty speculation.

On the other hand, history shows that even major technological revolutions can go through phases of excessive optimism. The internet really changed the world, but that did not prevent the dot-com bubble. Many ideas were correct, but stock prices were not always reasonable. The same can happen in the AI sector: the technology can be revolutionary, while the market valuation of certain companies can still become too high.

So the key question is not only: “Will AI change the world?” It probably will. The more important question for investors is: “How much of that future growth is already included in today’s prices?”

## What does this mean for retail investors?

In this context, the old saying “Sell in May and go away” can be useful as a reminder, but not as a strategy by itself. It reminds us that markets have cycles, that optimism does not last forever and that periods of strong growth often increase the risk of short-term corrections. But it cannot replace analysis, diversification and an understanding of personal risk.

For retail investors, the more useful question may not be whether they should sell everything in May. A better question is: am I too exposed to one single theme? If a portfolio is almost entirely tied to AI stocks, semiconductor companies or large technology giants, the seasonal saying may be a reason to check risk. If a portfolio is balanced, long-term and aligned with the investor’s personal situation, panic because of a calendar month can do more harm than good.

The same logic applies to crypto. Bitcoin, Ethereum and the broader crypto market often react to the same risk sentiment as technology stocks. When investors want risk, crypto often rises. When they fear a correction, they tend to move away from riskier assets. That means “Sell in May” is not only a stock market topic. In 2026, it can also affect crypto, especially if global risk appetite weakens.

But crypto also has its own dynamics: ETF flows, regulation, liquidity, halving cycles, institutional interest and global monetary policy. That is why there is no simple rule here either. The summer can bring weakness, but it can also bring a sudden rise if there is a positive regulatory signal, a new major institutional move or a shift in expectations around interest rates.

## The biggest risk is not May, but panic

The biggest risk for retail investors is not May. The biggest risk is making decisions out of fear and without a plan. When the market rises, it is easy to believe it will keep rising forever. When it falls, it is equally easy to believe everything is collapsing. Both emotions can become expensive.

That may be the best way to read the old market saying today: May can be a useful moment to pause, look at what you hold, check how much risk you are taking and understand why you are invested in the first place. That does not automatically mean selling. It also does not mean buying without thinking. It means that investors should understand their own time horizon, risk tolerance and strategy.

In 2026, “Sell in May and go away” should not be understood as a sell order. It should be understood as a reminder to be careful. The AI rally can last longer than skeptics expect, but it can also experience sharp corrections. Markets can continue rising while still remaining sensitive to bad news, high valuations, interest rates and geopolitical risks.

The old market saying may not be dead, but it is no longer enough on its own. In a world where artificial intelligence can change the expectations of the entire market within a single quarter, the calendar is only one part of the story. The more important question is whether an investor understands what they are buying, why they are holding it and whether they can tolerate volatility if the market moves against them.

For Metaadvisor.eu, this is exactly why such old market phrases are still useful — not because they give a perfect answer, but because they open the door to better questions. The market in 2026 is not only about dates, seasons and historical averages. It is also about technology, psychology, valuation and the ability to stay rational when the narrative becomes too euphoric or too fearful.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

- “Sell in May and go away” has a historical basis, but it is not an automatic rule that should be applied blindly every year.
- The AI rally in 2026 changes the market context because technology and semiconductor stocks can support major indexes even during periods that were historically weaker.
- The biggest risk for retail investors is not the month of May, but emotional decision-making without a strategy.
- Seasonality can be useful as a reminder to be cautious, but it should not replace analysis, diversification and risk awareness.
- In the age of AI markets, the key question is not only whether the technology will grow, but how much of that growth is already priced in.
