# Coinbase sued over frozen crypto funds after $55M DeFi Saver phishing hack

Coinbase has found itself at the center of a new legal and cybersecurity controversy that once again raises one of the most sensitive questions in crypto:

👉 Who really owns “frozen” funds after a hack?

The story began with a 2024 phishing attack that allegedly stole around $55 million in DAI stablecoins. According to the lawsuit, the attacker used a fake DeFi Saver login page to trick the victim into granting access to crypto wallets.

After the theft, part of the funds allegedly moved through Tornado Cash — a well-known crypto mixer designed to obscure blockchain transaction trails — before eventually ending up in a Coinbase retail account.

That is where the case becomes particularly interesting.

Coinbase froze the assets, but refused to return them without a court order. According to the complaint, the exchange acknowledged it was holding “traceable stolen funds,” yet argued that it could not independently determine legal ownership.

The plaintiff is now asking a California federal court to officially recognize him as the rightful owner and order Coinbase to release the frozen crypto.

## Who is the victim?

The victim’s identity remains undisclosed.

Several reports state that the plaintiff is identified only as:

👉 “D.B.”
👉 a crypto whale from Puerto Rico

According to available information, parts of the court filings were redacted, and the lawsuit was filed under seal in federal court in San Francisco.

So far, no major source has revealed the plaintiff’s full identity.

That anonymity has only increased the mystery surrounding the case.

## Fake .app domain and Inferno Drainer

As more details emerge, the case appears even more sophisticated.

According to reports:

* a fake DeFi Saver login page was used
* the phishing domain allegedly used `.app` instead of the legitimate `.com`
* the attackers reportedly used the wallet-draining tool Inferno Drainer
* stolen funds were routed through Tornado Cash

The investigation was reportedly carried out with the help of blockchain intelligence firms:

* Zero Shadow
* Five Stones Intelligence

However, it is important to stress:

👉 there is no confirmation that he was the main hacker

At this stage, his name is only connected to alleged laundering activity involving the movement and concealment of stolen funds.

## Coinbase caught between regulation and decentralization

This lawsuit could have broader implications for the entire crypto industry.

Crypto exchanges have spent years balancing between:

* the decentralized philosophy of “not your keys, not your coins”
* growing legal and regulatory pressure to respond to theft and money laundering

The problem is that freezing funds creates a new dilemma:

👉 should centralized exchanges act as judges of ownership?

Coinbase appears determined to avoid exactly that responsibility.

If the exchange released the assets without a court process, it could potentially face legal consequences if ownership was later disputed.

Critics, however, argue that this approach makes recovery far harder for victims of crypto theft.

## Tornado Cash back under scrutiny

The case also places Tornado Cash and similar privacy tools back into the spotlight.

Privacy advocates argue such tools are legitimate technologies designed to protect financial anonymity, while regulators increasingly associate them with money laundering, ransomware operations and cybercrime.

One particularly important aspect of this case is the claim that investigators were still able to trace the funds even after they passed through Tornado Cash.

That could become a major legal precedent for future crypto investigations.

## What is Tornado Cash?

Tornado Cash is a privacy tool built on Ethereum that “mixes” crypto transactions in order to hide the connection between sender and receiver.

In practice, users deposit funds into a shared pool and later withdraw them to a different wallet, making it much harder to directly connect deposits and withdrawals on the blockchain.

Because of this, regulators and security agencies have repeatedly claimed that Tornado Cash has been used to launder funds after major hacks.

One of the most controversial cases involves Roman Storm, one of Tornado Cash’s co-founders, whom US authorities accuse of helping facilitate the laundering of more than $1 billion tied to cybercrime and the North Korean Lazarus Group.

## What does this mean for ordinary users?

For everyday crypto users, the case sends an important message:

👉 even when stolen funds are located and frozen, recovery can still take years and depend on courts.

That is one of the biggest differences between traditional banking and crypto, where ownership, private keys and decentralization often enter legal gray zones.

If the court rules that ownership and transaction trails can still be proven after Tornado Cash, exchanges may dramatically change how they handle future hacks.

At the same time, exchanges could become more aggressive in freezing suspicious transactions to protect themselves from liability.

In other words:

👉 this is not just a story about one hack — it may be a signal of where the entire crypto industry is heading.
