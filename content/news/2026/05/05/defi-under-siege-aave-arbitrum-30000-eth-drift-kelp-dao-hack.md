# DeFi Under Siege: Aave, Arbitrum and the Battle for 30,000 ETH After a Mega Hack

The crypto market is once again under pressure — but this time, it’s not just another hack. It’s a collision between technology, capital, and the legal system that could redefine how decentralized finance operates.

After the nearly $300 million exploit of Kelp DAO, the entire DeFi ecosystem has entered a chain reaction: stolen funds, frozen assets, coordinated recovery efforts — and now a legal battle that could set a precedent for the future of crypto.

Metaadvisor.eu continues to follow this case closely, as its implications go far beyond a single protocol.

{{< support1 >}}

## How the chain of events began

It all started in mid-April when Kelp DAO — a liquid restaking protocol — was exploited through cross-chain infrastructure linked to LayerZero. The total damage is estimated at around $293 million.

The attackers didn’t just steal funds — they used them as collateral to borrow additional crypto assets across DeFi platforms, including Aave. This created so-called “bad debt” — liabilities that spread across multiple protocols and threaten systemic stability.

In the early stages, there were suspicions that the attack could be linked to North Korea. However, Aave later made it clear that such a connection is **not proven**, and remains based on assumptions drawn from patterns seen in previous incidents.

## Arbitrum freezes the funds

To prevent further escalation, Arbitrum — one of the leading Ethereum layer-2 networks — took an extraordinary and highly controversial step.

Its security council froze 30,766 ETH (worth over $70 million) linked to the exploit. The assets were moved into a special “frozen wallet” and are no longer accessible to the attackers.

The decision was made in coordination with security experts and relevant institutions, but it immediately divided the community.

For some, it was a necessary move to protect users.
For others, it raised a fundamental concern: if a small group can freeze funds, how decentralized is DeFi really?

{{< support2 >}}

## Aave proposes a solution: DeFi United

Shortly after the freeze, Aave Labs introduced a proposal aimed at stabilizing the situation — one that could become a blueprint for handling future crises.

The idea is straightforward: unfreeze the funds and redirect them into the “DeFi United” fund — a collaborative initiative designed to compensate affected users, restore the backing of the rsETH token, and stabilize the broader ecosystem.

So far, over $21 million has already been contributed, while an additional $200+ million could be allocated through governance proposals across multiple protocols.

The initiative has received broad support from major DeFi players, including LayerZero, Lido, Ether.fi, Compound, and others.

Such coordination is rare in decentralized environments — and highlights the seriousness of the situation.

Metaadvisor.eu notes that this could mark a shift toward more coordinated crisis management in DeFi.

## Legal battle: courts enter DeFi

Then comes the unexpected twist.

A US law firm has initiated legal action to block the transfer of funds, claiming that its clients are entitled to the Ether based on prior judgments against North Korea.

In other words, even if the funds were stolen, the question becomes: who has the legal right to them?

The firm argues that its clients have a valid claim. Aave, however, strongly contests this and has filed an emergency motion, stating that theft cannot create lawful ownership. It also warns that such a legal precedent could severely undermine future recovery efforts in similar cases.

Aave’s position is clear:
👉 theft does not establish legal ownership
👉 and there is no доказ that North Korea was behind the attack

This shifts the case from a technical issue to a broader legal conflict with far-reaching implications.

If the court upholds the restriction, future recovery efforts could become significantly more complex — as every case risks being tied up in legal disputes.

## The bigger picture: the end of “pure” decentralization?

This situation raises one of the most important questions in crypto today:

Is DeFi truly decentralized?

In theory, blockchain systems are immutable.
In practice, we now see:

* asset freezes
* coordinated interventions
* legal involvement

These mechanisms can enhance security — but they also reduce autonomy.

And that’s the core paradox of modern DeFi.

## Our take (Metaadvisor):

* DeFi is entering a direct confrontation with the legal system — with long-term consequences
* The ability to freeze assets reveals clear limits to decentralization
* “DeFi United” shows that industry coordination is possible — and necessary
* The unproven North Korea link weakens the legal narrative
* Legal risk is emerging as a major new threat in crypto — potentially bigger than hacks themselves
* The key question remains: do we want full decentralization — or controlled security?
