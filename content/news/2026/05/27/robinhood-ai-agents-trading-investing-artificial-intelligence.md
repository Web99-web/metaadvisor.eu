# Robinhood introduces AI agents for trading: Could artificial intelligence soon invest on behalf of users?

Robinhood is once again trying to move the line between an ordinary investing app and an automated financial platform. After years of being known mainly as an app that gave small investors simple access to stocks, options and crypto, the company is now entering a new phase: users will be able to connect AI agents to their accounts so that those agents can monitor markets, execute strategies and potentially place trades on their behalf.

At first glance, this sounds like a logical continuation of everything happening in financial technology. Artificial intelligence already writes texts, analyzes documents, helps programmers, answers customer questions and searches large amounts of data. But once AI starts getting access to real money, portfolios, cards and trading functions, the issue becomes much more serious.

## An AI agent is no longer just an adviser

Robinhood has announced two products: **Agentic Trading** and **Agentic Credit Card**. The idea is that users no longer need to constantly monitor the market, news, charts and prices, but can give an AI agent certain rules or a strategy that the agent then carries out.

Here it is important not to exaggerate expectations. This is not the same as a magic command: “Buy me the best stocks when they are cheap.” More realistically, the user sets concrete conditions, limits and rules, while the AI agent monitors the market and carries out actions within that framework. In other words, the novelty is not simply that a system waits for one specific price. That already exists through limit orders. The novelty is that an AI agent can monitor a broader set of conditions, analyze more data and carry out a defined strategy without constant manual supervision.

For example, a user may want exposure to a specific sector, such as stocks connected to artificial intelligence. Instead of checking several companies, prices, news and the state of the portfolio every day, the user could give the agent rules: which stocks it is allowed to monitor, how much money it may use, when it may buy, when it must stop and how much risk the user is willing to accept at all.

That is a major difference compared with classic investing tools. Until now, apps have mostly displayed data, offered charts, news, alerts and a simple interface for buying or selling. Here the model goes one step further: AI is no longer just a tool that explains the market, but potentially an operator that actually does something.

## Virtual cards and automated purchases

The second part of the story concerns the **Agentic Credit Card**, meaning virtual credit cards connected to AI agents. Robinhood wants to allow users to give agents conditions for purchasing products. For example, an agent could monitor prices, compare offers and complete a purchase when the defined conditions are met.

Here too, simple automation should be separated from true “agentic” logic. If a user simply says: “Buy this product when the price falls to 100 dollars,” that is not a particularly new idea. In fact, it is very similar to the logic of a limit order in the market. But if the agent monitors multiple sellers, compares prices, waits for a discount, checks the budget and buys only within the defined rules, then we are moving closer to a new phase of digital financial assistants.

## Why is Robinhood pushing AI now?

This news comes at a very interesting moment. Robinhood recently had a weaker first quarter, especially in the crypto segment. Revenue connected to crypto trading fell sharply, and the company is trying to reduce its dependence on crypto market cycles and find new sources of growth outside classic trading.

In addition, it was recently reported that Tanya Denisova, the chief operating officer of Robinhood Crypto, is leaving the company. The official reason for her departure has not been publicly explained, but the timing further suggests that a broader strategic shift is probably taking place inside Robinhood.

That is why this AI story should not be viewed in isolation. Robinhood is clearly trying to position itself as a broader fintech platform, not just an app for buying stocks and crypto. Within the same ecosystem, investing, crypto, cards, automation, prediction markets and AI agents are now increasingly coming together.

In other words, Robinhood is trying to become a place where users not only trade, but hand over an increasingly large part of their financial decisions to an automated system. That can be practical, but also dangerous if users do not understand the limits of such a system.

## Can a small user make money with an AI agent?

For small users, this will probably sound very attractive. If someone invests 100 dollars and lets an AI agent buy and sell for 30 days, the natural question is whether that can generate a profit. In theory, yes. In practice, it is uncertain, and no one should expect miracles.

With small amounts, the problem is not only the direction of the market, but also the size of the move. If a user earns 3% on 100 dollars, that is 3 dollars. If the user earns 10%, that is 10 dollars, which would already be a very good result for one month. But the same mechanism can also work in the opposite direction. A 10% loss means minus 10 dollars, and in sudden market moves the loss can be even larger.

That is why such tools may make more sense for beginners as a controlled learning experiment than as a promise of quick profit. With a small amount, the user can observe how the AI agent reacts, whether it follows rules, avoids panic decisions and how often it trades. But if the user expects guaranteed profit in 30 days, the problem is not only the technology, but the expectation.

AI can help with discipline, data monitoring and strategy execution. But AI does not know the future of the market. Markets move under the influence of news, panic, liquidity, interest rates, geopolitical events, corporate results and the behavior of other investors. No agent can remove risk.

## Automation is no longer reserved for Wall Street

Until now, serious automated trading systems have mostly been reserved for hedge funds, large banks and institutional investors. Such systems use quantitative models, large risk-management teams, huge databases and strict internal controls.

Robinhood is now trying to bring part of that logic into an app for ordinary users. That is technologically interesting, but also socially important. If millions of people get the ability to let AI agents trade automatically for them, it could change the way the retail market functions.

The question is no longer only whether AI will replace financial advisers. The question becomes whether ordinary users will soon allow AI agents to handle their money every day.

## A big opportunity, but also a big risk

Such a tool could be useful for more experienced users who know exactly what they want. If the user defines clear rules, limits, risk and strategy, an AI agent can serve as an assistant that saves time. Instead of the user watching charts all day, the agent can monitor conditions and react when they are met.

The problem appears with less experienced investors. Robinhood has always been especially popular among retail users, meaning ordinary small investors. If such users start handing decisions over to AI agents without understanding the risks, they could develop a false sense of security. AI can sound smart, but that does not mean it knows what will happen.

Regulation will play a major role here. If an AI agent makes a bad decision, who is responsible? The user who gave permission? Robinhood, which enabled the system? The third party that developed the agent? Or no one, because the user accepted the risk? These questions will not disappear just because the technology is practical.

## Robinhood is testing a new era of finance

Robinhood’s move shows where fintech is heading. Apps no longer want to be just tools. They want to become active systems that suggest, decide and execute. That can bring speed, convenience and new possibilities, but at the same time it demands much greater responsibility.

For Robinhood, this is also a business attempt to find new growth after a weaker crypto quarter. For users, it is an opportunity, but also a warning. AI can help, but it should not become a substitute for understanding risk.

The most important question will not be whether AI can trade. Technically, it can. The real question is how much control a user should hand over to a system that may sound convincing, but can still make mistakes.
