# AI influencers that don’t exist: how fake profiles like Emily Hart and Jessica Foster use emotions to earn thousands

We’ve just seen another fresh example of how a fake AI profile can quickly gather thousands of followers and start making money — until it eventually gets exposed.

At first glance, it looks like just another influencer: an attractive blonde, presenting herself as both a nurse and a MAGA supporter, posting lifestyle content with strong opinions and consistent engagement. But in reality, behind the profile was a completely different person — and in this case, a man.

## The Emily Hart case: a profile that never existed

![Emily Hart AI profile](/images/hack/Emily-Hart-MAGA.jpg)

This was an AI-generated identity named Emily Hart — a profile run by a 22-year-old medical student from India. What makes this story particularly interesting is not just that the profile wasn’t real, but how it was created and how it managed to attract an audience.

According to available reports and statements he gave to outlets like WIRED, the student didn’t start with this model right away. He tried multiple ways to make money: selling study notes to other students, creating YouTube Shorts, and even experimenting with generic influencer-style accounts using images of attractive women.

None of it worked.

Only after introducing AI into the process did things start to change.

Using tools like Google Gemini, he tried to optimize his approach. At one point, he said the AI told him that a “hot girl” profile was too generic because millions of others were doing the same thing. In other words, the competition was too high.

## AI + emotions = audience

That’s when the key shift happened. The AI suggested creating a more specific profile targeting a defined audience — particularly middle-aged, often lonely men who are conservative and have higher disposable income — and to focus on the MAGA niche.

Instead of generic content, the profile gained a clear identity. Emily Hart became a 20-year-old nurse from New York, with defined opinions and a consistent narrative.

The content began focusing on topics that trigger strong emotional responses: patriotism, belonging, political views, and provocative statements. This element — emotion — turned out to be the key.

Because people don’t react to facts. They react to feelings.

“Emily” would often post or ask questions like: “Unpopular question: People who don’t respect America should be kicked out of our great country. — Drop a USA flag if you agree.” These kinds of posts were not random.

They were designed to generate reactions and engagement.

And it worked. No one questioned whether the profile was fake — let alone that it was being run by a 22-year-old student from India.

## Making money without a real person

The profile quickly gained tens of thousands of followers and generated millions of views. But more importantly — it started making money.

The income didn’t come from traditional ads, but from direct interaction with followers. According to reports, monetization happened through platforms like Fansly, where “Emily” would chat with users and send AI-generated images.

In other words, people were paying for content from someone who doesn’t exist.

The creator himself said he had never seen an easier way to make money online. At one point, he even claimed that people “fall for it easily,” although it’s important to note that this reflects his personal opinion, not an objective assessment.

Another interesting detail is that this strategy wasn’t accidental. According to his own statements, the AI suggested that certain audiences — specifically conservative segments of the U.S. population — have higher purchasing power and respond more emotionally to content.

## Not an isolated case

However, this is not really a political story. Even though there are clear similarities with previous MAGA-related profiles.

Earlier, a similar viral account appeared claiming to be an American soldier — often referred to as “Jessica Foster.” That profile used the same formula: attractive visuals, patriotism, and emotionally charged messaging to quickly build an audience.

It reportedly gained over one million followers within a few months, used fake images with public figures and events, and acted as a funnel to paid platforms such as Fanvue or even OnlyFans, where monetization was based on subscriptions, exclusive content, and direct interaction with users.

While not all details of that case are as clearly confirmed as the Emily Hart story, the pattern is almost identical. As far as is known, the real identity behind Jessica Foster has not been publicly revealed.

The key is emotion.

## What this means for the internet

Which brings us to the core question: if someone who has never lived in the United States can convince thousands of Americans to share “her” values — what does that say about the internet today?

We are entering a phase where identity is no longer essential. AI can generate faces, personalities, and opinions. The audience reacts to emotion, not reality.

It’s similar to how people tend to trust someone in a suit more than someone poorly dressed — even if the person in the suit has no real knowledge or credibility.

In other words — we don’t follow people.

We follow the feeling they give us.

And that is exactly why these profiles work — and why we will see many more of them in the future.
