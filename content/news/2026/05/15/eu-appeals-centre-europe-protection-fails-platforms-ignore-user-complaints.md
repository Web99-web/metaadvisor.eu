# EU protection fails: social platforms ignore user complaints

Related to the topic we have been covering — AI moderation, or more precisely AI ERASING PEOPLE, and mass silent automated bans or removal of thousands of normal users from social media — which we have followed through several articles: https://metaadvisor.eu/news/social-media-bans-eu-ai-moderation-accounts/ — in that article we highlighted, as a positive point, that in Europe users supposedly have stronger protection and rights, because the EU takes care of its citizens. However, we are now receiving opposite information suggesting that this protection often exists only “on paper”, while in reality nothing changes and platforms still do not restore accounts, ignore emails, and it appears they have outplayed everyone — including the EU. So what is actually happening?

## What is really happening here — why people think only spammers are removed, while normal users are affected too

What we are talking about is not an isolated issue, but a growing global phenomenon that more and more people are reporting — the mass removal of users from major platforms, especially Facebook and Instagram. https://metaadvisor.eu/news/facebook-instagram-accounts-mass-disabled-us-canada-philippines/

Why is this not being talked about more loudly? **Because users are removed suddenly and instantly — and they cannot collectively share what is happening to them. AI ERASES PEOPLE, their digital voice and their ability to communicate and share information.**

![Meta wrongfully disabling accounts](/images/Meta/Meta-wrongfully-disabling-accounts-Facebook-Instagram.png)

This issue is increasingly being highlighted through initiatives such as online petitions, where users report that they were wrongly removed due to AI moderation, further confirming that this is not a set of isolated cases, but a growing pattern.

Most people do not use dozens of platforms, but are present on a few major ones — where everyone is, where contacts, news, communication, and business all exist. In today’s world, especially after Covid, large social platforms have become a kind of “utility”, a core digital infrastructure (similar to how internet access is now considered standard). And that is exactly why the impact is so severe when users are removed overnight. The profile receives a short message: you violated our terms of service. No clear explanation. No context. No contact email. People start questioning themselves — what did I do, where did I go wrong — even when they did nothing wrong. In many cases, this is AI moderation. Automated systems delete profiles, business pages, contacts, photos, and years of communication — without real explanation, without effective appeal, and without human support.

Other people, who are not currently affected, still believe this only happens to scammers or “bad actors”. That is simply not true. This issue of AI moderation and user removal is increasingly affecting ordinary users and small businesses. **The most problematic part is how it happens. Systems remove people silently. One moment everything works, the next the account is gone — and no one around even notices. Friends assume the person simply stopped using social media.**

AI can be an extremely powerful tool. But systems with this level of control over people’s digital lives should not operate without real and accountable human oversight. Someone built them. Someone approved mass AI moderation removing people. Someone is responsible.

## European Union **Digital Services Act** and what happens when platforms simply do not respond

The European Union, through the **Digital Services Act**, is trying to establish a system that provides real protection to users against large digital platforms. The idea is clear: if a platform makes a decision that affects a user, that user has the right to appeal and access an independent body that should assess whether the decision was correct. On paper, this is a serious attempt to create balance.

In practice, however, the system breaks down the moment a platform decides not to participate.

{{< support1 >}}

**When a user loses access to their account, they also lose the ability to directly contact the platform**, because they can no longer log in. The appeal process formally exists, but only through the platform itself, since there are no publicly available contact emails, and even previously available ones are ignored — meaning everything often ends with no response. Total silence from platforms. Platforms do not communicate, do not provide additional information, and do not engage in dialogue. The entire process becomes waiting without an outcome, while at the same time the user has limited time — **often 180 days** — before permanent deletion of the account.

## Appeals Centre Europe: who they are and why their decisions have no real effect

![Appeals Centre Europe 1](/images/Meta/EU-Appeals-Centre-Europe-1.png)

In such situations, **Appeals Centre Europe** is supposed to act as an independent mechanism for resolving disputes between users and platforms. When a user is blocked and loses access, they often turn to this path to recover their personal account, business page, or group. If they are in the EU, it is logical to expect some level of protection — at least on paper, everything looks like a solution. The presentation of the Appeals Centre itself is convincing: the website is full of positive messaging and visuals suggesting user support. But the key question is — does it actually work?

![Appeals Centre Europe 2](/images/Meta/EU-Appeals-Centre-Europe-2.png)

In practice, this is exactly where the system loses its strength. **It appears that social platforms can formally ignore the EU Appeals Centre Europe. How is that possible?** The answer is simple: Appeals Centre Europe is an independent body without enforcement powers. It cannot penalize platforms or force them to cooperate. Additionally, available information suggests that its work is funded by platforms themselves, and it is also located in Dublin — the same city where major tech companies, including Meta, have their European headquarters. This fact alone does not necessarily mean a problem, but in a system that relies on platform goodwill, it raises important questions.

![Appeals Centre Europe 4](/images/Meta/EU-Appeals-Centre-Europe-4.png)

After a user initiates a case through Appeals Centre Europe, another waiting period follows — often weeks, sometimes without any clear response. In some cases, it is particularly concerning that platforms appear not to have participated in the process at all. If a decision is made with a note that insufficient information was provided by the other side, it raises a key question: did communication even take place, or was the entire process conducted without any real response from the platform?

Meanwhile, time works against the user. Many platforms have deadlines after which data is permanently deleted, often around 180 days. If no response or implementation happens within that time, the loss becomes irreversible.

**The core problem appears at the end of the process. Even when Appeals Centre Europe rules in favor of the user, that decision is not legally binding.** In other words, the platform can simply ignore it — without consequences.

![Appeals Centre Europe odluka](/images/Meta/Appeals-centre-Europe-dispute.png)

This leads to an absurd situation: there is a process, there is a decision, the user is told they are right — but there is no mechanism to enforce it.
**The platform can ignore the user, ignore the process, and ignore the decision — without consequences.**

If the system is based on the assumption that platforms will cooperate, but that cooperation is neither mandatory nor enforced, then the balance of power does not change. Platforms still decide whether they will participate, and the user remains without real protection, even when formally proven right.

## What can the user actually do?

At this point, a fundamental question arises: what is a user supposed to do with a decision in their favor? If they have no access to their account and the platform does not respond — where should that decision even go? To a platform that ignores them? One they cannot even reach because they are blocked? **Or should they symbolically “nail it” to the doors of EU institutions?**

Without communication channels and enforcement mechanisms, the decision remains just a document with no real value — **an empty piece of paper.**

In today’s digital world, this is no longer a small issue. Platforms have become infrastructure for communication, business, and access to information. Losing an account means losing contacts, content, and digital identity, which for many users has real social and economic consequences.

## AI moderation erases everything: users without accounts and without explanation

User experiences with Appeals Centre Europe are mixed. There are reports that some users managed to recover their accounts, but most of those cases date back to 2025. This is significant, because from mid-2025 onward, a stronger wave of AI moderation and mass user removals begins, with more and more people reporting the same issue — being removed from platforms without explanation or warning.

![Appeals Centre Europe 3](/images/Meta/EU-Appeals-Centre-Europe-3.png)

It is important to understand the full scope of the problem. When such systems block someone, they often do not remove just one account. If **Facebook**, **Instagram**, **Messenger**, and **Business Suite** are connected to the same email or profile, a user can **lose access to everything instantly — and often permanently**. Contacts, messages, years of communication, photos, business pages, and work history disappear. In many cases, there is no real human support to explain what happened or help restore access.

## Time works against the user

While the process is ongoing, the user is effectively in a race against time. After a certain period — often 180 days — the account is permanently deleted.

This means that even a decision in favor of the user can come too late or be completely ignored.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

The problem is not the lack of rules, but the lack of enforcement. A system that can determine a user is right but cannot ensure the implementation of that decision does not function as protection.

If there are no consequences for ignoring decisions and platforms can simply not respond, then digital protection remains on paper — while real power stays with the platforms.
