# “What you learned in your first year may already be outdated”: students booed pro-AI speakers

A story from a US university graduation ceremony shows how sensitive artificial intelligence has become for young people who are only just entering the labor market. While business and technology leaders often present AI as a tool that must be accepted and used, many students increasingly see it as a threat to jobs, career prospects and the value of the degree they have just earned.

According to a report by The Guardian, Scott Borchetta, CEO of the record label Big Machine, spoke at the graduation ceremony of Middle Tennessee State University. He told students that industries are changing faster than ever before and that artificial intelligence is already changing the way work is done in music production, but also in many other sectors.

{{< support1 >}}

## The sentence that triggered the boos

As the students’ disapproval grew louder, Borchetta continued: “You can hear me now or you can pay me later … then do something about it. It’s a tool. Make it work for you.” He then added: “The things you learned in your first year here may already be obsolete.”

That sentence hit a nerve. For some graduates, it did not sound like a motivational speech, but like a cold reminder that they are entering a world in which the rules are changing faster than the education system can keep up. They had spent years investing time, money and energy into a degree, only to hear at the very end that part of that knowledge may already have lost value.

## Knowledge is no longer static

This story is not only about one speech and a few boos. It points to a much broader problem of our time: knowledge is changing incredibly fast. What was enough for a secure career ten or twenty years ago can now change within months. New tools, new software, automation and AI are changing the way work is done across almost every industry.

That does not mean a university degree is worthless. But it does mean that a degree is no longer the final point. It is more like a starting point. In a world where technology changes from month to month, the ability to keep learning, adapt and understand new tools becomes one of the most important skills. This is also why Metaadvisor.eu often treats AI not only as a technology story, but as a wider social and economic shift.

## Students have a right to be frustrated

At the same time, young people have every right to feel frustrated. It is not the same when someone who already has a career, capital and a position of power says that AI is “just a tool”, and when students hear that while they are still looking for their first job. For them, AI is not an abstract technology. It may become a real barrier between a degree and employment.

That is why tone matters. Telling young people to simply “deal with it” may sound realistic, but it can also sound deeply insensitive. If a new generation is expected to accept AI, then it also needs better education, better tools and more realistic support for entering a labor market that is clearly changing faster than before.

## AI will not disappear, but the fear is not irrational

The reality is probably somewhere in between. AI will not disappear, and ignoring it makes little sense. Those who learn how to use it well will have an advantage. But it is also not fair to tell young people that everything is only a matter of “adapting”, while AI is also being used as a justification for cutting costs and reducing headcount.

The most important message of this story may be that education must change together with technology. If knowledge from the first year of university can already be described as outdated by graduation, then the problem is not only with students. It is also with a system that still prepares them for a world that no longer exists in the same form. For Metaadvisor, this is exactly why AI should be discussed in plain language: not as hype, but as something that is already reshaping everyday careers.

## Our take (Metaadvisor)

- AI is no longer only a technology topic, but also a generational issue.
- Students are not necessarily rejecting the technology itself, but the way they are often told to “just adapt”.
- A degree still has value, but it is no longer enough without continuous learning.
- Employers and educational institutions will need to explain much more clearly how young people can enter an AI-shaped labor market.
- The biggest challenge will not only be using AI tools, but reducing the feeling that an entire generation has been left to face this shift alone.
