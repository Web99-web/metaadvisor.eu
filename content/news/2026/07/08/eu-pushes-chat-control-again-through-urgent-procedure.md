# EU pushes Chat Control again through urgent procedure

The European Parliament is again voting on the controversial rules critics call **Chat Control**. Formally, this is about extending a temporary legal framework that allows online platforms to scan messages in order to detect child sexual abuse material. Politically, however, it is the continuation of one of Europe’s most sensitive digital debates: can private communication be scanned in the name of safety, and what then happens to encryption, privacy and the risk of abuse?

What is especially controversial this time is the way the proposal is returning. On Tuesday, the European Parliament voted to use a **rarely applied urgent procedure so that the extension can be voted on already on Thursday**. The procedural vote was close: **331 MEPs voted in favor**, **304 against**, and **11 abstained**. According to Pirate Party MEP Markéta Gregorová, rejecting or amending the proposal would now require an absolute majority of **361 votes**.

This is not just a technical detail. In March, Parliament had already rejected a temporary extension of this regime, and the legal framework then expired in early April. Now the issue is returning through an accelerated procedure, just before the summer break, which critics see as an attempt to push a very sensitive legal framework forward again without a sufficiently broad debate.

## What is actually being voted on now?

It is important to distinguish between two things. This vote concerns the so-called **Chat Control 1.0**, meaning the extension of a temporary exemption from ePrivacy rules. That exemption allows platforms to voluntarily scan communications to detect CSAM, or child sexual abuse material.

This is not the same as the broader and even more controversial **Chat Control 2.0**, which could introduce stronger and more mandatory scanning mechanisms. But for opponents of the measure, even this extension is not harmless. Their argument is that each new extension keeps alive and normalizes a model in which private communication can be automatically analyzed.

Supporters of the proposal say platforms need a legal framework so they can detect and report abusive content, grooming and sextortion. After the regime expired in April, major technology companies warned that the legal gap had made their child protection measures more difficult. Critics, however, respond that the problem lies in the logic of scanning itself: if private messages are automatically checked, the line between safety and mass surveillance becomes very thin.

## Chat Control has a long history of controversy

Chat Control is not appearing out of nowhere. This issue has been debated for months in European politics, the technology industry and among digital rights organizations. MetaAdvisor has previously written about two important aspects of this debate: the possible precedent for mass scanning of private messages and the possible shift of users toward decentralized communication alternatives.

In November 2025, we wrote about the politically most sensitive aspect of Chat Control: the possibility of creating a **system in which citizens are monitored while political elites may be exempt or better protected from the same surveillance**. Such a model is not only a technical issue, but a democratic problem. If citizens’ private messages can be scanned without individual suspicion and without a court order, then the question becomes who controls the system, who is exempt from it and who can decide how it will be used in the future.

## Child protection or a precedent for surveillance?

The official goal of the proposal is to protect children from sexual abuse online. This is a serious and real problem. No reasonable person disputes that platforms, police and regulators need effective tools to fight child abuse, grooming and the spread of illegal material.

But that is exactly why the method must be questioned clearly. **In democratic systems, legal mechanisms for surveillance, investigation and access to data already exist when there is suspicion of a criminal offence. Such procedures usually involve an investigation, competent authorities, a legal framework and a court order. When serious crime is involved, the state already has ways to act in a targeted manner against suspects.** Chat Control raises a different question: why should this model move from targeted surveillance of people under suspicion toward preventive scanning of the private communication of a large number of citizens? That is the core of the dispute. Critics are not saying that child abuse should not be investigated. On the contrary, this crime must be investigated seriously and effectively. But they argue that the fight against the worst forms of digital crime must not become an infrastructure for automatically scanning the private messages, images, videos and files of all users.

Once a system is introduced that can technically inspect private communication, it is difficult to guarantee that its use will remain limited forever to a single purpose. Today, the justification is child protection. Tomorrow, someone could demand the scanning of another type of content. The day after, the issue could become political extremism, disinformation, hate speech or other categories that are often broadly defined and politically sensitive.

That is why Chat Control cannot be judged only by its intention. The intention may be justified, but the tool can still be dangerous. The key question is not whether children should be protected, but whether this should normalize a system that treats the private communication of all citizens as a space for constant automatic checking.

## Encryption is the central problem

The biggest technical problem is **end-to-end encryption**. In such communication, only the sender and the recipient should be able to read the message. The platform, the state or any third party should not have access to the content of the message.

If platforms are expected to scan the content of messages, a simple question arises: how can this be done without weakening encryption?

One proposal often mentioned in such debates is scanning content before the message is encrypted, meaning on the user’s own device. But for many experts, this is especially dangerous because it turns a personal device into a tool for automatic content checking. In other words, surveillance does not happen after the message is intercepted, but before the message even leaves your phone.

For the user, the difference may sound technical. But the consequence is significant. If an app or operating system can analyze private content before it is sent, then end-to-end encryption no longer protects what the user thinks it protects. The message may be encrypted in transit, but it has already been checked on the device.

This is why Chat Control so often brings child-safety arguments into conflict with the arguments of cryptographers. One side says abusive content must be found. The other side warns that the security model protecting all users must not be broken in the process, including journalists, whistleblowers, activists, lawyers, doctors, entrepreneurs and ordinary citizens.

## Why is the urgent procedure problematic?

An urgent procedure may make sense when deciding on a short-term and technically clear issue. But Chat Control is not such an issue. This debate concerns private messages, encryption, child protection, citizens’ rights, the role of platforms and the limits of state surveillance.

When such a topic returns after a previous rejection, and does so through an accelerated procedure, mistrust naturally grows. Especially because the vote on the procedure itself was close. When 304 MEPs vote against the urgent procedure, it shows that a significant part of Parliament considers this way of deciding inappropriate.

The political timing is also interesting. According to reports, the latest attempt to revive the proposal is linked to the European People’s Party, the largest group in Parliament, even though part of that group had previously opposed the extension. This shows that the political balance around Chat Control is still shifting, and the outcome is not just a technical question of digital regulation, but also a test of the relationship between safety, privacy and citizens’ trust in EU institutions.

## What does this mean for ordinary users?

For ordinary users, the most important question is: will private messages remain private? Today, people send almost everything through messaging apps: family conversations, photos of children, business information, financial data, health details, political views and private thoughts. If a model of automatic scanning is introduced or normalized, users will no longer be able to be sure that their communication is analyzed only when there is suspicion and a court order.

This does not mean that every message will be read by a human being. But it does mean that systems could automatically analyze content in search of prohibited or suspicious material. And once such a model is accepted, the biggest question becomes: who decides what is being searched for, who controls the algorithms, who has access to the results and can the system be abused? A particular problem is false positives. A private family photo, for example an image of a baby sent by one parent to the other, can be completely innocent and personal. But an automated system does not always understand context, intent and the relationship between people. If an algorithm wrongly flags such content as suspicious, the private communication of an ordinary family can end up in a review process.

This risk is not only theoretical. Large platforms have already had problems with automated moderation and the incorrect flagging of content or user accounts. Meta, for example, has faced numerous complaints from Facebook and Instagram users over mistaken suspensions, including cases in which accounts were flagged for alleged violations of rules related to child sexual exploitation, even though users said they had posted nothing of the kind.

That is why critics warn that this is not only about criminals and illegal content. It is also about the risk that automated systems wrongly capture ordinary citizens, family conversations and private moments that were never meant to leave that conversation. In democratic societies, such questions must not be secondary. Privacy of communication is not a luxury. It is a foundation of a free society, free journalism, political opposition, business security and personal dignity.
