# Facebook Parent Meta Sued Over AI-Driven Layoffs

A group of 26 employees has sued Meta over the way the company selected workers during a major round of layoffs affecting approximately 8,000 jobs.

According to the lawsuit, automated systems disproportionately affected employees who took maternity, parental, medical or family leave, as well as people who requested workplace accommodations because of a disability. Meta is the parent company of Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp. It denies the allegations and says employment decisions were made by people, not AI systems.

## What do the employees claim in the lawsuit?

The lawsuit was filed in the US District Court for the Northern District of California after Meta decided to eliminate approximately **8,000 jobs** in May. A group of **26 employees** now claims that internal AI systems, automated performance ratings and digital activity data influenced who was placed on the layoff list.

Meta allegedly used a combination of internal artificial intelligence systems, automated performance assessments and data concerning employees’ digital activity. According to the lawsuit, these systems were used to score, compare and rank workers and may have influenced who was selected for dismissal. The employees claim that decisions with serious consequences for their jobs, income, health insurance and immigration status were based on systems whose operation they could neither examine nor effectively challenge.

## Are 26 plaintiffs really the final number?

The fact that 26 employees filed the lawsuit does not necessarily mean that only 26 people were affected in a similar way. It represents the number of people who managed to find one another, contact lawyers and join the legal action before it was filed.

Employees at major technology companies communicate through corporate email addresses, internal phone numbers, chat platforms and closed business systems. Access to those channels can disappear almost overnight after a dismissal. When colleagues have not previously exchanged private contact details, finding one another after leaving the company can be extremely difficult, particularly when they worked in different teams, cities and states.

The number of plaintiffs may therefore not reflect the full scale of the problem. It may show only how many people managed to reconnect outside Meta’s internal infrastructure within a relatively short period. However, there is currently no public confirmation that a larger organised group of employees is pursuing the same claims, so this remains a possible explanation rather than an established fact.

## Did employees on leave appear less productive?

A central part of the lawsuit concerns the way automated systems interpreted periods during which employees were not active. A person taking maternity, medical or another form of legally protected leave will naturally have fewer recorded work activities, fewer completed tasks and less data available for measuring performance.

The employees claim Meta’s systems failed to properly distinguish poor performance from a justified absence. As a result, a lack of activity during protected leave may have been interpreted as lower productivity.

Meta employees are now also seeing how artificial intelligence can operate without context and struggle to understand the real world. Numbers may show that someone was less active during a particular period, but an algorithm without sufficient context may not understand that the person was pregnant, receiving medical treatment, caring for a family member or exercising another legally protected right.

All 26 plaintiffs took some form of protected leave or requested or received a workplace accommodation related to a disability. They include pregnant women, parents, people receiving medical treatment and employees caring for family members.

## What data did Meta allegedly use?

The lawsuit refers to a collection of internal artificial intelligence systems and tools used to monitor employee performance. These allegedly included AI ratings, productivity data, the use of internal tools and other indicators of employees’ digital activity.

It also refers to the monitoring of activity connected to keyboards, computer mice, web browsers and workplace applications. The employees claim such data may have been used without adequate individual review and without removing the effects of legally protected absences.

The problem is not necessarily that a company measures performance, but how its systems interpret the collected data. An employee who spent several months on maternity leave cannot have the same amount of digitally recorded activity as someone who worked throughout the entire period. When an algorithm fails to recognise that difference, figures that appear objective can produce an unfair or discriminatory result.

## What are the employees asking the court to do?

The plaintiffs are seeking a preliminary court order that would stop their dismissals while their claims are considered. Potential remedies include reinstatement, back pay, lost equity, benefits and other damages.

For some employees, losing their jobs could have additional consequences, including the loss of health insurance or problems with an immigration status that depends on continued employment.

Meta says the allegations are unfounded. According to the company, organisational and employment decisions were made by people rather than artificial intelligence. The court has yet to determine whether AI systems were used in the way described in the lawsuit or whether employees taking protected leave were subjected to discrimination.

## Facebook and Instagram employees now face what social media users have experienced for more than a year

The allegations in the lawsuit are particularly notable in the context of Meta’s broader approach to automated decision-making. Employees now claim that opaque AI ratings and activity data may have influenced decisions directly affecting their jobs, income and future.

Facebook and Instagram users have described a very similar problem for months, and perhaps for more than a year. AI moderation has relentlessly deleted profiles and blocked long-term users, often ordinary people who were neither spammers nor owners of newly created suspicious accounts.

Automated systems can restrict or disable a profile, remove a page, block access to business tools or erase a digital identity built over many years, often without a clear explanation and without a genuine opportunity for a human to review the case.

When an automated AI system makes a wrong decision about a user, another AI often serves as the supposed “review” and confirms within seconds that the first system’s decision to keep the account blocked was correct. The user is not told what the system identified as a problem or which rule they allegedly violated.

After being removed from the platform, users often lose access to human customer support as well. They are left trying to defend themselves against an accusation they cannot see, before a system with which they cannot communicate.

The company’s employees are now making a very similar complaint. They claim they were assessed and ranked by systems whose operation they could not see, verify or challenge before being selected for dismissal.

A particularly important question is whether an algorithm considers a person’s years of work, results, experience and reputation or bases its decision primarily on recent and easily measurable data. The same issue affects user profiles. An account that has existed for 15 or 20 years, contains thousands of legitimate posts and has never presented a serious security risk can be disabled after a single incorrect automated assessment.

The same question arises in both cases: what is the value of a reputation built over many years when an algorithm can disregard it based on a handful of indicators that the affected person can neither see nor challenge?

## Can a human simply confirm a flawed AI decision?

The case raises an important question about the responsibility of companies using algorithms, automated assessments and AI recommendations to manage employees.

A company cannot necessarily avoid responsibility simply because a manager formally approved the final decision. When the manager receives a ranking or recommendation based on incomplete or incorrectly interpreted data, human confirmation does not eliminate a problem already embedded in the decision-making process.

An algorithm may appear neutral because it applies the same rules to every employee. However, equal rules do not necessarily produce equal outcomes when a system fails to account for pregnancy, disability, illness, parental leave or other legally protected circumstances.

The same applies to social media moderation. The fact that a user eventually receives a generic notification, or that an automated decision has been formally approved through an internal process, does not mean the content was actually examined by a person who understood its context.

The case against Meta could therefore become an important example for future disputes involving the use of artificial intelligence in employment, performance assessments and dismissals. It also demonstrates that the consequences of opaque automated decision-making no longer stop with platform users and may affect the people working for those companies as well.

## Our view

- The lawsuit does not prove that Meta broke the law. It presents allegations from 26 employees that the court has yet to examine.
- The employees claim AI systems and automated activity indicators contributed to their ranking for dismissal.
- A system that does not understand context may incorrectly interpret a justified absence as poor performance.
- Meta employees are now also seeing how AI can make serious decisions without understanding the real life of the person affected.
- Facebook and Instagram users have long warned about similar consequences of automated moderation, including the deletion of long-standing profiles and loss of access to business pages.
- Human confirmation of a final decision does not automatically remove possible bias in the data and AI recommendations on which it was based.
- Meta denies the allegations and says employment decisions were made by people.
- Companies must be able to explain how artificial intelligence is used in decisions affecting a person’s job, income, rights or digital identity.
