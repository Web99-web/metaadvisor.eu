# USDT Faces a New U.S. Regulatory Deadline

Tether’s USDT has already lost access to some regulated crypto platforms in the European Union because the stablecoin is not compliant with MiCA rules. Now the world’s largest stablecoin is facing a new regulatory challenge in the United States.

The U.S. GENIUS Act introduces stricter requirements for stablecoin issuers, including full backing with permitted and highly liquid assets. Tether must therefore find a way to bring USDT within the new U.S. framework over the coming years or risk reduced availability on regulated American platforms.

## Why are Tether’s reserves a problem?

USDT is designed so that one token is worth approximately one U.S. dollar. Tether therefore holds reserves intended to cover all USDT tokens in circulation.

A large share of those reserves is held in U.S. Treasury securities and other liquid instruments. However, Tether also keeps part of its reserves in gold, Bitcoin, loans and other asset categories.

That is where a potential problem arises under the GENIUS Act. The new U.S. framework favors cash, short-term U.S. government securities, bank deposits and certain repo instruments. Gold and Bitcoin are not among the standard permitted reserve assets for regulated stablecoins.

Tether may therefore not need to increase the total value of its reserves, but it could be required to change their composition and replace part of its gold, Bitcoin and other holdings with permitted assets.

## What does the regulatory countdown mean?

The GENIUS Act provides that, after a transition period, U.S. crypto platforms may no longer offer a stablecoin that was not issued by a permitted issuer. An exception may apply if a foreign issuer successfully completes a special regulatory process.

Because Tether is a foreign issuer, it would need to be supervised in a jurisdiction whose rules are considered comparable to U.S. requirements. It would also need to register with the relevant American regulator, hold sufficient reserves at a U.S. financial institution and be able to comply with lawful orders from U.S. authorities.

The law generally provides a three-year transition period before non-compliant stablecoins can be restricted by U.S. digital asset service providers. However, key agreements and rules for foreign issuers are expected to be developed earlier, which is why the next two years are considered crucial for Tether.

## This does not mean USDT will disappear

A possible restriction in the United States would not automatically mean the end of USDT. The stablecoin could remain available outside regulated U.S. platforms, on international exchanges and in private crypto wallets.

The U.S. law also includes exceptions for direct transfers between individuals and for the use of self-hosted software and hardware wallets.

The real risk for Tether is not an immediate collapse of USDT, but the possible loss of access to part of the regulated U.S. market. That could reduce its availability to American users and create more room for competing stablecoins.

## USDC is already one step ahead

Tether’s main competitor, USDC, is in a more favorable regulatory position. Its issuer, Circle, has already brought USDC into compliance with MiCA through a licensed company in France.

USDC therefore remains available on many regulated European platforms that have removed or restricted USDT for users in the European Economic Area.

Circle also holds USDC reserves mainly in cash, short-term U.S. Treasury securities and liquid money-market instruments. That structure is closer to the GENIUS Act requirements than Tether’s reserves, which also include gold, Bitcoin and loans.

This does not mean USDC will quickly overtake USDT. Tether still has the greatest global liquidity and a strong position on exchanges outside the United States and the European Union.

## USDT between MiCA and the GENIUS Act

Tether is now under regulatory pressure in two major markets. In the European Union, USDT is not a MiCA-compliant stablecoin, which is why some regulated platforms no longer offer it to users in the European Economic Area.

In the United States, the GENIUS Act introduces additional requirements concerning permitted reserves, registration and supervision of foreign issuers.

Tether has not yet published a detailed plan for bringing the existing USDT fully into compliance with the new U.S. rules. The company could change the structure of its reserves, seek an appropriate regulatory status or develop a new product specifically for the U.S. market.

The countdown therefore does not mean that USDT is facing an immediate ban. It means that Tether can no longer assume its existing model will automatically be accepted on regulated markets in the United States and the European Union without major changes.
