# BONK DAO: attacker spent $4M to vote through a $20M theft

**BONK is one of the best-known memecoins on the Solana network, created as a community token within the Solana ecosystem. But its DAO is now at the center of a serious governance attack: around $20 million was drained from the treasury after an attacker deliberately bought enough BONK tokens to push through a malicious proposal.**

This was not a classic hack where someone broke the code or exploited a technical smart contract bug. The problem was in the governance model itself. BONK DAO uses a governance system where token holders can vote on proposals, and if a proposal passes, it can be executed on-chain.

That is exactly what the attacker exploited. According to reports from CoinDesk and other crypto media, the attack began on **June 30**, when an anonymous wallet submitted a proposal to transfer funds from the treasury to a wallet controlled by the attacker. For the proposal to pass, it had to reach quorum, meaning the minimum amount of votes required for execution.

## The attacker bought votes for several days

On **July 4 and 5**, a second wallet began buying BONK tokens in order to gain enough voting power. According to available data, the attacker spent around **$4.4 million** buying BONK on exchanges such as Bybit and Binance, while part of the funds was reportedly obtained through DeFi lending platforms.

The goal was simple: buy enough tokens so the malicious proposal could be voted through.

The proposal was titled **“BIP #76 - Sowellian BonkDAO”**. It passed with only a few wallets voting, while most of the community did not participate. According to reports, the proposal passed with only **7 wallets voting**, while **more than 18,000 BONK DAO members did not take part in the vote**. Turnout was only **2.9 percent**.

That means a treasury worth around **$20 million** was not decided by the broader community, but by a very small number of active wallets. This is exactly where the weakness of DAO governance becomes visible: if most of the community does not vote, someone who buys enough tokens can take control of the decision.

## The treasury was drained through a “legal” vote

The most uncomfortable part of this story is that individual steps on the blockchain looked like legitimate transactions. Tokens were bought, a proposal was submitted, votes were cast, and the proposal was executed. The final result, however, was the withdrawal of around **$20 million** from the BONK DAO treasury.

That is why this case cannot be viewed only as ordinary theft. It was a governance attack, meaning an attack on the rules of decision-making. If a system allows enough votes to be bought on the market and then used to transfer the treasury, the problem is not only the attacker, but also the design of the DAO itself.

After news of the attack, BONK came under market pressure, and the token price fell in the hours after the announcement.

## Why this matters for DAO projects

DAO models are often presented as a democratic way to govern crypto projects. Instead of a traditional company, the community votes on changes, proposals and the use of treasury funds. But this case shows that “community voting” is not automatically safe if most members do not participate and a small number of wallets can decide over a large amount of money.

It becomes especially risky when treasury funds are large and quorum is low. In that situation, an attacker can calculate how much it costs to buy voting power and compare that cost with the value of the treasury they can take over.

In this case, according to available reports, the attacker spent around **$4.4 million** to gain voting power, while around **$20 million** was drained from the treasury. That is a very clear economic problem.

## BONK is not the only risk, but it is an important example

BONK remains one of the better-known Solana memecoins, but this incident shows that even large and liquid meme projects can have weaknesses beyond the token price itself.

With memecoins, people often talk about volatility, hype and community. But here, the problem was different: treasury and governance.

If the community is not active, if quorum is not strong enough and if there are no additional protections for large fund transfers, a DAO can become vulnerable to an attack that does not happen through a bug, but through the rules themselves.

## Our view

- BONK DAO lost around **$20 million** through a malicious governance proposal.
- The attacker reportedly spent around **$4.4 million** to buy enough BONK tokens and gain voting power.
- This was not a classic smart contract hack, but an attack through the voting system.
- The problem is that token-weighted governance can allow someone with enough capital to buy influence.
- Low community turnout increases the risk further because a small number of wallets can decide over large funds.
- The BONK case shows that a DAO treasury needs stronger protections than a simple vote.
- Conclusion: if a treasury can be drained by buying votes, governance is not only a democratic tool, but also a potential security hole.
