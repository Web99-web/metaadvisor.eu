# Apple App Store: Fake Sparrow Wallets Steal Bitcoin

Apple is facing a new federal lawsuit after three users allegedly lost a combined $1.84 million in bitcoin through fake apps that impersonated Sparrow Wallet in the App Store.

The plaintiffs claim they downloaded the apps because they believed they had passed Apple’s security review. After entering the seed phrases for their Bitcoin wallets, the attackers gained full control of the funds and transferred the bitcoin to other addresses.

A particularly serious part of the lawsuit is the allegation that one fake app remained available for more than a week after the first user reported a theft worth approximately $875,000 to Apple. During that period, another user allegedly lost around $840,000.

## Three Users Lost a Combined $1.84 Million

The lawsuit was filed on 24 July 2026 in the US District Court for the Northern District of California. It was brought by James Ramirez, Christopher Ellis and Jalen Delgado.

James Ramirez claims he downloaded the fake app on 25 July 2025. After entering his wallet information, 7.4 BTC, then worth approximately $875,000, was allegedly transferred to a scammer.

According to the lawsuit, Ramirez reported both the app and the theft to Apple on the same day. Christopher Ellis nevertheless allegedly installed the fake Sparrow Wallet on 3 August 2025, more than a week after the first report, and lost bitcoin worth around $840,000.

The third plaintiff, Jalen Delgado, downloaded the app around 1 May 2025. He allegedly lost 1.05 BTC, then worth approximately $120,000.

## The Seed Phrase Gave Attackers Full Control

The fake app asked users to enter their seed phrase, a set of words used to restore and access a cryptocurrency wallet.

A seed phrase is not an ordinary password. Anyone who possesses it can restore the wallet on another device, access the assets and transfer them without further approval from the owner.

When a user enters a seed phrase into a fake app, they effectively hand the attacker the keys to the entire wallet. Bitcoin transactions generally cannot be reversed once confirmed, and there is no bank that can stop the transfer or recover the stolen funds.

## The Real Sparrow Wallet Has No iPhone App

Sparrow Wallet is a free, open-source Bitcoin wallet designed for desktop computers. There is no official version for the iPhone, and it is not distributed as a mobile app through the App Store.

This means that apps presenting themselves in Apple’s store as the official Sparrow Wallet were using the name and appearance of the real product without the developer’s approval.

The absence of an official mobile version created an opportunity for scammers. Users searching for Sparrow Wallet in the App Store could reasonably assume the app was authentic because Apple had allowed it into its tightly controlled marketplace.

## Plaintiffs Say They Trusted Apple’s Review Process

Apple has promoted the App Store for years as a safe and controlled digital marketplace where apps are reviewed before becoming available to users.

The plaintiffs claim they relied on those messages and concluded that the app was authentic. They argue that an ordinary user has little reason to expect that a fake financial app asking directly for a seed phrase could appear in the App Store.

The lawsuit accuses Apple of violating consumer-protection laws, fraudulently and negligently misrepresenting the safety of the App Store, concealing risks, acting negligently and failing to warn users.

The plaintiffs argue that Apple did not make it sufficiently clear that an app’s presence in the App Store does not necessarily confirm the identity of its developer or guarantee that a financial or cryptocurrency app is safe.

## Apple Allegedly Failed to Act Quickly Enough

The court filing states that Ramirez and Ellis reported the fake Sparrow Wallet to Apple in July and August 2025.

The plaintiffs claim Apple did not respond to their reports and that fake Sparrow apps continued to appear in the App Store and place other users at risk.

The central allegation is therefore not simply that one fake app passed an initial review. The plaintiffs are attempting to show that Apple had been warned about a specific scam and a major theft but did not remove the danger quickly enough or warn other users.

If the first loss was reported on 25 July and the second user downloaded the app on 3 August, the court will need to determine when Apple received the report, when it reviewed the app and when the app was removed.

## The Real Developer Had Warned Apple Earlier

Craig Raw, the developer of the real Sparrow Wallet, had publicly warned about fake mobile apps using the name of his product.

In January 2024, Raw said that a fake Sparrow Wallet app was still available in the App Store even though he and other users had reported it several weeks earlier. According to his claims, more than ten fake Sparrow apps had appeared in Apple’s store since 2023.

The fake apps used the recognisable name of the legitimate wallet and asked users to enter their seed phrases, after which attackers could empty their wallets.

Raw’s claims are an important part of the story because they suggest Apple may have known about the problem before the losses suffered by the three current plaintiffs.

## Apple Threatened the Real Developer After a Warning App

After fake apps continued to appear, Raw attempted to publish his own simple app in the App Store.

It was not intended to function as a mobile Bitcoin wallet. Its sole purpose was to warn users that the real Sparrow Wallet exists only for desktop computers and that mobile apps using its name are fake.

Apple allegedly classified his actions as dishonest activity and threatened to terminate his developer account. Losing the account could have affected the signing and distribution of Sparrow Wallet’s official macOS version.

The result was an almost absurd situation: fake apps impersonating Sparrow Wallet passed through the App Store, while the real developer faced problems after trying to warn users that no official mobile version existed.

## What Does Apple Say?

Apple says apps that impersonate other products violate App Store rules and are removed once the company identifies them.

The company said it had removed the reported fake Sparrow apps and terminated the related developer accounts.

Apple also says it rejected more than 371,000 submitted apps in 2025 for copying other products, containing misleading material or distributing spam.

Those figures do not answer the central question in the lawsuit: how much time passed between the first specific theft report and the removal of the app, and whether the second user’s loss could have been prevented during that period.

## Apple Previously Won a Similar Case

Apple has faced a lawsuit involving a fake cryptocurrency wallet app before.

In a 2021 case, a court accepted Apple’s argument that the company was not the creator of the malicious app but a platform distributing third-party content. The lawsuit was dismissed.

The new case attempts to establish a different basis for liability. The plaintiffs are not merely claiming that a fake app accidentally appeared in the store. They argue that Apple controlled its distribution, promoted the App Store as a safe environment and allegedly failed to act quickly enough after specific warnings and a reported theft.

The court must now decide whether those circumstances could be sufficient to hold Apple legally responsible for the resulting losses.

## What Are the Plaintiffs Seeking?

The three users are seeking the return of their lost assets, damages, interest and reimbursement of legal and court costs.

They are also asking for court orders that would require Apple to improve its review of cryptocurrency apps and provide clearer warnings about the limits of App Store oversight.

The case could therefore have consequences beyond the individual losses. If the court accepts the plaintiffs’ arguments, Apple’s strict control over the App Store could become a basis for greater responsibility rather than only protection from liability for third-party apps.

## The App Store Is Not a Substitute for Security Checks

Cryptocurrency users should not enter a seed phrase into an app simply because it appears in Apple’s or Google’s official store.

Before installing an app, users should verify the project’s official website, the exact developer name, the link to the official app and whether a mobile version of the product exists at all.

Seed phrases should never be sent through messages, email or customer support. A legitimate developer, exchange or support team will never ask for one to verify an account or solve a technical problem.

A user who loses control of a seed phrase does not merely lose access to an account. They give the person who obtained it complete control over all assets held in the connected wallet.
