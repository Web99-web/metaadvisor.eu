# Samsung, Micron and SanDisk Fall on China Fears

Global chipmakers are once again under heavy pressure. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix recorded double-digit losses in South Korea, while the sell-off spread to US memory producers Micron and SanDisk.

The immediate trigger came from China. The strong stock market debut of Chinese memory-chip producer CXMT and reports of progress in domestic semiconductor manufacturing equipment raised fresh questions about how quickly China could reduce its technological dependence on the West and challenge the world’s leading memory manufacturers.

However, the decline on 28 July was not an isolated event. Technology and AI stocks had already been under pressure for several days because of high valuations, rising data-centre costs and growing doubts about how enormous AI projects will be financed.

## Samsung and SK Hynix Dragged the KOSPI Almost 11% Lower

Samsung Electronics fell by around 14.4% on 28 July, while SK Hynix lost approximately 14.7%. South Korea’s KOSPI index closed 10.84% lower at 6,023.66 points, recording its largest one-day decline since 4 March 2026.

Samsung and SK Hynix together account for more than half of the KOSPI’s weighting, so the collapse of the country’s two largest memory-chip producers quickly developed into a broader sell-off across the South Korean market. Of the 917 shares traded, only 36 closed higher, while foreign investors sold approximately five trillion won worth of South Korean equities.

SK Hynix is one of the world’s leading memory-chip manufacturers and an especially important supplier of HBM memory used with advanced AI processors. Samsung is a much more diversified company, but it is also one of the world’s largest producers of DRAM and NAND memory. Both companies are therefore highly sensitive to changes in expectations for future memory-chip supply and prices.

## China’s CXMT Became a Stock Market Giant Overnight

The stock market debut of Chinese company ChangXin Memory Technologies, better known as CXMT, attracted the most attention. The company raised 57.92 billion yuan, or approximately $8.6 billion, in the largest Asian IPO of 2026 and the biggest listing by a Chinese semiconductor producer to date.

CXMT shares rose by approximately 466% during their first day of trading, temporarily making the company the most valuable publicly listed business in mainland China. Such a large increase does not mean that CXMT has already overtaken Samsung, SK Hynix or Micron technologically. The small proportion of shares available for public trading also probably amplified the initial price surge.

Nevertheless, the IPO gives the Chinese producer access to a large amount of capital that can be used for technological development and expansion of manufacturing capacity. CXMT produces DRAM memory used in computers, smartphones, servers and numerous other devices, placing it directly in a market currently dominated by Samsung, SK Hynix and US-based Micron.

## Fears of a New Wave of Chinese Overproduction

Investors have already seen how the massive expansion of Chinese manufacturing capacity transformed global markets for solar panels, batteries, electric vehicles and steel. There are now concerns that a similar scenario could develop in memory chips.

Should Chinese manufacturers significantly increase DRAM and NAND production with strong government and financial support, larger quantities of cheaper chips could enter the market. This would push prices lower and place pressure on the profit margins of established producers, particularly companies whose results depend heavily on cyclical changes in memory prices.

CXMT still lags behind the leading manufacturers in the most advanced HBM memory, which is essential for AI data centres. However, markets are not reacting only to the current situation. They are also pricing in the possibility that China could rapidly reduce its technological gap over the coming years and capture a larger share of the traditional DRAM market.

## China Is Also Advancing in Chipmaking Equipment

Additional pressure came from reports that Chinese companies are making progress in developing domestic DUV lithography systems. Lithography machines transfer extremely complex patterns onto silicon wafers during semiconductor production, and China has long depended on imported equipment because of US and European export restrictions.

Domestic DUV systems are not yet a complete replacement for the most advanced technology produced by Dutch company ASML. However, they could allow China to expand production of older and some more advanced chips without remaining entirely dependent on foreign suppliers.

The market interpreted the reports as another sign that export restrictions may slow China’s technological development but cannot stop it completely. Concerns about stronger long-term Chinese competition therefore spread beyond Korean manufacturers to the entire global semiconductor sector.

## Micron and SanDisk Are Hit by the Same Fears

Micron competes directly with Samsung, SK Hynix and CXMT in DRAM memory. The company also produces NAND and advanced HBM memory for AI systems, leaving it exposed both to fears of Chinese competition in conventional memory and to questions about whether current growth in AI-chip demand can continue long enough to justify high market expectations.

SanDisk is particularly exposed to NAND flash memory, which is used in SSDs, data centres, smart devices and data-storage systems. NAND is a highly cyclical industry: when supply is limited, prices and manufacturer profits can rise quickly. When excess capacity and weaker demand emerge, prices can fall just as rapidly.

The possibility that Chinese manufacturers could expand capacity therefore presents a particularly significant risk for SanDisk and other companies that depend on flash-memory prices. After the strong rise in SanDisk shares during 2026, profit-taking and the forced closure of leveraged positions intensified the decline.

## The Problems Did Not Begin on 28 July

The current sell-off represents the culmination of pressure that had been building for at least five days. Wall Street weakened on 23 July after results from major technology companies again demonstrated how much capital is required to build AI infrastructure. At the same time, oil prices above $100 increased fears of inflation and interest rates remaining high for longer.

Selling pressure spread to chipmakers on 24 July. Investors began reducing their exposure to AI hardware and increasingly questioned how quickly enormous investments in data centres, processors, networking equipment and electricity infrastructure could generate corresponding profits.

Another concern emerged over the weekend. According to media reports, Nvidia held discussions about a potential guarantee of up to $250 billion to help finance a major OpenAI data centre. Such a structure would make it easier for OpenAI to finance infrastructure that would subsequently use large quantities of Nvidia chips, raising fresh questions about circular financing and how independent the underlying demand really is.

## The China News Hit an Already Weakened Market

CXMT’s share-price surge and reports of Chinese progress in lithography equipment arrived when investors were already concerned about AI-stock valuations, the sustainability of enormous capital expenditure and the financing of new data centres. The reaction was therefore much stronger than might have been expected from one Chinese IPO alone.

Several pressures converged at the same time: high chipmaker valuations, fears of Chinese competition, concerns about AI-infrastructure financing, profit-taking after strong gains and the forced sale of leveraged positions. When Samsung and SK Hynix began falling sharply, automated selling and capital withdrawals from the entire sector accelerated the decline.

The current sell-off does not mean that global demand for memory or AI infrastructure has suddenly disappeared. Data centres still require enormous quantities of HBM, DRAM and NAND memory, but the market is no longer prepared to automatically reward every company associated with AI.

## The Memory-Chip Market Is Entering a New Phase

For years, the main argument supporting memory-chip producers was limited supply combined with explosive demand from the AI industry. The emergence of stronger Chinese competition changes that calculation.

Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron and SanDisk have not lost their technological advantages overnight. CXMT still cannot offer the same products as the world’s leading manufacturers across every segment. However, the company now has substantial access to capital, support from its domestic market and a strategic role in China’s plans for technological independence.

The decline on 28 July is therefore not merely a reaction to one bad trading session. It is a warning that investors are beginning to price in the possibility that China will become a significantly stronger competitor in the global memory-chip market.
