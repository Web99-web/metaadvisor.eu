## When an algorithm gets it wrong, the individual pays the price

An incident at a Sainsbury’s store in London has once again sparked debate over the use of facial recognition systems in everyday settings. Customer Warren Rajah was asked to leave the store after staff, relying on facial recognition technology, mistakenly identified him as someone connected to a criminal incident.

Rajah, a long-time customer of the store, said staff were unable to explain why he was being removed. Instead, he was directed to a QR code linking to the website of Facewatch — a private company that operates databases of individuals deemed high-risk for parts of the retail sector.

## “I shouldn’t have to prove that I’m not a criminal”

Following the incident, Rajah contacted Facewatch, where he was asked to submit a photo of himself and a copy of his passport to verify his identity. Only after this process was he informed that he was not listed in the database and had no connection to any criminal activity.

“One of the reasons I was angry was because I shouldn’t have to prove that I’m innocent,” Rajah said. He described the experience as “Orwellian,” likening it to dystopian scenarios in which technology delivers judgments without clear explanations or a right to appeal.

## Technology — and the human factor

According to available information, the Facewatch system had flagged a different individual who entered the store, but staff mistakenly confused Rajah with that person. This further raises questions of responsibility: how much of the error lies with the algorithm, and how much with the way humans interpret and act on technological signals?

In practice, the shopper found himself caught between two parties — Sainsbury’s and Facewatch — without a clear answer as to who bears responsibility for the mistake or what rights a wrongly identified person has.

## The broader issue of privacy and surveillance

The case comes at a time of increasing use of facial recognition technology in retail, aimed at preventing theft and improving security. Critics warn that such systems, particularly when deployed without transparent procedures and clear appeal mechanisms, can lead to serious violations of privacy and human dignity.

While proponents argue that the technology enhances security, this incident demonstrates that errors in the real world have tangible consequences — and that the burden of those errors is borne not by algorithms, but by people.

## Our take (MetaAdvisor):

- Facial recognition systems remain far from infallible.
- Misidentification can have serious consequences for individuals.
- Responsibility between retailers and technology providers is often unclear.
- Transparency and the right to explanation are crucial for public trust.
- Cases like this call for clearer regulatory frameworks.
