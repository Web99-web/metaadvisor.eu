# Bitcoin "Fakeout" and "Shakeout" on US Holiday: $120M Liquidated in Hours

<small><em>Image is symbolic.</em></small>

While Wall Street was closed for a US bank holiday, the crypto market reminded traders why it remains one of the most volatile financial arenas. Bitcoin (BTC) briefly surged toward $70,000 before sharply reversing, wiping out gains and triggering approximately $120 million in liquidations within just a few hours.

The abrupt moves reignited discussion around so-called “fakeout” and “shakeout” patterns — situations where price breaks key levels only to reverse quickly and force overleveraged traders out of their positions.

## What Are Fakeouts and Shakeouts?

A “fakeout” occurs when price breaks through an important resistance or support level, luring traders into new positions, only to reverse direction shortly after. A “shakeout” refers to the process of forcing weaker participants out of the market, often through stop-loss triggers or forced liquidations.

In this case, Bitcoin’s move toward $70,000 attracted new long positions before a rapid downturn hit both longs and shorts. Such dual-sided liquidations are more common during periods of reduced liquidity.

Data from market monitoring platforms shows roughly $120 million in crypto positions were liquidated in a four-hour window, most of them highly leveraged trades.

## The Holiday Effect and Thin Order Books

A key factor was the US bank holiday. When traditional financial markets close, trading volumes in crypto can decline as well. Lower volume leads to thinner order books, meaning fewer large buy and sell orders are available at each price level.

Under such conditions, relatively modest capital flows can move prices more aggressively. This creates an environment where large players — often referred to as “whales” — can influence short-term price action more easily.

During the session, blocks of bids and asks were rapidly cleared, and new walls were placed just above or below price, amplifying volatility and accelerating the cascade of liquidations.

## Comparisons to 2022 and RSI Signals

Some analysts pointed to similarities with 2022, particularly in weekly RSI (Relative Strength Index — a technical indicator measuring overbought or oversold conditions) patterns.

However, market structure in 2026 differs significantly from that of 2022. Spot ETFs are now established, institutional participation is deeper, and liquidity pools are broader.

Technical comparisons therefore require context.

## Who Took the Biggest Hit?

Short-term leveraged traders were the primary casualties. When price rapidly crosses liquidation thresholds, exchanges automatically close positions to prevent further losses.

These forced liquidations can intensify price moves, creating a snowball effect that generates sharp swings without necessarily reflecting a change in long-term fundamentals.

Long-term investors, by contrast, often view such episodes as short-term noise.

## Manipulation or Market Mechanics?

Periods of reduced liquidity naturally make markets more sensitive to large orders. That does not automatically imply illegal manipulation, but it does highlight the structural differences between crypto and traditional markets.

Despite growing regulation and institutional adoption, crypto remains a 24/7 global market with high leverage participation and strong retail involvement — a combination that fosters rapid price swings.

## What Does This Signal for 2026?

The episode underscores that volatility remains embedded in the crypto ecosystem. Integration with traditional finance and the growth of ETFs have not eliminated abrupt movements — they have reshaped them.

Bitcoin continues to trade within a relatively narrow broader range, without a decisive long-term breakout. Fakeouts and shakeouts are often part of consolidation phases rather than clear trend reversals.

For traders, it is a reminder of leverage risks. For investors, it is a reminder that short-term turbulence does not necessarily alter long-term theses.
