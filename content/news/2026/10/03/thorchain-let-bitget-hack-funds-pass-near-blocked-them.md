# THORChain Let Bitget Hack Funds Pass, NEAR Blocked Them

**After the Bitget hack worth nearly $388 million, some of the stolen funds began moving through cross-chain protocols and more private blockchain channels. That has triggered a new debate across the crypto industry: should a decentralized protocol intervene when it knows that funds linked to a theft are moving through its infrastructure? THORChain and NEAR Intents have taken very different positions.**

Bitget CEO Gracy Chen publicly called on THORChain to reject transactions linked to the attacker’s addresses. THORChain did not do so. The protocol maintained that permissionless infrastructure should not selectively decide who is allowed to use it, even when the funds are connected to a major hack.

NEAR Intents took the opposite approach. Its SHIELD security layer identified tens of millions of dollars in attempted flows linked to the Bitget incident and tried to stop some of them. The debate therefore shifted from who was hacked to a much broader question: what does decentralization actually mean in practice?

## THORChain maintained its permissionless approach

THORChain has long positioned itself as neutral cross-chain infrastructure that should not discriminate against users or individual addresses. That is why it did not comply with requests to block transactions associated with the Bitget attacker.

The logic behind that position is clear. If a protocol can decide that one address is not allowed to use its infrastructure, it is no longer fully neutral. Someone then has to decide which addresses are permitted, which are not, and according to what rules.

For supporters of the permissionless model, that is precisely the line decentralized systems should not cross.

## NEAR Intents chose active control of suspicious flows

NEAR Intents took a different path through its SHIELD system. Instead of complete neutrality, it chose a model in which suspicious transactions can be identified, filtered and, in some cases, stopped.

According to available reports, SHIELD identified more than $50 million in attempted transfers linked to the Bitget incident. Most of those attempts did not go through, while a smaller amount still passed through the system.

NEAR is therefore sending a very different message from THORChain. Its argument is not that a blockchain itself has to become centralized, but that applications built on top of a blockchain can still decide which transactions they are willing to process.

In other words, a user may still be able to hold and send tokens, while a specific application or service does not necessarily have to process every transaction presented to it.

## The debate is no longer only technical

At first glance, this looks like a conventional security story. In reality, it touches one of the oldest and most sensitive questions in crypto.

If a decentralized protocol never intervenes, it can become an attractive channel for moving stolen funds. If it does intervene, the next question is who gets to make those decisions, under which criteria, and how decentralized the system remains afterward.

THORChain and NEAR now represent two very different models for the future of crypto: one radically neutral, the other more pragmatically controlled.

## Critics point to earlier THORChain interventions

The debate has also intensified because critics point to earlier cases inside the THORChain ecosystem. Validators have intervened in the past when the protocol itself was under pressure or when its own infrastructure needed protection.

That creates an uncomfortable question: if intervention is possible when the system itself is at risk, why should intervention be considered unacceptable when funds publicly linked to a hack are moving through the same infrastructure?

That does not mean the answer is simple. There is a significant difference between protecting the operation of a protocol and introducing a mechanism that maintains a list of unwanted wallets. But that distinction is now at the center of the argument.

## NEAR’s model also has problems

An active blocking model may look more responsible at first, but it raises serious questions of its own. Someone has to decide whether a specific transaction is suspicious. Someone has to determine when funds should be blocked or released and what happens if the system gets it wrong.

That creates the risk of false positives and broader governance problems. Even when the goal is protection, every additional layer of control introduces some degree of reliance on the actors enforcing it.

NEAR’s model may therefore be more useful for compliance and ecosystem protection, but at the same time it moves further away from the ideal of complete neutrality.

## Some funds also entered Zcash’s shielded pool

The Bitget case also shows that blocking funds in one place does not necessarily end the attacker’s route. On-chain investigators linked 2,746 ZEC, worth about $3.9 million at the time, to wallets associated with the attacker before the funds entered Zcash’s Ironwood shielded pool.

That matters because the entry into a shielded pool remains publicly visible, but the relationship between sender, recipient and individual amounts becomes hidden inside the pool. After that point, the public blockchain no longer provides a clear path directly linking a specific deposit to a specific withdrawal.

This does not mean the funds become completely invisible. It does mean that tracing them becomes significantly more difficult and can no longer rely on a simple deterministic transaction trail.

## Our view

* **The Bitget hack has become much more than a single security incident.** It is now a real-world test of some of decentralization’s core principles.
* **THORChain remained committed to its permissionless approach.** That protects protocol neutrality, but also accepts the risk that hack-linked funds can move through the same infrastructure.
* **NEAR Intents chose a more active security model.** That can make suspicious flows harder to process, but it also introduces actors who must make sensitive decisions.
* **The central question is no longer only whether something can be blocked, but who should have the authority to decide.**
* **Zcash’s shielded pool illustrates the limits of both approaches.** Even when one service attempts to block suspicious flows, funds can move toward infrastructure where tracing becomes much more difficult.
* **The crypto industry is increasingly forced to choose between ideological purity and practical user protection.**
* **THORChain and NEAR currently represent those two opposing positions particularly clearly.**
