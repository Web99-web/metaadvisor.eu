# Ledger Investigates Nearly $93 Million Crypto Theft as Reseller's Ownership Change Raises Questions

**Hardware wallet manufacturer Ledger is investigating reports of missing cryptocurrency involving customers of its authorized reseller CryptoBilis in Southeast Asia. Blockchain analysts estimate potential losses at $92.9 million, while the case has taken a new turn following revelations that CryptoBilis changed ownership several months before the incident. The company's former founders say a contractual confidentiality agreement prevented a public announcement of the acquisition until mid-October 2026, almost exactly when the security incident became public.**

On October 9, 2026, Ledger confirmed that it was investigating reports of missing funds involving customers who purchased its hardware wallets through CryptoBilis, a reseller operating in Malaysia, Indonesia, and the Philippines. The company requested a temporary suspension of sales and shipments through that reseller. Customers who purchased devices within the previous 90 days were advised not to activate them for the time being, while those already using the devices were encouraged to consider transferring their cryptocurrency to a new, trusted wallet with an entirely new recovery phrase.

Although the cause of the incident has not been officially established, the investigation has raised broader questions about hardware wallet supply chain security. Particular attention has turned to the ownership change at CryptoBilis, which occurred months before the reported losses, and the fact that public disclosure of the acquisition had been delayed under a confidentiality agreement.

## CryptoBilis Changed Ownership in 2026

CryptoBilis was not an unknown online merchant. It was listed among Ledger's authorized resellers in Southeast Asia. According to publicly reported information, the business underwent an acquisition and management transition during 2026. Its former founders, Arravind Prabu and Vimalatheethan, issued a statement saying they had completed the transfer of all operational, managerial, and administrative responsibilities to the new owners in March 2026.

According to their account, they no longer had access to the company's internal systems, customer databases, order histories, administrative accounts, or corporate communications following the handover. They also stated that they continued to provide limited advisory and coordination support during certain industry events, but without direct access to daily operations or IT infrastructure. Their statement emphasized that they were no longer managing CryptoBilis when the cryptocurrency losses were reported.

## New Owner Reportedly Acquired 100% of the Company

According to published information attributed to Malaysian corporate records, 100% of CryptoBilis shares had been transferred by August 3, 2026, to an individual identified as Jiaming, with a registered address in China's Heilongjiang province. These reports point to a complete ownership transfer following the earlier operational handover.

However, a change in ownership is not evidence of involvement in a theft. At the time of writing, there is no publicly confirmed evidence that the new owner participated in device tampering, accessed private keys, or transferred customer funds. Nevertheless, the timeline is relevant to the broader circumstances because the security incident became public after control of the distribution channel had changed.

## Confidentiality Agreement Delayed the Acquisition Announcement

One of the most unusual aspects of the case involves a non-disclosure agreement, or NDA, associated with the sale of CryptoBilis. The former founders say the parties were contractually required to postpone the joint public announcement of the acquisition until mid-October 2026. According to their statement, suppliers had been informed earlier about the ownership and management changes, but the joint public announcement had not yet been scheduled.

This means that the major security incident became public only days before the period in which the ownership change was expected to be announced. Some reports mention October 19 as a possible date when restrictions on public disclosure would expire. If accurate, Ledger confirmed its investigation just ten days before that date. However, the former founders' direct statement refers to mid-October without independently confirming that specific deadline.

The timing has attracted attention within the cryptocurrency community, but it does not prove that the acquisition, confidentiality agreement, and disappearance of funds were components of a coordinated plan. NDAs are common in corporate acquisitions. Establishing any connection would require technical evidence and a verified timeline of the distribution of potentially compromised devices.

## Blockchain Analysts Trace $92.9 Million in Suspected Losses

While Ledger investigates the circumstances, blockchain researchers are attempting to reconstruct the movement of funds. Initial public estimates suggested losses exceeding $72 million, later increasing to more than $86 million. On October 9, blockchain analytics company Bitquery published an investigation identifying approximately $92.9 million in suspected outflows from 311 wallets across five blockchain networks.

According to the analysis, Tron accounted for the largest estimated losses at approximately $70.5 million, followed by Bitcoin at around $16.8 million. The remaining funds were associated with Ethereum, BNB Chain, and Polygon. Importantly, the figure of 311 refers to wallets included in the analysis, not necessarily 311 separate victims.

Ledger has not officially confirmed the total amount lost or established that every transaction identified by Bitquery involved a device purchased through CryptoBilis. Blockchain records can help investigators reconstruct movements of funds, but they do not independently establish the cause or full scope of a security incident.

## Suspicions of Physically Modified Hardware Wallets

One of the theories being discussed is that some hardware wallets may have been physically modified before reaching their customers. This type of attack is commonly known as a *supply chain attack*. Unlike a conventional cyberattack targeting a computer system, an attacker could potentially compromise a device during storage, distribution, or preparation for sale.

Discussions surrounding the possible attack method have included additional electronic components or other modifications capable of intercepting sensitive information during device use. Of particular importance is the recovery phrase, a sequence of words that allows access to a cryptocurrency wallet to be restored. If an attacker obtains this phrase, they may be able to access the funds without possessing the original hardware device.

Some reports have raised the possibility of hidden electronic implants, but it has not been publicly confirmed that such modifications caused the reported losses. There is also no official confirmation that Ledger's secure element was breached or that all devices manufactured by the company share a common vulnerability. The distinction between a compromised device within the distribution chain and a fundamental security flaw in Ledger's technology remains essential.

## Did the Attacker Collect Private Keys in Advance?

Bitquery's analysis describes unusual transaction patterns that could indicate a coordinated operation. According to the researchers, smaller test transactions were observed over approximately two weeks before the major outflows. These were reportedly followed by a large number of transfers occurring within very short intervals.

This pattern raises the possibility that an attacker had already obtained access to multiple private keys or recovery phrases and waited for a particular moment to move substantial amounts of cryptocurrency simultaneously. Under such a scenario, users could continue operating their wallets normally for months, receiving funds and assuming their assets were secure, while an attacker already possessed the information needed to gain access later.

For now, this remains a hypothesis based on blockchain activity rather than an officially established reconstruction of the attack. Transaction records can reveal the timing, amounts, and destinations of transfers, but they cannot independently prove how recovery phrases were compromised or who was responsible.

## Tether Reportedly Froze Around $10 Million in USDT

According to Bitquery, Tether froze approximately $10 million in USDT associated with the suspicious transactions. Investigators also identified transfers of some ETH through Tornado Cash, a protocol that makes subsequent movements of cryptocurrency more difficult to trace. At the time of its analysis, Bitquery estimated that approximately $79 million could still be tracked across blockchain networks.

However, the ability to trace cryptocurrency does not mean those funds can necessarily be recovered. Even when investigators identify the addresses holding the assets, freezing or returning the funds can involve significant technical and legal obstacles. These figures reflect the transactions examined as of October 9 and may change as the investigation progresses.

## Why the Authorized Reseller Status Matters

The CryptoBilis case is particularly concerning because customers were not purchasing devices from an unknown seller with no established business history. They were buying from a merchant listed among the manufacturer's authorized distribution partners, giving them reason to expect a higher level of reliability and security.

The incident raises questions about how frequently hardware wallet manufacturers review their distribution partners and what happens when those businesses change ownership. Changes in management, warehouse access, or product-handling procedures may also alter the security profile of a distribution channel. An ownership change is not inherently dangerous, but the case illustrates why supply chain security cannot depend solely on whether a retailer previously received authorized reseller status.

## What Should Customers Who Purchased Ledger Devices From CryptoBilis Do?

Ledger's current warning primarily concerns devices purchased from CryptoBilis within the previous 90 days. Customers who have not yet activated their devices have been advised to postpone setup while the investigation continues. Those who have already used them should consider transferring their cryptocurrency to a new, trusted hardware wallet using an entirely new recovery phrase.

Simply moving funds to another address derived from the same potentially compromised recovery phrase is not sufficient. Recovery phrases should never be entered into websites, shared with customer support representatives, or disclosed to individuals offering to recover stolen cryptocurrency. Customers should follow official security announcements and retain documentation relating to their device purchases.

Other Ledger owners should not automatically assume that their wallets have been compromised. There is currently no confirmation of a universal security vulnerability affecting all Ledger products.

## Acquisition, NDA, and Crypto Theft: What Still Needs to Be Established?

The CryptoBilis case now involves several significant events whose chronology can be outlined, although a causal relationship between them has not been demonstrated. According to the former founders, operational control was transferred in March 2026. Published corporate information indicates that the new owner held all company shares by early August. The joint public announcement of the acquisition was contractually delayed until mid-October, while Ledger confirmed its investigation into major losses associated with CryptoBilis customers on October 9.

At the same time, blockchain analysts are tracing transactions valued at nearly $93 million, while the possibility of devices being compromised during distribution remains under discussion. What is still missing is technical evidence linking these developments into a single established sequence. Investigators would need to determine whether devices were physically modified, where any modifications occurred, how many devices were affected, and who had access to sensitive information.

Equally important is determining whether the ownership change had any causal connection to the incident at all. Without such evidence, it would be unjustified to conclude that the acquisition was carried out to facilitate a theft or that the new owner was responsible for the reported losses.

## Our Take

* **This is no longer just a story about missing cryptocurrency.** The ownership change at an authorized reseller months before the security incident raises additional questions about distribution chain security and responsibility for products reaching end customers.
* **The timing is particularly noteworthy.** The former founders say the acquisition announcement was contractually postponed until mid-October, while Ledger confirmed its investigation on October 9. The overlap warrants closer examination but is not proof of a premeditated theft.
* **An NDA is not inherently suspicious.** Confidentiality agreements are common in corporate acquisitions. However, this case demonstrates why transparency surrounding ownership changes can matter when a business sells products designed to protect significant financial assets.
* **Customers trusted an authorized sales channel.** This is precisely why a potential supply chain attack can be especially dangerous. Buyers may have little reason to question a device purchased from a manufacturer-approved reseller.
* **The estimated $92.9 million has not been officially confirmed as the total loss.** Bitquery identified 311 wallets and suspicious transactions, but Ledger has not confirmed the final amount or the total number of affected individuals.
* **Physically modified devices remain an unconfirmed possibility.** If investigators establish that recovery phrases were collected through compromised products, it would demonstrate how an attack could bypass the security protections of the hardware itself.
* **Ownership changes must be separated from allegations of responsibility.** Available information indicates that CryptoBilis changed hands, but it does not establish that the new owner participated in the theft.
* **The incident could change how authorized resellers are evaluated.** Hardware wallet manufacturers may need to strengthen oversight not only of device authenticity but also ownership changes, storage, distribution, and access to products before delivery.

The central question is no longer simply how cryptocurrency worth nearly $93 million could disappear, but whether someone may have exploited customers' trust in an authorized reseller to place compromised devices in their hands. If such a scenario is confirmed, the CryptoBilis case could become one of the more significant examples of a supply chain attack in the cryptocurrency industry.

Until technical investigation results are published, any connection between the acquisition, the delayed public announcement, and the disappearance of funds remains an open question.
