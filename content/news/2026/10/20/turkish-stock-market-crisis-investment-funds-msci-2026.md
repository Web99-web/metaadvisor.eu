# Turkish Stock Market in Deep Crisis: 131 Funds Face Liquidation as MSCI Warns of Possible Market Downgrade

**Turkey's stock market is facing significant turmoil after its benchmark BIST 100 index lost 16.65% in September, marking its worst monthly performance since 2008. The investment fund crisis has triggered forced asset sales and a regulatory order to liquidate 131 funds, while MSCI has suspended certain adjustments to Turkish equity indexes. Meanwhile, social media claims that one trillion Turkish lira disappeared from the market in a single morning, accompanied by emergency sales of US Treasury bonds and gold reserves, remain unsupported by sufficiently reliable evidence.**

The Turkish stock market has been under pressure for weeks, driven by far more than ordinary daily share price fluctuations. At the center of the crisis are investment funds with substantial exposure to illiquid stocks, investor redemption requests and allegations of manipulation involving individual securities. A further warning came from global index provider MSCI (Morgan Stanley Capital International), whose decisions can significantly influence investment funds with exposure to emerging markets.

## September Marked the Worst Monthly Decline Since 2008

The BIST 100 ended September down 16.65%, recording its weakest monthly performance since the global financial crisis of 2008. According to Reuters, the index had fallen more than 20% from its record closing high in May by the end of September, meeting the commonly used definition of a bear market. The decline accelerated in mid-September as investors increasingly questioned whether certain funds could meet redemption requests.

It is important to distinguish the broader index from individual stocks. Shares of smaller companies with limited trading activity can be especially vulnerable to forced selling because large positions may be difficult to liquidate without substantial price reductions. When investment funds urgently need cash, they may also be forced to sell more liquid holdings, spreading downward pressure across the wider market. This mechanism was one of the central factors behind Turkey's stock market crisis.

## Why Are 131 Investment Funds Being Liquidated?

Turkey's capital markets regulator ordered the liquidation of 131 investment funds. According to a Reuters report published on October 2, approximately $20 billion in assets and more than half a million investors were affected. Some of the funds had previously delivered exceptionally high returns, attracting capital in a country struggling with high inflation and persistent pressure on its domestic currency.

However, rising fund valuations do not necessarily mean that all underlying assets can be sold quickly at their reported prices. When a fund holds substantial positions in thinly traded stocks, large redemption requests can force sales at increasingly lower prices. Those declines may then trigger further withdrawals, creating a cycle of falling valuations and deteriorating liquidity.

Allegations involving coordinated trading and the artificial support of certain share prices are under regulatory and judicial investigation. Such allegations must be distinguished from legally established findings. The existence of investigations does not automatically establish the guilt of every fund, company or individual involved.

Liquidation also does not necessarily mean that a fund's entire portfolio has become worthless or that every investor will lose all invested capital. The final outcome depends on the value of assets that can be recovered, outstanding liabilities and the applicable liquidation procedures. Nevertheless, the crisis illustrates how quickly concentrated investments, limited liquidity and a sudden loss of confidence can destabilize financial markets.

## MSCI Freezes Changes to Turkish Equity Indexes

On October 15, MSCI announced a temporary suspension of certain changes involving Turkish securities in its indexes. The measures affect adjustments associated with regular index reviews and specific corporate events, including increases in certain inclusion factors, changes to recognized share counts, additions to the MSCI Turkey Investable Market Index and upward movements between market capitalization size segments.

MSCI cited concerns raised by institutional investors about potentially coordinated trading activity involving funds connected to smaller listed companies. Such practices may undermine the reliability of free-float estimates, which measure the portion of a company's shares genuinely available for public trading.

Regulators are expected to improve transparency regarding beneficial ownership, ensure effective market surveillance and demonstrate credible enforcement against market manipulation.

**Turkey has not lost its emerging market classification.** However, MSCI warned that if sufficient progress is not demonstrated by June 2027, it could consider additional measures, including consultations on potentially reclassifying Turkish indexes as Frontier Markets or Standalone Markets.

Such a downgrade has not been announced or approved. It remains a possibility rather than an established outcome. Nevertheless, a change in classification could affect international investment funds whose allocation rules depend on MSCI's market categories, potentially creating additional pressure on Turkish equities.

## Selling Pressure Continued Into October

Turkish equities remained under pressure following MSCI's announcement. The BIST 100 declined 2.89% on October 16 and lost another 2.49% on October 19, ending the latter session at 11,430.14 points.

These declines confirm continuing market instability, but they should not be interpreted as proof of a complete collapse of Turkey's financial system.

On October 6, Turkey's central bank stated that, in its assessment, problems involving investment funds did not represent a systemic threat to the wider financial system. Central bank governor Fatih Karahan also pointed to measures designed to maintain liquidity and indicated that authorities remained prepared to take further action if necessary.

That statement reflects the central bank's official assessment. It does not guarantee that all risks have disappeared or that financial conditions cannot deteriorate further.

## Did One Trillion Turkish Lira Really Disappear in a Single Morning?

Posts circulating on X claim that more than one trillion Turkish lira in market capitalization was wiped out immediately after trading opened.

However, no sufficiently reliable independent confirmation has been established for that precise amount or the claimed timeframe. A stock market heatmap filled with red figures can demonstrate falling share prices, but it cannot independently verify the total value lost, when those losses occurred or what caused them.

It is also essential to distinguish market capitalization from actual capital outflows. When a stock price falls, the estimated market value of all outstanding shares declines, even if only a relatively small portion of those shares has changed hands.

Consequently, a statement that a certain amount of market value has been "wiped out" does not mean that an equivalent amount of cash has physically left the stock market or disappeared from the financial system.

## Is Turkey Selling US Treasury Bonds and Gold Reserves?

The same viral post claims that Turkey is urgently selling US Treasury securities and gold reserves in an attempt to stop its domestic stock market from collapsing.

**Neither an extraordinary sell-off of those reserves nor the claimed connection to an emergency stock market rescue has been confirmed by sufficiently reliable information.**

Official US Treasury data on foreign holdings of government securities are released with a time lag. Previously published monthly figures therefore cannot establish which transactions may have occurred during trading on October 20.

Furthermore, changes in recorded holdings do not necessarily represent direct purchases or sales by a country's central bank. Valuation changes, custodial arrangements and other factors can affect how holdings appear in official statistics.

Although some countries are gradually diversifying their foreign exchange reserves, this broader trend does not establish that Turkey is currently conducting emergency sales of US government debt or gold to support its stock market.

## What Could the Crisis Mean for International Investors?

So far, the problems affecting Turkish equities have primarily involved the domestic capital market, investment funds and confidence in regulatory oversight.

International consequences could become more significant if instability spreads to banks, the Turkish lira, sovereign financing conditions or investment funds with exposure to multiple countries.

Future MSCI decisions deserve particular attention because funds tracking specific indexes may need to adjust their portfolios if Turkey's market classification or index composition changes.

At present, there is no confirmed evidence that the Turkish stock market crisis has directly caused broader declines in the Nasdaq, European stock markets or cryptocurrencies.

Although global financial markets are interconnected, potential transmission channels should be evaluated using actual capital flows, bond yield movements and official financial data rather than assumptions based solely on simultaneous price changes.

## What Should Investors Watch Next?

One of the most important developments will be the progress of the fund liquidation process and the ability of Turkish regulators to credibly address allegations of coordinated trading.

Equally important will be improvements in transparency regarding beneficial ownership and the calculation of shares genuinely available for public trading.

Market participants will also be watching the BIST 100, movements in the Turkish lira, financing conditions and any further announcements from MSCI.

A short-term recovery in the benchmark index could ease immediate selling pressure, but rising stock prices alone would not resolve the structural problems that contributed to the crisis.
