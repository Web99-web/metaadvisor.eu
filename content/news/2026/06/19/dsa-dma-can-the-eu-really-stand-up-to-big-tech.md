# DSA and DMA: Can the EU Really Rein in the Power of Big Tech?

In recent years, the European Union has presented the **Digital Services Act (DSA)** and the **Digital Markets Act (DMA)** as landmark laws designed to limit the power of the world's largest technology companies, protect users and create fairer digital markets. Brussels often presents these regulations as proof that Europe can set global standards and require the most powerful platforms in the world to respect the rights of citizens.

A few years after their introduction, however, a simple question remains: **do ordinary users actually feel this protection in practice?** Many people who have lost access to accounts, pages, groups or business tools because of automated platform decisions argue that the answer is far more complicated than official statements suggest.

## What Is the DMA?

The DMA primarily focuses on competition. The European Union identified several companies as so-called “gatekeepers” – digital giants that control key access points between businesses and consumers. These include **Alphabet (Google), Apple, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, ByteDance (TikTok) and Booking.com.**

European institutions believe these companies possess extraordinary power over digital markets. They control search, apps, advertising, communication and access to millions of users. The goal of the DMA is to prevent situations where platforms use their dominant position to suppress competition or force users into their own ecosystems.

Interestingly, the European Parliament and the European Commission regularly describe major platforms as **"key access points"** between businesses and citizens. In other words, they acknowledge that these platforms are no longer just apps but have become a crucial part of modern digital infrastructure.

That is precisely why many people ask a logical question: **if platforms have so much power that Europe must regulate them separately, what happens when that power is used incorrectly?**

## The DSA Was Supposed to Protect Users

While the DMA focuses on market power, the **Digital Services Act (DSA)** focuses on content, moderation, transparency and user rights. The idea was to create a framework in which users would not be left completely powerless when an algorithm or AI system makes a mistake.

On paper, the DSA looks impressive. Platforms should explain why content was removed, users should have the right to appeal, and large platforms should be more transparent in their decision-making. All of this sounds like an important step toward a fairer internet.

However, this is exactly where the real debate begins.

**If the DMA was created because the European Union concluded that Big Tech has too much power, and the DSA was created because users need greater protection, why do ordinary users still feel completely powerless when AI moderation gets it wrong?**

This question is increasingly being asked by people who have lost access to accounts, pages, groups or business tools because of automated decisions. European institutions speak about user protection and platform accountability, yet many citizens still do not see clear and effective protection in real life when an algorithm makes the wrong decision.

## AI Moderation and Real Life

In recent years, there have been numerous cases where users lost access to accounts, pages, groups or audiences because of decisions made by automated systems. Some lost years of work, contacts and business activities without ever receiving a clear explanation of what actually happened.

**For many small businesses, social media platforms are no longer simply entertainment apps. They have become business infrastructure.** Through Facebook and Instagram, companies find customers, run marketing campaigns, communicate with clients and build their reputation. When such an account disappears because of an algorithmic mistake, the consequences are not only social but also economic.

The European Union frequently talks about protecting users, but in the area of AI moderation the DSA still has to prove that it can provide genuine protection when algorithms make mistakes.

## Appeals Centre Europe: Protection on Paper or Real Help?

One of the most interesting tests for the DSA today is how much these new appeal mechanisms actually help users. Through the Digital Services Act, the European Union attempted to create a system in which citizens would not be completely at the mercy of decisions made by large platforms. Among these mechanisms is **Appeals Centre Europe**, which is intended to help users resolve disputes with platforms.

On paper, this sounds like a significant step forward. A user can initiate a procedure, receive an opinion or decision, and obtain confirmation that a platform may have made a mistake. However, this is precisely where many users see the biggest problem.

**A user can receive an Appeals Centre Europe decision in their favour and still not get their account back.**

**Many users do not have any functional email address or direct contact through which they can even deliver that decision to Meta.**

**Without an email address, without real human support and without a mechanism that can force a platform to implement the decision, the entire process often ends as nothing more than a piece of paper.**

For a user whose account has been disabled, the problem is not solved until access is actually restored. If they have lost access to Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Business Suite, pages or groups they spent years building, a symbolic confirmation that they may have been right does not restore their audience, contacts or business.

**In other words, a user can receive confirmation that they may have been wronged and still remain without an account, without an audience and without the ability to continue their work.**

That is why many affected users ask the same question: **what value does a decision have if there is no practical way to enforce it?**

## The Biggest Test for European Regulation Is Yet to Come

This does not mean that the DSA and DMA are failures. On the contrary, they represent some of the most ambitious attempts in the world to regulate the digital space. The European Union has placed issues on the agenda that many other countries still avoid and has acknowledged that major platforms have significant influence over the economy, competition and the daily lives of citizens.

However, laws alone are not enough. **Rights only truly exist when citizens can easily exercise them, and protection only exists when users can feel that the system works in their favour.**

If Europe believes that major platforms have become essential digital infrastructure for communication, marketing and business activity, then it will have to find ways to provide users with genuine protection rather than merely formal protection when automated systems make mistakes.

## Our View (Metaadvisor)

* The DSA and DMA represent a serious attempt by the European Union to limit the power of the world's largest technology companies and introduce greater accountability into the digital space. However, the experiences of many users show that there is a significant gap between rights that exist on paper and rights that can actually be exercised in practice.

* **The biggest problem with the DSA may not be a lack of rules, but a lack of enforcement.** Users who receive an Appeals Centre Europe decision in their favour often still do not know what to do with that decision. They do not have access to their account, they do not have access to their business tools, and they do not have a clear way to communicate with the platform.

* **If the DMA is the response to the excessive power of Big Tech, and the DSA is the response to the need for greater user protection, then the success of both laws will ultimately be measured by a simple question: can an ordinary user receive real help when AI moderation gets it wrong?**

* If social media networks and digital platforms have become critical infrastructure for the modern economy, then mistakes made by AI moderation systems should be treated with the same level of seriousness as failures in other important systems that people depend on.
