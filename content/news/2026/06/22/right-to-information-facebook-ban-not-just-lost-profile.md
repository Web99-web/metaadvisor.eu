# The Human Right to Information: A Facebook Ban Is No Longer Just a Lost Profile

Imagine that you are reading online news. You see a short article, notice or announcement that interests you. Maybe it is about a local campaign, an event in your neighbourhood, a notice from a school, association, city, singer, politician, humanitarian group or public event. You want to see the details. You click. But the details are not in the article. **The details are on a Facebook profile, Facebook page or inside a Facebook group.** If you have been permanently removed from Facebook, that part of the information no longer exists for you. It is not secret. It is not forbidden by law. It is not dangerous. It is available to others. **But not to you. A private platform has closed the door to a space where a large part of everyday public life now takes place.**

Many people will now think: if someone was removed, there must have been a good reason. And that would be logical — if we were talking only about scammers, violent users, fake profiles or people who knowingly abuse platforms. But the problem is precisely that this is not the whole story. **After following this topic for months, it becomes clear that many completely ordinary people have lost access through automated AI moderation.** We are not talking only about influencers, politicians or public figures. We are talking about teachers, social workers, people working in marketing, owners of small businesses, local shops, group administrators, parents, volunteers and ordinary citizens. **People like your friends, neighbours, colleagues or family members.**

What happened to them? One moment they were online. The next moment a message arrived: you have been removed from the network. The decision was not explained by a human being. **It was made by an automated system.** After that, a message appeared saying that the user could appeal. People hoped that now someone would actually look at the case, understand the mistake and restore the account. But very often, after only a few minutes, another decision arrived: **you have been permanently removed for violating our rules.** Without human support. Without a clear explanation. Without a real chance to explain what happened. Without a person who can look at the context. **For the user, this does not look like moderation. It looks like an AI verdict without a court.**

And then comes the second blow. Because Meta is not only Facebook. **When a person loses a Facebook account, they often lose Messenger, Instagram and Business Suite at the same second.** That means they do not lose only the ability to post a status. They lose private conversations, business contacts, access to pages, groups, clients, ads, message archives and years of their own work.

For a small business owner, this can mean the loss of a business channel. For the administrator of a local group, the loss of a community built over many years. For a person who uses Messenger as their main communication tool, the loss of contacts and conversations. For someone who manages pages or advertising, it can mean an immediate business interruption.

## This Is Not Happening in Just One Country

This problem is not limited to Canada, Australia, Belgium, Germany, Europe or one group of users. Stories about sudden bans, wrong AI moderation and lost accounts appear around the world — from the United States and Canada to Australia, the Philippines and other countries. In many cases, the pattern looks similar: **an ordinary user, a small business, a teacher, an artist, a humanitarian organization or a local page suddenly loses access, receives an automated message and is left without real human contact.**

It is interesting that major media outlets are increasingly reporting on this topic, with **The Guardian and Guardian Australia** standing out in particular. That matters because the problem can no longer be reduced to isolated complaints from angry users. When newspapers begin documenting cases of teachers, small business owners, cultural organizations, associations that used the platform for years, or people who lost photos, contacts and business channels, then the question of bans becomes a question of digital rights.

This is not about saying that platforms should not remove real abuse. Of course they must. The problem begins when a punishment happens in seconds, without a clear reason, without human conversation and without a real path back. **If a teacher, a social worker, a small shop or an ordinary user suddenly loses an account and does not even know what exactly they did wrong, then we are no longer talking only about content moderation. We are talking about digital exclusion.**

That is why a Facebook ban is no longer just a lost profile. **A social network today functions like a global village.** It has its neighbourhoods, squares, interest groups, notice boards, public discussions, private messages, business shop windows and local communities. In this digital space, people move, read, ask, buy, sell, seek help, comment, organize events, follow schools, associations, neighbourhoods, politicians, singers, companies and friends.

Yesterday, you could still walk through that space. You could explore. You could talk to friends. You could publish your opinion and read other people’s opinions. You could find out in seconds whether something dangerous had happened in your neighbourhood. You could see whether a public celebration was being organized. You could post that you had lost a dog, were giving away a bed, were looking for a handyman recommendation or were following a post from a local association. **And then suddenly — you cannot anymore.** You cannot see certain posts. You cannot enter groups. You cannot send messages to people you communicated with for years. You cannot edit pages you created. You cannot access business tools. You cannot reach part of your own digital past. You cannot even tell your friends, because the exclusion was immediate, and friends often think you removed yourself.

**And worst of all: most people do not know why they were removed at all.**

In the physical world, such a situation would sound absurd. As if someone were banned, without a court, from entering part of a city, a library, a notice board, a business space, a post office, a marketplace, a neighbourhood group and an office where their documents are stored. As if they were told: maybe you violated something, but we will not clearly tell you what, we will not give you a real defence and we will not allow you to speak to a person.

**In the digital world, this happens with one automated click.**

## The Canadian Example: When a Platform Blocks News for an Entire Country

Canada may be one of the clearest examples of how far the power of digital platforms can go. Since 2023, Meta has blocked news content from Canadian publishers on Facebook and Instagram — not only from large media organizations, but also from smaller local outlets. The trigger was Canada’s Online News Act, through which the state tried to force large digital platforms to negotiate with media organizations over compensation for the value that journalistic content brings to their platforms. Meta did not respond by paying. It responded by removing news from Facebook and Instagram in Canada. In other words, a private platform was able to restrict access to news for an entire country because it did not like the rules that country had adopted.

## The Platform as Judge, Rule-Maker and Enforcer of Punishment

**A court in the real world cannot punish someone however it wants.** Not even a judge can invent a rule, decide alone that you are guilty, determine the punishment and close all paths to appeal. There are laws. There is procedure. There is evidence. There are degrees of guilt. There are mitigating and aggravating circumstances. There is at least a minimal idea that a person has the right to know what they are accused of and the right to say something in their own defence. And that judgments can be different and proportional.

And then we come to social networks. **A platform that operates almost everywhere in the world writes its own rules. It interprets them itself. It decides that you violated them. It removes you itself.** It closes your Facebook, Messenger, Instagram and Business Suite. It takes away your access to messages, contacts, pages, groups, ads and years of work. And then it often offers you an “appeal” that is again processed by an automated system. A few minutes later, the final message arrives: you have been permanently removed.

**That is not just moderation. It is a private digital punishment without a real procedure.** How long can this be tolerated if the world is a global village and everything is moving online?

Of course, platforms must have rules. Nobody claims that social networks should tolerate fraud, violence, threats, identity theft or real abuse. But it is an entirely different matter when an AI system wrongly flags an ordinary user, and then another automated system confirms the punishment without real human review. That is no longer just moderation. **That is digital exclusion.**

## The Right to Information, Not Only the Right to Publish

**It is especially important to understand that this is not only about the right to publish. It is also about the right to access information.** Newspapers will not publish every small local piece of information. They will not carry every post from an association, school, neighbourhood, musician, politician, small shop, trade business, humanitarian campaign or citizen initiative. Many such pieces of information now live precisely on Facebook pages, in groups and on profiles. **If you are permanently removed, that part of the world closes for you.**

The paradox becomes even greater when we see that public institutions also direct citizens to social networks. **The European Parliament, governments, municipalities, cities, schools, politicians and institutions use Facebook, Instagram, X, YouTube and other platforms as communication channels.** Public information is therefore increasingly travelling through private platforms.

But those same private platforms can remove an ordinary citizen through an automated decision, without a clear explanation and without a real human appeal.

The European Union has tried to respond to part of this problem through mechanisms such as **Appeals Centre Europe.** On paper, this sounds almost ideal. A user who believes the platform made a mistake can request an independent review of the decision. But then comes the cold shower. First of all, its decisions are not legally binding. **Appeals Centre Europe itself states in its decisions that its decisions are “non-binding” and that it is up to the platform to decide whether to implement them.** This means that even if you are right, no one — especially not social networks — has to respect that decision. **And it seems that social networks themselves are aware of this, so they can ignore Appeals Centre Europe, fail to respond and provide no further explanation.**

Even when you receive a decision from Appeals Centre Europe saying that you were right, what can you do with it? **Nothing.** You cannot send it to a real person at Facebook because there is often no functional email contact. You cannot force the platform to restore your account. You cannot open Messenger. You cannot get Instagram back. You cannot get Business Suite back. You cannot re-enter your pages and groups. You cannot reach contacts, conversations and the digital life you built over years.

**You have a decision. Maybe even a decision that says you are right. But the door remains locked.** That is the absurdity of the digital age: an appeal system exists, but for the user it often means nothing. It looks like protection. It sounds like protection. But if the decision does not have to be implemented and you have no one to give it to, then this is not a real restoration of rights. **It is only a nicely packaged confirmation of powerlessness.**

**If even convicted persons in a democratic society retain the fundamental right to information, then we must ask how ordinary citizens can be permanently excluded from a digital space without a clear procedure.** This is not about saying that everyone must have the right to break the rules. It is about saying that no one should be digitally excluded and erased without a clear reason, a real appeal and the possibility of human review.

Because when AI decides and a person has no one to speak to, the user is no longer just banned. **They become an online social convict and outcast — without a court.**

## Our View (Metaadvisor)

* **A Facebook ban is no longer only the private problem of one user.** In today’s digital society, such a ban can mean the loss of communication, business tools, contacts, groups, pages, information and access to a space where a large part of everyday public life takes place.
* **The biggest problem is not that platforms have rules. Rules must exist.** The problem is when automated AI moderation makes a decision within minutes that has consequences similar to punishment for the user, but without a clear explanation, without human support and without a real path back.
* **If social networks increasingly function as digital squares, neighbourhoods, business spaces and information notice boards, then the permanent exclusion of a user cannot remain only an internal decision of an algorithm.**
* It is especially problematic that appeal systems often exist more as a formal facade than as a real path of return. **If a user receives a decision saying that the platform was wrong, but has no one to give that decision to and it does not restore the account, then protection exists only on paper.**
* **A human being’s right to information, communication and access to their own digital work should not disappear with one automated click.**
