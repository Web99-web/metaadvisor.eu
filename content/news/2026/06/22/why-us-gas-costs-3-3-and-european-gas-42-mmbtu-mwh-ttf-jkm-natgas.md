# Why US Gas Costs 3.3 and European Gas 42: MMBtu, MWh, TTF, JKM and NATGAS

When people talk about the price of natural gas, it is very easy to get a completely wrong impression if we only look at the raw numbers. US gas is often shown at around **3.3 USD/MMBtu**, while European gas is shown at around **42 EUR/MWh**. At first glance, it looks as if European gas is more than ten times more expensive than US gas. But these two numbers are not speaking the same language.

It is like comparing the price of fuel per gallon with the price of fuel per litre, only with even more complicated math behind it. The number alone is not enough. It is also important to look at the unit written after the price.

{{< support1 >}}

In the United States, the price of natural gas is most often expressed in dollars per million BTU. The unit looks like this: **USD/MMBtu**. BTU means **British thermal unit**, a unit of heat. When we say that US Henry Hub gas is around **3.3 USD/MMBtu**, it means that the market is paying about 3.30 dollars for the amount of gas that can produce one million BTU of thermal energy.

In Europe, gas is more often shown differently. European TTF, one of the most important European gas benchmarks, is usually expressed in euros per megawatt-hour of energy. The unit looks like this: **EUR/MWh**. When we say that European gas is around **42 EUR/MWh**, it means that the market is paying about 42 euros for one megawatt-hour of energy.

That is why **3.3 USD/MMBtu** and **42 EUR/MWh** cannot be compared directly. First, they need to be brought into a similar unit.

Approximately:

**1 MWh = 3.412 MMBtu**

or the other way around:

**1 MMBtu = 0.293 MWh**

The rough formula for converting a European gas price from EUR/MWh into USD/MMBtu looks like this:

<div style="background:#f5f7fa; border-left:4px solid #2563eb; padding:18px 20px; margin:26px 0; border-radius:10px; font-size:1.08em; line-height:1.6;">
  <strong>Formula:</strong><br>
  Price in USD/MMBtu ≈ (price in EUR/MWh ÷ 3.412) × EUR/USD exchange rate
</div>

If we take an example in which European gas is around **42 EUR/MWh**, the calculation looks like this:

**42 ÷ 3.412 ≈ 12.3 EUR/MMBtu**

When those 12.3 euros are converted into dollars, depending on the exchange rate, the result is roughly **13.3 to 13.5 USD/MMBtu**.

This means that the real comparison is not **42 versus 3.3**. When the prices are converted into a similar unit, US gas is around **3.3 USD/MMBtu**, while European gas is around **13.4 USD/MMBtu**.

So European gas is not thirteen times more expensive just because the screen shows 42 instead of 3.3. A large part of that apparent difference comes from different units. But that does not mean there is no difference. After conversion, European gas is still roughly four times more expensive than US gas.

## Why Is European Gas Still More Expensive?

At this point, the difference is no longer mathematical. It is about market structure, geopolitics and logistics.

The United States has enormous domestic natural gas production, especially from shale. The US market has its own production, its own infrastructure and a large domestic supply base. That is why the Henry Hub price is often far lower than the European price.

Europe is in a much more difficult position. After losing a large part of cheaper Russian pipeline gas, Europe relies much more heavily on LNG, or liquefied natural gas. LNG is not just gas that is moved from one place to another. For gas to reach Europe, it must be cooled to a very low temperature, around **-162 °C**, liquefied, loaded onto special LNG tankers, transported across the ocean and then converted back into gas at European terminals. Every one of these steps costs money.

In addition, Europe competes with Asia for LNG. If Asian buyers offer higher prices, part of the LNG cargo can go to Asia. That is why European TTF does not depend only on European consumption, but also on the global LNG market, ships, terminals, weather conditions, storage levels and geopolitical risks.

{{< support2 >}}

That is why it is important to understand two things at the same time. First, US and European gas prices cannot be compared only by looking at the first number, because they use different units. Second, even after the units are converted, European gas remains significantly more expensive than US gas because of market structure, import dependence and the cost of LNG infrastructure.

## Where Is Gas Actually Traded?

Natural gas is traded on commodity exchanges through futures contracts, options and other derivatives. This is not a “token” like in crypto. It is a market for commodities, energy and financial contracts linked to the price of gas.

In the United States, the most followed contract is **Henry Hub Natural Gas Futures**, traded on CME/NYMEX. The basic symbol is **NG**, and on many financial portals it can also be seen as **NG=F**. This is the main US benchmark for natural gas.

In Europe, the most followed benchmark is **Dutch TTF Natural Gas Futures**, traded on ICE Endex. TTF means **Title Transfer Facility**, a Dutch virtual gas trading hub, and today it is the most important European gas benchmark.

For the LNG market, especially in Asia, traders often watch **JKM**, or the **Japan Korea Marker**. This is an important benchmark for liquefied natural gas delivered to the Asian market. JKM is used to track and hedge prices for LNG cargo deliveries.

The simplest way to remember it:

**US gas:** Henry Hub, ticker **NG / NG=F**

**European gas:** Dutch TTF, benchmark **TTF**

**Asian LNG:** Japan Korea Marker, benchmark **JKM**

## What Is NATGAS on Hyperliquid?

Separate from traditional energy exchanges, natural gas can also be followed and traded as a derivative on some crypto/perpetual platforms. On Hyperliquid, the market appears as **NATGAS**, or **xyz:NATGAS / NATGAS-USDC perpetual market**.

It is important to be clear: this is not trading real physical gas, nor is it buying LNG or gas deliveries. It is a futures/perpetual instrument that tracks the price of natural gas. In other words, the user is not buying gas, but speculating on the price movement of gas through a derivative market.

This is also not the same as the ticker **GAS**. GAS is a separate crypto ticker and does not refer to the natural gas market. That is why **NATGAS** and **GAS** should not be mixed up when discussing energy gas.

## Why Does This Matter for Qatar and LNG News?

Qatar does not have one simple “Qatar gas ticker” in the way a stock has a symbol or a crypto token has a ticker. Qatar is one of the major LNG exporters, and the impact of Qatari LNG is seen through global gas benchmarks, especially **JKM** for the Asian LNG market and **TTF** for Europe.

If there is a problem in important LNG infrastructure, the market will not necessarily react through one single “Qatar gas ticker”. The reaction can be seen through LNG prices, TTF, JKM, cargo availability, shipping premiums, winter expectations and the global competition between Europe and Asia.

That is why it is always important to know what exactly we are looking at when discussing gas. Henry Hub does not say the same thing as TTF. TTF does not say the same thing as JKM. And NATGAS on Hyperliquid is not the same thing as traditional exchange-traded futures on CME or ICE. All of these instruments can point in a similar direction, but they do not always tell the same story.

## The Simplest Way to Remember It

US Henry Hub at around **3.3 USD/MMBtu** is roughly equal to **11 USD/MWh**.

European TTF at around **42 EUR/MWh** is roughly equal to **12.3 EUR/MMBtu**, or about **13.3 to 13.5 USD/MMBtu**, depending on the exchange rate.

That is why, with gas, we always need to look at what is written after the price. **USD/MMBtu** and **EUR/MWh** are not the same thing. If that is ignored, the market can look much more dramatic, or much simpler, than it really is.

## Our View (Metaadvisor)

* **Natural gas does not have one single global price.** US Henry Hub, European TTF and Asian LNG benchmarks often tell different stories.

* **Numbers should not be read without units.** When we see US gas around **3.3** and European gas around **42**, the first reaction may be that Europe is in an incomparably worse position. But only when we look at the units do we see the real picture.

* **The difference is not literally 42 versus 3.3.** After conversion, European gas is roughly four times more expensive than US gas. That is still a large difference, but not the same difference that appears when only the raw numbers are compared.

* **The reason is not only math, but the market.** Europe lost part of its cheaper pipeline supply, depends more on LNG, pays for transport, liquefaction, regasification and competes with other major buyers.

* **With tickers, context matters.** **NG** is the US Henry Hub future, **TTF** is the European benchmark, and **JKM** is the LNG benchmark for Asia.

* **NATGAS on Hyperliquid is a separate story.** It is a perpetual/futures derivative that tracks the price of natural gas, but it is not trading real physical gas.

* **GAS is not natural gas.** It is a separate crypto ticker and should not be confused with the natural gas market.
