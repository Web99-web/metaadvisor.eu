# Google tests 5 GB for new accounts: is the free internet becoming less free?

For years, we got used to one simple sentence: a Google account comes with 15 GB of free storage shared between Gmail, Google Drive and Google Photos. For many users, that was enough for emails, documents, images, attachments and basic backup. There was not much to think about. You opened a Google account and received storage.

But according to newer reports, Google is testing a different model for some new accounts in selected regions. Instead of automatically receiving 15 GB, a new user may start with only 5 GB of storage, while the full amount up to 15 GB is unlocked only after adding a phone number. Google presents this as a test and as a way to improve account security and access recovery.

## It is not only about 5 GB

At first glance, this looks like a small change in cloud storage. Five gigabytes or fifteen gigabytes. But the issue is bigger than the number itself. Gmail is not just email. A Google account today is the entry point to Google Drive, Google Photos, Android, YouTube, Google Docs, Google Calendar, Chrome sync and many other services people use every day.

If more free storage starts to depend on a phone number, the question is no longer only how much storage the user receives. The question is what the user must give in order to receive something that used to be standard for years. A phone number is not an ordinary data point. It connects an online account with a real-world identity, a device, a country, a mobile operator and the possibility of account recovery.

Google can argue that this is about security. And that is not without reason. A phone number can help with account recovery, protect against mass fake account creation and reduce abuse. But for the user, another question remains: when does a security measure become an additional condition for accessing a “free” service?

## Free no longer means without a price

A large part of the internet was built on the idea of free services. Free email. Free cloud. Free photos. Free documents. Free social networks. But more and more often we see that “free” does not mean there is no price. The price can be a data point, identity, phone number, user behavior, advertising profile or deeper dependence on one ecosystem.

Google is not the only example. The entire digital world is moving in this direction. Platforms want to know more about users, reduce anonymity, limit abuse and connect accounts more strongly with real people. That can have a security logic, but it also changes the relationship between the user and the platform.

Before, a user could say: “I will open an email account and use it.” Today, a new version appears more and more often: “I will open an account, but to get the full capacity, I need to provide one more identification point.”

## The Google account has become digital infrastructure

For many people, a Google account is no longer just private email. It is digital infrastructure. Through it, people store or manage documents, photos, business files, family archives, school materials, invoices, contracts, passwords, logins for other services and access to an Android phone.

That is why every change around the Google account has a much greater meaning than a technical setting. If a user loses access to a Google account, they can lose much more than an inbox. They can lose access to documents, photos, backups, a YouTube channel, apps, business files and other accounts connected to the Gmail address.

In that context, the 5 GB and phone verification test shows how important the Google account has become. It is no longer only a service the user uses. It is a central point of digital identity.

## Security or additional identification?

Google’s argument will probably be security. A phone number can reduce abuse, make mass account creation harder, help with password recovery and protect the user if someone tries to take over an account. That is a real and understandable side of the story.

But there is another side too. When a platform says that you will get the better version of a free service if you add a phone number, the user is effectively entering an exchange. More storage for more personal data. More functionality for stronger identification. More security, but also less anonymity.

That does not automatically mean the change is bad. But it does mean that users should understand what is happening. The internet is slowly moving away from the old idea of anonymous or semi-anonymous accounts and toward accounts that are increasingly tied to a real person.

## Why this is not a small story

Some will say this is not important because most people add a phone number to their Google account anyway. Others will say that 5 GB is enough to start. Others will say it is normal for Google to want to prevent abuse. All of that may be true.

But the symbolism matters. If one of the world’s largest digital companies is testing a model in which full free storage is no longer automatically available without additional identification, it shows where the internet is moving. Free services increasingly come with conditions. And those conditions are not only about storage, but about identity, data and access control.

For individual users, this may look like a small detail. For the wider digital picture, it is a sign that the boundary is moving. What used to be standard can become conditional tomorrow.

## What users can learn from this

The first lesson is simple: do not keep everything in one account. Gmail, Drive and Photos are practical, but if all important documents, images and business data are tied to one Google account, the user takes on a major risk. An additional backup is not a luxury. It is basic digital hygiene.

The second lesson is that users should read changes in digital service terms more carefully. When storage, verification or account recovery rules change, it can have real consequences.

The third lesson is that “free” should be read differently. Free can mean that you do not pay with money, but you pay with data, habits, attention or deeper dependence on one ecosystem.
