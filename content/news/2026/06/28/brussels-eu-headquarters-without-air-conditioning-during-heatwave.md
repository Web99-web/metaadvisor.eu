# Brussels: EU Headquarters Without Air Conditioning During Heatwave, Cooling Shut Off From Floors 1 to 7

**The European Commission headquarters in Brussels, Belgium, had to forcibly shut down part of its cooling system on Friday during a heatwave.** According to reports by Politico, E&E News and Euractiv, employees in the Berlaymont building received a message saying that, due to extreme weather conditions, **cooling would be shut off from floors 1 to 7 until the end of the day**.

The news is striking precisely because it happened at the moment when cooling was most needed. Belgium recorded its hottest day of 2026 on Friday, with **35.3 °C (95.5 °F)** at the official weather station in Uccle. In Brussels, street temperature displays showed around **37 °C (98.6 °F)**, while local highs in Belgium in recent days reached or approached **38 °C (100.4 °F)**. Across wider Europe, the heatwave brought temperatures **above 40 °C (104 °F)**, with some countries setting new temperature records.

In the internal message to employees, as quoted by media reports, the shutdown was described as a **“forced shutdown of the air cooling system” due to extreme weather conditions**. The exact technical reason has not been publicly explained in detail. Available reports do not specify whether electricity demand, fire risk or another technical fault was the cause. They refer only to a general strain connected with the heat.

The Berlaymont is one of the best-known buildings of the European Union. It houses the **headquarters of the European Commission**, the institution that proposes a large share of EU legislation and is involved daily in shaping European policy. That is why the news that part of the building was left without cooling during extreme heat carries broader symbolic weight.

{{< support1 >}}

According to Anadolu Agency, citing a Politico report, **the office of European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen on the 13th floor was not affected by the cooling shutdown**. The same report said that most offices of EU commissioners, located on the **8th floor and above**, were also not affected by the same shutdown, according to media coverage of the incident.

## Heatwave as a Test for Europe’s Infrastructure

At first glance, this may look like an ordinary office inconvenience. But it fits into a much wider picture: **Europe is increasingly facing heatwaves that put pressure on buildings, workplaces, transport, energy systems and healthcare**. When cooling has to be shut off inside the headquarters of one of Europe’s most important institutions, the question is no longer only how hot it is outside, but how ready the infrastructure is for such conditions.

This is where the paradox appears. **Cooling is not crucial when it is a comfortable 22 °C (71.6 °F) outside. It becomes crucial when temperatures climb toward 35, 37 or 40 °C.** That is what makes the Brussels case so striking: the cooling system was not shut off on an ordinary summer day, but **in the middle of a heatwave that was already putting people, buildings and urban infrastructure under stress**.

Heatwaves are no longer just weather stories. They directly affect **working conditions, productivity and human safety**. Offices, schools, hospitals and public institutions increasingly have to adapt to days when standard cooling systems may not be enough or may not function under the expected load.

Europe built much of its infrastructure for a cooler climate. In many countries, air conditioning was not as standard as in hotter parts of the world. But **more frequent and more intense heatwaves are changing that calculation**. Cooling is no longer only about comfort. It is increasingly about health, business continuity and building resilience.

## It Is Not Only About Air Conditioning, but Adaptation

The Berlaymont case shows how important **adaptation to heat** will become for Europe. On one hand, the need for cooling is growing so people can work and live normally. On the other hand, greater use of air conditioning increases energy consumption and adds pressure to systems that are already under strain.

This creates a new challenge for cities and institutions. **It is not enough simply to install more air conditioners.** Buildings will have to be better designed, renovated and cooled in a way that is energy efficient, but also reliable enough during extreme weather conditions. That includes insulation, shading, ventilation, smart energy management and systems that can withstand hotter summers.

In that sense, the news from Brussels is not just a story about one day without cooling. It is a reminder that **climate adaptation is not an abstract policy issue, but a very concrete question of working conditions, buildings and daily life**.
