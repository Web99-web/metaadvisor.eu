# EU wants to tax Big Tech, crypto and online gambling: but who controls platform power?

**The EU wants to tax Big Tech: but who protects people and small businesses when platforms shut them down overnight?**

The European Union is looking for new sources of revenue and is increasingly turning toward major digital sectors. In discussions about the future European budget, new levies on Big Tech, crypto and online gambling are being considered. At first glance, the logic is clear: if global platforms and digital industries earn huge amounts of money in the European market, Europe wants part of that money for common priorities.

The estimates involve serious money. **New levies on crypto, digital giants and online gambling could, according to European estimates, generate around €68 billion in the 2028–2034 period**, or almost €11 billion per year. That is why it is not surprising that the EU is looking more closely at Big Tech, crypto and online betting in the budget debate.

But this is where the more important question begins. **If Big Tech is large enough for the EU to see it as a source of public revenue, why do we still treat these platforms like ordinary private apps when they shut down the account of a person or a small business?**

## Platforms are no longer just apps

Facebook, Instagram, Google, YouTube, WhatsApp, advertising accounts, marketplace platforms and AI tools are no longer just “apps” for many people. They are a workspace, a sales channel, a contact list, a reputation system and a communication line with customers. Many people do not start a business by immediately opening a large company, hiring a team and buying expensive advertising. They start with small steps: an Instagram profile, a Facebook page, a YouTube channel, a TikTok account, an ad for a local service or a small campaign for their own business.

Influencers, micro-entrepreneurs, small tourism hosts, local salons, web shops, freelancers and small companies often test their business first on platforms. That is where they find an audience, customers and their first orders. For them, the platform is not entertainment. It is the beginning of a business.

## When something becomes a “utility”, a profile should not be shut down in one second

The English word “utility” can be understood as basic infrastructure or a service of public importance. These are things like electricity, water, telephone service, internet access or a bank account. Nobody would consider it normal if a person’s electricity, water or access to a bank were cut off in one second, without a clear reason and without a real appeal process.

But in the digital world, that often happens. With **Meta, the problem is not only one profile. If a user’s Facebook** or **Instagram** account is disabled, access to **Messenger**, connected pages, groups, the advertising account and **Business Suite** often disappears with it. For an ordinary user, that can mean an automatic loss of contacts and communication. For a small business, it can mean losing the market.

**Did you feel that brief shock when Facebook and Instagram went down on 12 June?** When you cannot log in, when you do not know whether the problem is on your side, whether you have been hacked or whether something bigger has happened? For most users, that feeling lasted only briefly. But this is exactly what people experience when automated moderation throws them out of the system — only permanently. The account disappears, pages disappear, Messenger no longer works, Business Suite is no longer accessible, and the user receives a generic message with almost no real path to human support.

If a platform has become digital infrastructure of public importance, it should not have the power to remove a person or a small business from the digital market in one second without human review, a clear explanation and a real possibility to appeal.

## The EU must not look only at revenue

Of course, the EU has the right to discuss whether Big Tech, crypto and online gambling should contribute more to public revenue. Large platforms earn money from European users, European attention, European advertisers and European data. The crypto sector and online gambling are also parts of the digital economy that are increasingly difficult to ignore.

The problem starts when the entire debate is reduced only to the question of how much money can be collected. If the EU sees digital platforms as a source of revenue, it must also see them as a source of power. Because that power is not abstract. It decides who can communicate, who can advertise, who can sell and who is allowed to exist online at all.

## Appeals Centre Europe is not enough if decisions have no real force

Europe has tried, through the **Digital Services Act**, to introduce more responsibility for large online platforms. There are also mechanisms such as **Appeals Centre Europe**, which are supposed to help users in disputes with platforms. On paper, this sounds like serious progress. In practice, for many users, the key question remains the same: **the decision exists only on paper, and the user cannot deliver it to anyone afterwards — because they no longer have access to the platform. What is a decision worth if the platform does not actually have to restore the account?**

If a user receives a decision showing that the platform may have made a mistake, but still cannot recover their Facebook or Instagram profile, nor access to Messenger, connected pages, groups, the advertising account and Business Suite linked to the same account, then the problem is not solved. This is especially painful for small businesses. They do not need a symbolic confirmation that a mistake may have happened. They need real access restored — to the profile, pages, contacts, ads and business visibility.

## Small businesses do not have legal teams like large corporations

Large companies have lawyers, agencies, internal contacts and budgets. If they run into a problem with a platform, they may be able to solve it through an account manager or legal pressure. A small user usually has none of that.

A small business or individual may have only one audience built over years. If that disappears overnight, it is not just a digital inconvenience. It can mean the loss of clients, revenue, contacts and trust.

That is why Big Tech must not be treated only as an ATM for the EU budget. If it has become digital infrastructure of public importance, then it must also have infrastructure-like obligations: clear rules, human review, the right to a real appeal, deadlines, accountability and the possibility of legal protection before someone’s digital life or business disappears overnight.

## Digital dependency is becoming a business risk

This issue is not only about social networks. Today it may be Facebook or Instagram. Tomorrow it may be an AI tool, a cloud platform, a payment processor, a marketplace, a Google profile or an advertising account. More and more people work, sell, learn and communicate through systems they do not control.

A tool that exists today can disappear tomorrow. Not because the user did not pay. Not because the user necessarily did something wrong. But because an automated system, a platform, a regulatory decision or an internal policy changed the rules of the game.

If the EU wants to speak seriously about digital sovereignty, then it must also protect small users of the digital economy — not only seek revenue from the largest players.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

* The EU has the right to discuss new revenue from Big Tech, crypto and online gambling, but that debate must not remain only a story about money.
* If large digital platforms have become basic infrastructure for work, communication and business, then they must also carry infrastructure-like responsibility.
* The weakest point of the digital market is not large companies, but ordinary people and small businesses that start on platforms, build an audience for years and can then disappear from the system in one second.
* **Appeals Centre Europe** and similar mechanisms can be a beginning, but they are not enough if decisions have no real force and the user still ends up without an account, pages and market access.
* If Big Tech is large enough to fill the EU budget, then Big Tech is also large enough to bear responsibility when its decisions or automated systems destroy the digital work of small users.
