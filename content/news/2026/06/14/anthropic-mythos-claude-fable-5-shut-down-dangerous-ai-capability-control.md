# Anthropic Mythos and Claude Fable 5 shut down after only a few days: can dangerous AI capability really be controlled?

Only a few days after Anthropic introduced Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5, two of its most advanced artificial intelligence models, something happened that may mark a new phase in the relationship between the AI industry, governments and users. The US government issued a directive requiring access to these models to be suspended for foreign nationals, citing national security.

In practice, the consequence was even more dramatic. Anthropic said it had to abruptly disable access to the Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models for all users in order to comply with the directive, because it could not reliably verify every user’s citizenship status in such a short time. In other words, models that had been presented only days earlier as a major technological leap suddenly became an example of how quickly the most advanced AI can turn from a product into a security issue.

## A model meant to show a new level of AI capability

Claude Fable 5 was introduced as one of the most advanced models Anthropic had made available for broader use. According to the company’s description, the model was supposed to be especially strong in long and complex tasks, programming, document analysis, visual understanding, scientific work and autonomous problem-solving. This is no longer the level of an ordinary chatbot answering short questions. It is the level of a tool that can support work almost like a highly advanced digital colleague.

Claude Mythos 5 was the even more sensitive part of the story. Anthropic described it as a model intended for a limited group of vetted partners, especially in the field of cybersecurity and critical infrastructure protection. In essence, Mythos 5 was supposed to have similar or the same core capability as Fable 5, but with a different approach to safety restrictions in certain areas. That is why, from the beginning, it was clear that this was not just “another AI model”, but a technology that could carry serious security implications.

## Why did the US government react?

According to available information, the US government cited national security and export-control rules as the reason. Export controls are rules by which a state restricts the export of, or access to, certain sensitive technology, especially if that technology could help foreign states, military structures, cyber attackers or other risky actors. Until now, such measures were most often associated with chips, advanced computing systems, military components or specialised software. Now it is becoming increasingly clear that AI models themselves are entering the same category.

One of the concerns mentioned is a possible jailbreak. In the AI world, a jailbreak means an attempt to make a model bypass its safety rules and answer something it would normally refuse. If a model has very strong capabilities in programming, software vulnerability analysis or understanding complex systems, a jailbreak is no longer just an uncomfortable reputational problem for the company. It can become a security risk.

## Fable 5 as a useful tool and a potential risk

This is where it becomes important to understand why the story is so sensitive. The same AI model that can help developers find bugs in code, support cybersecurity experts in patching vulnerabilities or help researchers accelerate scientific work can, theoretically, also help attackers. The difference is not always in the technology itself, but in the user’s intention. This is the old problem of dual-use technology, meaning technology that can have both civilian and harmful applications.

That is why the question of control is so difficult. If a model can analyse code, it can help with defence, but also with attack. If it can reason through complex technical steps, it can support useful software development, but also help bypass protections. If it can work autonomously through longer tasks, that is excellent for productivity, but it also raises the question of how far such a tool should be allowed to go without human oversight.

## Can dangerous AI capability really be locked inside safe boundaries?

Anthropic is one of the companies that has spent years presenting itself as especially cautious when it comes to AI safety. That is exactly why this case is even more interesting. If even a company that speaks so much about safety ends up in a situation where the government restricts its most advanced models overnight, the question becomes whether the industry can really credibly control what it is building on its own.

On one hand, state intervention can appear logical. If the most advanced models reach a level where they can help discover software vulnerabilities, support cyber operations or assist in the development of sensitive technical processes, governments will not simply stand aside and watch. This is especially true for the United States, because a large part of global AI infrastructure, leading companies, cloud systems and models falls under US jurisdiction.

On the other hand, such a move also raises a serious transparency question. How much does the public know about the actual risk? Was the problem concrete and proven, or was it preventive? Was this a genuine security necessity, or political control over the most important technology of the future? When a model is shut down globally, the consequences are not felt only by large companies. They also affect developers, researchers, startups, small businesses and users who may already have started building workflows around that tool.

## AI is no longer just a product, but infrastructure of power

This case shows that the most advanced AI models are no longer just commercial products. They are becoming infrastructure of power. Whoever controls the models controls access to knowledge, automation, analysis, programming, research and an increasingly large part of digital work. That is why the question “who is allowed to use AI?” is no longer just a business question. It is becoming a geopolitical question.

For Europe and the rest of the world, this is an especially important signal. If the US government can order access restrictions on models used by people and companies outside the United States, then the question of digital sovereignty opens again. Europe often talks about regulation, data protection and rules for large platforms, but here we see something even deeper: the most powerful tools may not always be available to everyone, even when they are formally offered as commercial products.

## Today the user has a tool, tomorrow perhaps nothing

The most important lesson of this story is not only that Anthropic had to shut down Fable 5 and Mythos 5. The most important lesson is that users are increasingly dependent on global systems over which they have no real control. Today it can be an AI tool. Tomorrow a cloud platform. The day after tomorrow a social network, an advertising account, a payment processor or an entire digital identity.

A tool that exists today can disappear tomorrow. Not because the user did not pay. Not because the user necessarily broke the rules. Not because the user did something wrong. But because a company, a government or an automated system decided that access is no longer allowed, safe or commercially acceptable.

In the case of Anthropic’s models, the reason is national security. In the case of social platforms, the reason can be automated moderation. In the case of advertising, it can be suspicious activity that the user never manages to explain to a real person. In the case of AI tools, it can be an export-control directive. The mechanism is different, but the feeling of powerlessness for the user is often the same: the system closes, and the person or small business remains outside.

## This is the beginning of a new phase of AI regulation

Until now, much of the discussion about AI regulation has focused on safety standards, ethical frameworks, copyright, privacy and liability for harm. But the case of Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 shows that we are entering a new phase: regulation of access to capability itself. It is no longer only about what a model is allowed to say. It is about who is allowed to use it at all.

If this model continues to spread, we could enter a world in which advanced AI is not equally available to everyone. Some users may receive the strongest models, others restricted versions, and others perhaps nothing at all. That could further widen the gap between states, companies and individuals with access to the most modern tools and those who depend on what others allow them to use.

For Metaadvisor.eu, this is also why the Anthropic case is not just a short technology-news item. It is a warning about how fragile access to digital infrastructure can become once powerful tools are controlled by a small number of companies and governments. The same issue that appears today in AI may tomorrow appear in finance, social media, payments, advertising or cloud infrastructure.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

* This case shows that frontier AI models are no longer just applications or digital tools, but strategic technology that can trigger a government reaction almost overnight.

* Anthropic has spent years building a reputation as a company that takes AI safety seriously. Yet even that was apparently not enough to avoid an urgent intervention by the US government.

* The biggest problem is not only the shutdown of the models, but the uncertainty for users. If access can be interrupted abruptly and globally, every business that depends on a single AI tool becomes vulnerable.

* For Europe, this is another argument that digital sovereignty is not an empty political phrase. If the most important AI tools come from the United States, decisions about access to them can also come from the United States.

* The open question remains: can dangerous AI capability really be controlled with safety rules, or will the most advanced models increasingly end up under political and state control?
