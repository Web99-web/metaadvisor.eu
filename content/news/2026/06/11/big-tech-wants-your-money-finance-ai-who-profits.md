# Big Tech wants your money to finance AI. But who really profits if you invest?

Artificial intelligence is no longer only a story about new tools, chatbots and productivity. It is increasingly becoming a story about money, debt, stocks, data centers, chips, energy and investors who are expected to believe that enormous spending will one day pay off. Big Tech has spent months repeating that AI is the future. But the question that now matters more and more is simple: who is financing that future — and who will profit most from it in the end?

The title may sound harsh, but it increasingly matches market reality. Companies building AI infrastructure need huge amounts of capital. They need new servers, new data centers, more energy, more chips, more network infrastructure and more capacity to process data. This is not a cheap software upgrade. It is the industrial construction of a new digital infrastructure, and that construction requires money. The money comes from company profits, debt, new share issuance, funds and, in the end, often from investors who buy the story of future growth.

## Oracle: from 6 AM layoffs to the question of who pays for AI

Oracle is a good example of how quickly the AI story can change. In late March and early April, we wrote about large layoffs at Oracle, where some employees reportedly learned that they had lost their jobs through an email sent at 6 AM. Many initially thought it was an April Fool’s joke because the timing seemed almost unbelievable. But the message was real: a job can disappear in seconds, while the company redirects resources toward a new AI phase. We covered that earlier here: [Oracle fired 20,000 people in a 6 AM email: jobs vanish in seconds](https://metaadvisor.eu/news/oracle-fired-20000-email-6am/)

At the time, such cost-cutting could be read as a signal of efficiency. Fewer employees, higher productivity, more money for data centers, chips and cloud infrastructure. Investors often like these stories because they see lower costs and preparation for growth. According to the numbers we followed, Oracle’s stock rose sharply from the beginning of April to the beginning of June, moving from around 147 USD to about 248 USD. That was a huge increase in a very short period.

## Why did the Oracle story turn?

Now the other side of the same story is visible. When AI investments become so large that the market starts talking about massive capex, debt and additional financing, investors stop looking only at optimism. They start asking who will pay for all of it. Oracle has since fallen to around 184 USD. That is still above its early-April level, but significantly below its recent high. In other words, investors have not completely rejected the AI story, but they no longer want to blindly pay for every cost item simply because it is placed under the label of “AI future”.

That is the key change. The first phase of AI euphoria was simple: if a company had AI, the stock went up. The second phase is much colder: if a company has AI, it must show how much it costs, how it will be financed and when that money will come back. Oracle now shows that the market can first reward cost-cutting and AI optimism, and later punish the same company when it becomes clear that AI infrastructure requires enormous amounts of money.

## Meta: expensive AI and the question of new capital

We saw similar nervousness with Meta. Meta is also one of the companies investing heavily in AI, but investors are watching the price of that strategy more carefully. When reports appeared that the company was considering a major capital raise to finance AI infrastructure, the stock fell. We wrote about that earlier here: [Meta stock falls: expensive AI and lost users through AI moderation](https://metaadvisor.eu/news/meta-stock-falls-expensive-ai-lost-users-ai-moderation/)


In that article, we noted that META had fallen from around 629 USD to about 583 USD. That showed how sensitive investors had become to any news that raises questions about the real cost of artificial intelligence. Today, Meta is still trading below those earlier levels, around 568 USD. That does not mean the market does not believe in Meta or in AI. But it does mean that investors are no longer looking only at potential. They are also looking at the bill.

## Who really earns from the AI wave?

If a company wants to build huge AI data centers, if it needs more energy, more chips and more infrastructure, someone has to finance that transition. And whenever debt, new shares or a major capital raise are mentioned, investors immediately ask whether their ownership will be diluted and when the real return will become visible.

That is why the question “who profits” is not simple. In the short term, the clearest winners of the AI race are often not necessarily the companies using AI most visibly in their products, but those selling the infrastructure without which AI cannot function. These include chipmakers, networking equipment suppliers, data-center companies, energy infrastructure providers, cloud suppliers and everyone selling the “picks and shovels” of the new AI gold rush. If more data centers are built, someone has to sell the chips, servers, cooling systems, space and energy.

## The problem for investors: are they buying a winner or financing the cost?

The problem for investors appears when they buy a company that is not selling the shovels, but must buy those shovels at ever higher prices. If a company spends tens or hundreds of billions on AI infrastructure, while it is still unclear how much profit it will generate from that spending, the investor is effectively financing a transition period. Maybe the profit will come. Maybe it will be enormous. But maybe part of the promise will turn out to be too expensive, too slow or too optimistic.

That is the difference between investing in a real winner of the AI wave and investing in a company that is simply paying an increasingly expensive entry fee into the race. When interest rates, debt, share issuance and capex are part of the same story, investors should not look only at the word “AI” in the headline. They should look at the balance sheet, cash flow, debt, costs and the real possibility that investments will turn into profit.

## SpaceX IPO: the biggest test of market appetite for the future

Tomorrow, the SpaceX IPO fits directly into this story. It is an especially important moment because it comes at a time when the market is showing both hunger for major technology stories and fear of inflated valuations. SpaceX is not only a story about rockets. It is a story about Starlink, space infrastructure, communications, possible future AI data systems, Elon Musk and the belief that the next major infrastructure layer may be built beyond the traditional boundaries of the internet.

According to figures now being cited in financial media, the SpaceX IPO is expected at around 135 USD per share. The plan is reportedly to raise around 75 billion USD by selling roughly 555.6 million shares, with a target valuation of around 1.75 to 1.78 trillion USD. That would make it the largest IPO in history and immediately place SpaceX among the most valuable publicly listed companies.

## The 135 USD price and the question of fair value

That is exactly why SpaceX is such a good example of the question in the title. If the IPO starts at around 135 USD per share, while Morningstar estimates fair value closer to 63 USD per share and total company value around 780 billion USD, then the question is not only whether the stock jumps on the first trading day. The question is how much investors are willing to pay for the future before that future becomes stable profit.

More optimistic analysts see a very different picture. Some price targets are far above the IPO price, which shows how divided expectations are. Some investors look at Starlink, launches, space infrastructure and AI as a combination that could open huge new markets. Others warn that the valuation is already so high that it leaves very little room for mistakes.

## Retail investors and the danger of major IPO enthusiasm

It is especially important that retail investors are likely to be strongly attracted to the SpaceX IPO. When Elon Musk, space, Starlink, AI and the largest IPO come together, market psychology can become dangerous. People do not want to miss the “next big train”. But the history of major IPOs often shows that a famous name does not automatically mean the best entry point. A major IPO can be an opportunity, but it can also be a moment when early investors exit and small investors enter at a very high price.

Some reports warn that the share of individual investors in this offering could be unusually large compared with typical IPOs. That can increase interest and create a strong first trading day, but it can also increase risk if the price later falls. If the IPO happens at 135 USD and part of the analyst community believes fair value is much lower, then a small investor needs to understand that they are not buying only a stock. They are also buying a very expensive story about the future.

## Oracle, Meta and SpaceX tell the same bigger story

That is why Oracle, Meta and SpaceX together tell the same broader story. Oracle shows that layoffs and AI optimism can push a stock up in the short term, while AI capex and debt can later frighten the market. Meta shows that even the largest platforms must explain how they will finance AI infrastructure. SpaceX will tomorrow test how much investors are still willing to pay for a massive technology vision, even when valuations are extremely high.

For small investors, the most important question is not only “which stock will rise tomorrow”. A much more important question is: where is the real profit model, who is paying for the buildout and who is best positioned to extract money from the whole story? If a company has to raise debt, issue shares or cut staff to finance AI infrastructure, that is not automatically bad. But it is not automatically good either. It means the investor has to understand the risk, not just the headline.

## The AI future is not built for free

AI may be the future, but the future is not built for free. Someone has to pay for the chips. Someone has to pay for the data centers. Someone has to pay for the energy. Someone has to pay for developers, security, networks and maintenance. If companies cannot finance that transition from existing profits, the money will come from the market. And when money comes from the market, investors become part of financing that future.

That is why the title “Big Tech wants your money to finance AI” is not just provocation. It asks the right question: if you invest in this story, are you the owner of a future winner — or are you simply one of those helping to pay for the most expensive infrastructure of the new digital era?

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

* The AI investment story is entering a more serious phase. It is no longer enough to say that a company is investing in artificial intelligence.
* Investors now want to know how much AI costs, who is financing the costs, whether new shares will be issued, how much debt is rising and when the investment will turn into real profit.
* Oracle shows how the market can first reward AI optimism and cost-cutting, then punish a company when the price of infrastructure becomes visible.
* Meta shows that even the largest platforms are not immune to concerns about expensive AI and possible capital raising.
* SpaceX brings a new test tomorrow: will investors once again buy a huge future story at a very high price?
* An IPO price of around 135 USD per share and a valuation of around 1.75 to 1.78 trillion USD show how much the market is willing to pay for expectations.
* Morningstar’s estimate of around 63 USD per share and 780 billion USD in company value shows how far fair-value views can differ.
* The biggest short-term winners of the AI wave may be those selling infrastructure.
* The biggest risk may sit with those who must spend enormous sums before proving the return.
* Small investors need to be especially careful not to buy only hype, but to understand who really earns, who takes on debt and who ultimately pays the bill.
