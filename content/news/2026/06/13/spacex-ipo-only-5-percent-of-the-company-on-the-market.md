# SpaceX put only 5% of the company on the market: why not everyone got the shares they wanted

The SpaceX IPO looked like an event that retail investors could finally access. After years in which SpaceX had been closed to most retail investors, the public offering created the impression that a door was opening to something long reserved for institutions, large funds and selected private investors.

But behind the huge hype stood a very simple piece of math: only a small part of the company was brought to the market. According to available reports, SpaceX sold about 75 billion dollars worth of shares in the IPO, which represented roughly **5% of the company** at the IPO price. After the stock rose on its first trading day, the market value moved close to or above the 2 trillion dollar mark, but the number of shares released in the IPO remained limited.

## The biggest IPO does not mean there are enough shares for everyone

When people hear that SpaceX raised about 75 billion dollars, it sounds like an enormous amount of available shares. And it is enormous. But at the IPO price of 135 dollars per share, the company was valued at around 1.75 to 1.77 trillion dollars. After the first-day stock rally, the market value approached or passed **2 trillion dollars**. In both cases, those 75 billion dollars still represented only a small part of the total ownership structure.

{{< support1 >}}

That is the key to understanding the whole story. Investors were not buying “SpaceX” as if the entire company had suddenly become available. They were buying only the part that was placed into the public offering. That small part had to be divided among institutional investors, large funds, private clients, brokers, retail channels and new platforms trying to offer tokenized or derivative access.

That is why what happened was almost inevitable: many investors wanted more than they could get.

## Retail received an unusually large share, but it still was not enough

One of the more interesting details of the SpaceX IPO was that retail investors received an unusually large part of the offering. According to reports, individual investors received about 20% of the IPO shares, far more than the usual 5% to 7% seen in many large IPOs.

At first glance, that sounds like a major win for small investors. And partly, it was. If retail investors are often pushed to the margins of major IPOs, SpaceX clearly tried to give individual investors a bigger role. That also fits the broader Musk/Tesla culture, where small investors often have a strong emotional relationship with the company and its founder.

But this is where the paradox begins: even when retail investors receive an unusually large part of the IPO, it does not mean that every retail investor gets the quantity they want. If the entire IPO represents only about 5% of the company, and retail receives about 20% of that package, retail is still receiving a part of a small part. Demand was so large that even those quantities quickly became too small.

{{< support1 >}}

## Why could someone request 100 shares and receive only a few?

In popular IPOs, allocation usually does not work like a normal purchase in a store. A user does not simply say “I want 100 shares” and automatically receive them. They submit interest or an order, and the broker or platform later decides how much will actually be allocated.

If there are enough shares, the user can receive everything they requested. If there are not enough shares, they receive only part of the request. In very popular IPOs, that can be a small portion of the desired amount. Someone can request 100 shares and receive 10. Someone can request 50 and receive 5. Someone can also receive no allocation at all.

That does not necessarily mean the platform deceived the user. It often simply means demand was larger than supply. But for the user, the feeling can be similar: they thought they were participating in a historic IPO, and in the end they received much less than expected.

## 5% of the company, 20% retail and one big misunderstanding

It is important to separate several numbers that can easily get mixed together. First, about 5% of the company was offered in the IPO. That is the portion of SpaceX that actually became available to the market.

Second, about 20% of the IPO shares went to retail investors. That is not 20% of the entire company, but 20% of the 5% that was offered.

{{< support2 >}}

Third, individual users may have received only 5% or 10% of the quantity they requested. Again, that is not the same as 5% of the company or 20% of the IPO. It is only their personal allocation result.

That is where the confusion begins. Someone hears “5%”, someone hears “20%”, someone hears “I only got 10% of my order”, and everything starts to blend into one story. But the real picture is simple: supply was limited, and demand was enormous.

## Why does this matter for tokenized platforms?

This story also explains why tokenized platforms had problems. If even traditional retail channels could not fully satisfy demand, it was even harder for platforms trying to offer tokenized access.

A tokenized share cannot appear out of nowhere if it is supposed to be backed by a real share in the background. If xStocks or another intermediary cannot secure enough underlying shares, then Bybit, Binance Wallet, Bitget or Kraken cannot deliver what users expected either.

{{< support2 >}}

That is exactly why the 5% story matters. It is not just a detail for financial analysts. It explains the entire chain of problems: from brokers and retail investors to wallets, tokenized products and crypto platforms.

## Huge demand does not always mean healthy distribution

SpaceX had all the elements of a perfect IPO hype story: a globally known name, Elon Musk, rockets, Starlink, Mars, an AI narrative, a massive valuation and the feeling that this was “one of those opportunities” investors did not want to miss.

But when the hype is that large, the market often does not behave like a straight and calm road. It behaves more like a crowd at a stadium entrance. Everyone has a ticket in mind, but there are not enough real seats. Some get in, some get in only partially, and some stay outside and have to buy later at a higher market price.

That is frustrating, but it is not unusual. In highly demanded IPOs, allocation is always a sensitive issue. SpaceX is only an extreme example because the company is huge, interest was global and the available share was relatively small.

## What should small investors understand?

The most important point is that “I have access to the IPO” does not mean the same thing as “I will get all the shares I want”. Access means the user can register, show interest or reserve funds. Allocation is the second step. Only when the platform confirms how much the user actually received can we talk about a real quantity.

That applies to traditional brokers, and even more to new models such as tokenized IPO products. There, the user must understand one additional question: is the product a real share, a tokenized representation, a derivative or only a request for possible allocation?

SpaceX showed that this exact difference can create the biggest disappointment.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

At first glance, the SpaceX IPO looked like a major win for retail investors. Small investors received an unusually large part of the offering compared with many traditional IPOs.

But at the same time, it showed the limit of that democratization. When only about 5% of a company comes to the market and demand is global, even a large retail package is not enough for everyone to receive what they want.

That is why this story is not only about SpaceX. It is a lesson about allocation, expectations and the difference between “I have IPO access” and “I will receive the quantity I requested”.

Retail can get broader access to major IPOs. But broader access is not the same as unlimited access.
