# Oil Surprisingly Low: WTI Around 71, Brent Heading Toward 74 — While Gas Still Shows Market Fragility

Oil prices currently look surprisingly low considering the geopolitical context. WTI is moving around 71 dollars, Brent toward 74 dollars, and negotiations are still not the same thing as fully stable peace. The market is behaving as if the worst-case scenario has already been avoided, although energy risks have not disappeared.

The biggest reason for the calming of oil prices is the expectation that flows through the Strait of Hormuz will remain stable. If tankers continue to sail, if additional Middle Eastern supply returns to the market and if negotiations continue without new escalation, then a lower oil price has a certain logic. But the problem is that the market may be pricing in the best-case scenario too quickly.

## Oil Is Pricing Peace Before Peace Is Secured

In oil markets, the same pattern often appears: as soon as fear of war, blockade or supply disruption emerges, the price quickly builds in a geopolitical premium. But as soon as a signal of calming appears, that premium can disappear almost just as quickly. That is exactly what is happening now.

Brent around 74 dollars and WTI around 71 dollars suggest that the market currently does not expect a major supply disruption. The price increasingly reflects a scenario in which the Strait of Hormuz remains open, oil flows normalize and a wider escalation is avoided.

{{< support1 >}}

That is an optimistic view. It may turn out to be correct, but it is not guaranteed yet. Negotiations can move forward, but they can also stall. Statements from the parties involved can change within a single day. One new incident in the region would be enough for the market to start pricing in a war premium again.

That is why the current oil price is interesting: it does not speak only about supply and demand, but also about market psychology. Oil is not pricing only barrels right now. It is pricing the expectation of peace.

## Why Does Brent Around 74 Dollars Matter?

Brent around 74 dollars is not historically an extremely low price, but in this context it looks low. Not long ago, markets reacted much more nervously to risks in the Middle East, Hormuz and potential supply problems. Now that fear has largely been removed from the price.

That may be rational if supply really stabilizes. But it may also be a too-fast reaction from a market that sometimes moves from panic to excessive optimism within just a few days.

Oil is therefore a good example of how markets do not wait for a political process to be completed. They try to guess the outcome in advance. If they believe the crisis will calm down, the price falls before security is truly confirmed.

## Gas Sends a Different Message

With gas, the picture is somewhat different. European TTF gas around 41–42 EUR/MWh is not a panic level, but it is not completely relaxed either. The gas market remains more sensitive because Europe depends on LNG, storage filling, weather conditions and the stability of key suppliers.

The explosion and problems linked to Qatar’s LNG infrastructure reminded the market how sensitive that system is. Qatar is one of the world’s most important exporters of liquefied natural gas. Even when the message is that production will gradually normalize, the market has to look at the bigger picture: how quickly the system returns, how reliable the infrastructure is and how much Europe will have to pay to secure enough volumes before winter.

That is why gas does not send the same message as oil. Oil says: “the market believes in calming.” Gas says: “maybe, but the supply system is still fragile.”

## Qatar Matters Because Europe Has Little Room for Error

After the energy crisis and the reduction of dependence on Russian gas, Europe turned more strongly toward LNG. That increased the importance of Qatar, the United States and other exporters of liquefied natural gas. LNG brought flexibility, but it did not remove the risk.

For LNG, gas reserves alone are not enough. Terminals, ships, contracts, safe maritime routes, stable production and reliable infrastructure are all needed. If one important hub gets stuck, the entire market immediately becomes more nervous.

{{< support2 >}}

That is why news from Qatar carries more weight than it may seem at first glance. Even if normal production returns within a few weeks, the very fact that a problem appeared in such an important LNG system is enough to remind Europe that energy security has not yet been solved.

## Oil Watches Negotiations, Gas Watches Winter

One of the key differences between oil and gas is the season. Oil is now mostly watching negotiations, Hormuz, Iran, the possible return of supply and transport security. Gas, alongside geopolitics, always watches winter as well.

Europe has to fill storage before the colder period. If LNG supply becomes less secure, gas prices can remain firm even without a dramatic jump. The market may not panic immediately, but it will not fully ignore the risk either.

That explains why oil can look “super low”, while gas still shows nervousness. Oil is currently reacting to a signal of de-escalation. Gas is reacting to the question: will the system hold if a new disruption appears?

## What Could Change the Direction of Prices?

For oil, prices could rise again if negotiations break down, if there is new military escalation, an attack on energy infrastructure, problems in the Strait of Hormuz or signs that additional supply will not return as quickly as the market expects.

For gas, prices could rise if LNG production recovers more slowly, if problems in Qatar continue, if Asian demand strengthens, if weather forecasts for Europe turn colder or if European storage filling slows down.

On the other hand, if negotiations continue, transport remains safe, Qatari production gradually normalizes and Europe fills storage without major problems, both oil and gas could remain under control. But that scenario requires several positive conditions to happen at the same time.

## Our View (Metaadvisor)

- Oil currently looks surprisingly calm. WTI around 71 dollars and Brent around 74 dollars suggest that the market is already pricing in de-escalation and more stable flows through the Strait of Hormuz.
- The problem is that negotiations are not the same as a permanently resolved crisis. The market can remove a war premium quickly, but it can bring it back just as quickly if new escalation appears.
- Gas shows a different picture. TTF around 41–42 EUR/MWh is not panic territory, but LNG risks, Qatar and Europe’s winter preparation show that the supply system remains sensitive.
- In short: oil is currently pricing peace before peace is secured, while gas is still pricing system fragility.
- For consumers, industry and investors, calmer prices do not mean that risk has disappeared. Sometimes they only mean that the market currently believes in the best-case scenario.
