# UK Regulator Takes Aim at Google Search: Will AI Overviews Have to Become Fairer?

The UK competition regulator, the CMA, or Competition and Markets Authority, has introduced new binding rules for Google Search. At first glance, that may sound like another technical regulatory story from the digital market. But for owners of websites, portals, blogs, affiliate projects and online stores, this is much more important than it may seem.

**Google Search is no longer just a search engine. It is increasingly becoming a system that gives the answer itself — before the user even clicks on a source.** That is why the UK regulator’s decision matters: it does not only concern traditional search results, but also Google’s AI Overviews.

{{< support1 >}}

## Why does this matter now?

For months, people on LinkedIn, X, Reddit, SEO forums and in comments from website owners have been discussing sharp drops in organic traffic after Google changes and the expansion of AI Overviews. Some site owners have publicly mentioned traffic declines of **20, 30, 40 or even more than 50 percent**. These numbers do not automatically mean that AI Overviews alone are responsible, because Google changes several parts of its search system at the same time. But the trend is large enough that it can no longer be ignored.

There are also more specific analyses showing why the issue is serious. One analysis related to Wikipedia estimated that exposure to Google AI Overview results could reduce daily traffic to affected articles by around **15 percent**. Another large analysis of Google AI Overviews found that AI summaries appeared in about **13.7 percent** of observed queries, while for question-based searches that share rose to around **64.7 percent**. That suggests AI Overviews are no longer just a small additional feature, but an increasingly important layer of search.

For smaller web projects, a traffic decline is not just a statistic. A 40 percent drop can mean lower ad revenue, fewer affiliate clicks, fewer sales, fewer inquiries and a lower value for the entire project. If traffic disappears almost overnight and the website owner receives no clear explanation, this is no longer only an SEO problem. It becomes a question of market power.

{{< support1 >}}

## AI Overview changes the relationship between Google and the web

In traditional Google Search, the user saw a list of results and decided where to click. With AI Overviews, Google summarizes information from different sources and displays the answer directly on the search results page. For the user, that can be fast and convenient. But for publishers, it raises an uncomfortable question: **if the user already gets the answer on Google, why would they click on the original article?**

That is the real problem. Websites create content, Google indexes that content, and then AI Overview can give the user a good enough answer without the user visiting the source. If this happens at scale, publishers lose part of their traffic while Google keeps the user inside its own system.

That is why the UK response matters. The CMA is now asking for Google Search, including AI Overviews, to become fairer and more transparent. Google is expected to show clearer ranking criteria, apply objective and non-discriminatory criteria for organic results and create better processes for companies and publishers affected by major changes in visibility.

## Germany talks about liability, the UK talks about traffic

This UK decision comes shortly after an important German ruling related to Google’s AI answers. A court in Munich ruled that Google can be held responsible for false claims appearing in AI Overview answers. In other words, if AI Overview does not merely display links but creates a new statement, a court may treat that statement as Google’s own content.

Metaadvisor has already covered that story here: <a href="https://metaadvisor.eu/news/google-ai-overviews-german-court-liability-ai-answers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Google AI Overviews and the German court ruling on liability for AI answers</a>.

The German ruling and the UK decision complement each other very well. **Germany asks who is responsible if AI Overview makes a false claim. The UK asks what happens when AI Search changes the visibility of the entire internet and websites lose traffic without a clear explanation.** One is a question of responsibility for content. The other is a question of market power.

{{< support2 >}}

## It is good that the UK regulator reacted quickly

The most positive part of this story is the speed of the reaction. AI Overviews did not exist for decades without oversight before someone started asking questions. This time, a regulator recognized relatively quickly that AI search can change the information market, website traffic and the relationship between platforms and publishers.

That does not mean the problem will be solved overnight. Google will certainly not publicly open its entire algorithm, and that would not be realistic to expect. But there is a big difference between a situation in which Google can simply say “the algorithm changed” and a situation in which the company has to show clearer processes, more objective criteria and better ways to handle complaints.

For website owners, this is an important signal. If a regulator is now addressing AI Overviews and fair ranking, it means AI search is no longer being treated only as a new technical feature. It is increasingly being viewed as part of digital infrastructure with real economic consequences.

## Will other regulators follow?

It is very possible that they will. If the UK is now raising questions about AI Overviews, fair ranking and traffic losses, it is hard to imagine that other regulators will completely ignore the issue. The European Union already has the Digital Services Act and the Digital Markets Act, and pressure on Big Tech is growing in other countries as well.

Germany has opened the question of responsibility for false AI claims. The UK is now opening the question of market harm, transparency and fairness in Google Search. The next step could be broader regulatory pressure on AI search, especially if complaints from publishers and website owners continue to grow.

{{< support2 >}}

**Europe is finally beginning to respond to Big Tech not only with political statements, but with concrete judicial and regulatory actions.** That matters because the rules of AI search are being shaped right now. If they are set too late, many smaller websites may no longer have enough strength to recover from traffic losses.

## What does this mean for small web projects?

For small web projects, the message is clear: Google traffic still matters, but dependence on a single search engine is becoming increasingly risky. High-quality content, SEO, good site structure, clear headings, internal links and a recognizable brand are still important. But on their own, they are no longer enough.

It is becoming more important to build an audience outside Google as well: newsletters, social media, direct visits, community and brand recognition. If AI Search becomes the new form of search, websites need to think not only about how they rank, but also about whether the user will still have a reason to click on the original source.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

This decision by the UK regulator may be more important than it first appears. AI Overviews are not just a new Google feature. They change how users receive information and how websites receive traffic.

The German ruling opened the question of responsibility: who is liable if AI Overview produces a false claim? The UK decision opens an even broader question: what happens if AI search reduces traffic to websites that have spent years creating the content on which those answers partly rely?

It is good that the UK reacted quickly. If rules are set in time, it may be possible to avoid a situation in which a large part of the internet produces content while most of the value remains with the platform that summarizes it.

For website owners, this is a warning, but also a small sign of hope. AI Search will not disappear. But if regulators begin to demand more transparency, fairer ranking and responsibility for consequences, then the relationship between Google, publishers and users may not develop in a completely one-sided way.

For Metaadvisor, this is one of the key AI stories to watch. The future of search will not be shaped only by better models and faster answers. It will also be shaped by legal responsibility, market power and the question of whether the open web can survive when AI systems increasingly answer before users click.
