# German ruling against Google: AI Overview is no longer just a link, but platform content

A German court has issued a decision that could matter for the entire AI industry, not only for Google. According to the ruling, Google can be held liable for false claims that appear in its AI Overview answers. In other words, when Google’s AI creates a summary by itself and shows it to the user as an answer, that is no longer just a neutral link to someone else’s content.

That is the key difference. A classic search engine shows links, titles and short snippets. The user still has to click, open the source and read the context. AI Overview does something different: it takes information, creates an answer and displays it at the top of search as a ready-made summary. If that answer is wrong, the damage can happen immediately.

## What happened?

The case involved two German publishers who claimed that Google’s AI Overview had wrongly linked them to scams and questionable business practices. That kind of mistake is not minor. When someone’s name, company or publication is connected with fraud in Google search, it can directly damage their reputation.

Google argued that users know AI can make mistakes and that they should verify information themselves. The court, however, did not accept the idea that all responsibility can be shifted to the user. If Google designs the system, operates it and displays the AI answer at the top of search, Google cannot simply say: “The AI made a mistake, the user should have checked.”

## Why does this ruling matter?

This ruling shows that AI can be regulated when there is a concrete product, concrete harm and a concrete company showing that product to the user. AI is not some fog without an owner. In this case, AI Overview is part of Google Search. Google displays it, shapes it and places it in front of the user.

That matters because large platforms are increasingly moving away from showing links and toward generating ready-made answers. And when a platform no longer only displays other people’s content, but creates the answer itself, the question of responsibility also changes.

## The same problem is coming to Facebook

Google is not the only example. Meta is now introducing **AI Mode** on Facebook, meaning AI search that does not only give users classic results, but summarized answers based on public content from Meta platforms. That includes public posts from Facebook Groups, Reels and other publicly available content.

We already wrote about this in an earlier article: <a href="https://metaadvisor.eu/news/facebook-ai-mode-meta-ai-groups-reels/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Facebook AI Mode: what happens when Meta AI summarizes public posts from Groups and Reels?</a>.

The problem is very similar to Google AI Overviews. When a platform does not show the user only a post or a link, but creates a summary itself, the user may get the impression that it is verified information. But public posts from Groups, Reels and social networks are often not verified information. They can be personal experiences, opinions, emotions, local recommendations, half-information or disinformation.

## An AI summary is not the same as a source

That is the core of the problem. A link leads to the source. An AI summary replaces part of the source. The user often will not click further because they already have a ready-made answer. That is exactly why AI summaries can have a greater impact than ordinary search results.

If Google’s AI wrongly connects a publisher with fraud, the damage can be real. If Meta AI summarizes public posts from Groups and wrongly represents a person, company, event, product or community, the consequences can also be real.

Large platforms cannot say at the same time that their AI answers are useful, smart and good enough for millions of users, and then step back when an answer causes harm by saying that the user should have checked everything themselves.

## What does this mean for Big Tech?

For Big Tech, this is an uncomfortable signal. For years, large platforms built the position of intermediaries: they supposedly only display content that others have published. AI answers change that role. The platform increasingly becomes the summarizer, editor and generator of answers.

That does not mean every AI mistake will end up in court. But it does mean that the warning “AI can make mistakes” should not be enough to escape responsibility. If a platform uses AI as part of its product and that product harms someone, there must be a way to establish responsibility.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

This German decision matters because it asks a simple question: when AI speaks through the product of a large platform, can that platform claim it is not responsible?

* Google’s AI Overview is no longer only a neutral display of links, but a ready-made answer that the platform creates and shows to the user.
* If such an answer contains a false and harmful claim, responsibility cannot simply be pushed onto the user.
* The ruling matters beyond Google because it shows that AI can be regulated when a concrete product and concrete harm exist.
* The same pattern is now coming to Facebook through Meta AI Mode, which summarizes public posts from Groups, Reels and other content.
* When AI summarizes social content, the risk can be even greater because public posts are often not verified information, but opinions, experiences or half-information.
* Large platforms should not get the right to produce mass AI answers without real responsibility for what those answers say.

The conclusion is simple: AI can be regulated when there is concrete harm and a clearly responsible actor. The problem is not that AI regulation is impossible. The problem is that large platforms must not be allowed to produce mass AI answers without real responsibility for what those answers say.
