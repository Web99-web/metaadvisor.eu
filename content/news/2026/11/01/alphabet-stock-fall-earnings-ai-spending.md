**Alphabet, Google's parent company, lost almost 14% of its Class A share value in two trading sessions after its latest results. Revenue did not fall: it rose 25%, while Google Cloud more than doubled its sales. Investors were troubled by a different question: how much cash will it take to keep that growth going?**

## How far did the shares actually fall?

The Class A shares, traded under **GOOGL**, closed at **$324.89** on October 28. After the evening earnings release, the regular session on October 29 ended at **$283.82**, down **12.64%**. On Friday, October 30, the stock fell another **1.40%**, closing at **$279.84**. Those closing prices agree in the historical tables published by Stock Analysis and Investing.com.

From the pre-release close to Friday, that is a **13.87%** decline, calculated from the prices. Daily percentages are not simply added because the second decline starts from a new, lower base. These are not today's trading figures or the decline from an annual peak. They cover two sessions after the third-quarter results; GOOG, another Alphabet share class, is not being mixed with GOOGL.

We covered the pressure on Google [on October 22](/news/google-alphabet-stock-decline-ai-spending-2026/), before the release. There are now new financial figures and a market reaction to examine.

## From a peak above $400 to a break below $300

Our October 22 article described pressure before the results. Its cited **$408.61** peak was an intraday price, not a close. Quantellect's historical daily table dates it to **May 18**. Comparing it with the October 30 close of $279.84 produces a decline of about **31.51%**. That is a different period from the post-earnings decline.

The **$300** threshold had been crossed by the **October 29** regular-session open at the latest: GOOGL opened at **$286.88**. Even its daily high was only $288.33. The entire regular session therefore traded below $296 as well. Daily data do not identify the exact minute of the first crossing in extended trading.

We found no reliable confirmation that **$296** was then an established technical support level, buying zone or analyst target. Support describes an area where buying may slow a decline; resistance describes one where selling may limit a rise. A level can change roles, but that interpretation requires price history and a defined timeframe. A number or an AI comment alone is not evidence.

The confirmed intraday low across the two post-earnings sessions examined was **$276.92** on October 30. Its **$279.84** close is a separate observation. The new chart displays daily closing prices from October 22 to October 30, without an invented minute-by-minute path or an unverified pause at $300 and $296.

## The business is growing, but expectations matter

Alphabet's report for the quarter ended September 30 shows **$127.6 billion of revenue**, up 25% year over year. Google Cloud reached **$30.7 billion**, an increase of 103%. Group operating income, the result of the underlying business before interest and taxes, rose about 30% to $40.6 billion.

Meanwhile, income available to common shareholders fell to $26.2 billion, roughly a quarter lower. That is different from a decline in operating income. The report also shows adverse effects from changes in investment valuations, so it would be wrong to attribute the entire drop in bottom-line income solely to AI costs. Officially reported diluted earnings per share were $2.12.

Reuters reported that total revenue missed analyst expectations, while slowing search growth added to investors' concerns. A stock responds not only to whether a company did better than last year, but also to whether it met the expectations embedded in its price.

## Why does AI spending matter so much?

In the third quarter, Alphabet spent **$51.4 billion** on capital expenditure, primarily technical infrastructure. Operations generated approximately **$42.3 billion of cash** over the same period. After those investments, free cash flow was negative by about **$9.1 billion**.

Free cash flow here means operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. A company can therefore report a profit while spending more cash on construction and equipment than its business generated in that quarter. A negative figure does not automatically mean it cannot pay its bills, but it makes reserves and financing more important.

According to Reuters' account of management's earnings discussion, the annual capital spending plan remained **$195-205 billion**. That is the total investment plan, not a separately measured bill exclusively for AI. Much of the infrastructure race concerns data centers, servers and capacity supporting AI and cloud services.

That equipment is paid for today, while revenue from it needs to arrive over years. Some costs enter the income statement gradually through depreciation, which spreads an asset's value over its useful life. Revenue growth alone therefore cannot establish the return on the investment.

## Was it the results or the wider market?

Both belong in the explanation. Technology stocks were under pressure over AI spending and uncertainty about interest rates. But Alphabet's reaction to its own report should not be reduced to general market nerves. Missed expectations, concerns about search and negative free cash flow together help explain its sharp response. That is an interpretation of available information, not proof that every seller had the same motive.

For the company, a lower share price is not the same event as an equivalent amount of money leaving its bank account. The market is reassessing future earnings. If a company raised money by issuing shares at a lower price, it would need to issue more of them for the same proceeds, diluting existing ownership more heavily.

## Our take (Metaadvisor)

* **Cloud growth demonstrates demand, but does not settle the return question.** The cost of building capacity and the cash left after investment also matter.
* **A large daily fall is not automatically a bargain.** A lower price can reflect excessive fear or changed earnings expectations. The percentage decline alone cannot distinguish between them.
* **Retail investors need to consider total exposure.** One company can appear directly in a portfolio and indirectly through several funds. Google's familiar name does not eliminate market risk.
* **The next test is the relationship between growth and cash.** Sustainable cash generation and clear investment discipline would do more for confidence than a promise of an even larger AI budget.

Alphabet needs to do more than use one impressive number to demonstrate that AI attracts users. It must finance growth in a way that makes the long-term benefit worth today's bill. The distinction between technological success and financial return now sits at the center of the story.
