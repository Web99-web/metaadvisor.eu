**OpenAI generated approximately $10 billion in revenue in the third quarter of 2026, up nearly 50% from the previous quarter. Yet the company behind ChatGPT reported a $10.9 billion adjusted operating loss. Figures presented to investors and reported on November 10 raise a central question: Can the extraordinary growth of artificial intelligence offset its enormous development and operating costs?**

## Revenue grows, but losses remain enormous

Between July and September, quarterly revenue reached around $10 billion, approximately 2.6 times the year-earlier level. Demand for newer AI models, enterprise products and APIs helped drive growth as businesses integrated artificial intelligence into their operations.

The adjusted operating loss increased from about $10.1 billion to $10.9 billion. At the same time, the adjusted operating margin improved from negative 150% to negative 109%. Under this measure, OpenAI loses less relative to each dollar of revenue, although its absolute loss has increased. These adjusted operating results are not final net losses under standard accounting rules and may exclude substantial costs, including stock-based compensation.

## Why does AI cost so much?

Advanced AI requires powerful processors, memory, high-speed networks and energy-intensive data centers. Unlike conventional software, operating an AI model continues to incur computing costs with every user request. Training new generations of models involves enormous datasets and extended use of specialized hardware.

Research, safety testing, infrastructure maintenance and highly skilled staff add further expenses. If usage grows faster than the cost per request declines, higher revenue can still be accompanied by rising total costs.

## Anthropic reports higher revenue with smaller losses

Reports indicate that Anthropic, developer of the Claude models, generated approximately $17 billion in revenue during the same quarter while reporting an operating loss of about $567 million. The apparent contrast is substantial, but the figures are not fully comparable.

OpenAI's adjusted operating measure excludes approximately $2.6 billion in stock-based compensation, whereas Anthropic includes such expenses. Business models differ too: OpenAI serves consumers, enterprises and API customers while maintaining broad research operations; Anthropic has a strong enterprise focus. Demand alone does not establish similar financial efficiency across providers.

## Microsoft's revenue highlights AI's financial ties

Microsoft is a major investor and infrastructure partner of OpenAI. It recorded approximately $10 billion in revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI in the quarter ended September 30. That figure is revenue recognized by Microsoft, not an additional $10 billion earned by OpenAI. Microsoft also reported roughly $7.2 billion in outstanding receivables from OpenAI.

The arrangements illustrate how a company can simultaneously act as investor, supplier, partner and competitor. Evaluating the AI industry's economics requires distinguishing customer spending from payments between interconnected technology businesses.

## High valuation despite continuing losses

Media reports suggest OpenAI is pursuing further financing at a potential valuation of approximately $1.25 trillion. Such a figure reflects expectations that artificial intelligence will become essential global infrastructure, rather than demonstrated current profitability.

Investors anticipate AI adoption across education, programming, healthcare, finance and business, especially through agents capable of completing complex tasks. However, technological success and financial sustainability are different. A company can attract millions of users and grow rapidly while spending more than it earns.

## Can AI become profitable?

More efficient chips, software optimization and smaller specialized models could reduce computing costs. At the same time, increased competition may constrain prices. Free users drive adoption but create ongoing expenses. Enterprise subscriptions, APIs and long-term contracts may offer more stable revenue, although delivering them still requires infrastructure.

Whether advertising and new services can cover the growing cost of research and computing remains uncertain. Ultimately, the balance between revenue and the cost of delivering AI services matters as much as user growth and model quality.
