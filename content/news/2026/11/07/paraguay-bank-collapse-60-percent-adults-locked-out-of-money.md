**Nearly three million people in Paraguay suddenly lost access to their bank accounts, payment cards and cash withdrawals after regulators intervened in digital lender Ueno Bank. That represents approximately 60% of the country's adult population. The bank's collapse triggered widespread protests, disrupted everyday payments and sparked a political crisis that ended with the removal of President Santiago Peña. The former president was initially placed under house arrest, and prosecutors filed criminal charges against him on November 7. Meanwhile, authorities began returning insured deposits to affected customers.**

## How Did One Bank Lock Millions of People Out of Their Money?

Within just a few years, Ueno Bank became one of Paraguay's most recognizable digital banks. Easy account opening, mobile banking, shopping rewards and aggressive advertising helped it attract approximately 2.9 million customers. According to media estimates, that figure represents around 60% of the country's adult population. Many customers depended on the bank for salaries, savings and everyday transactions.

In early November, customers suddenly found themselves unable to use their money. Cash withdrawals, card payments and bank transfers stopped functioning normally. Concerned depositors gathered outside branches, while businesses and employees struggled to find alternative ways to make payments. The problem extended far beyond access to savings. For many people, even purchasing essential goods became difficult.

However, being locked out of an account does not necessarily mean the money has been permanently lost. Deposits did not automatically disappear, and there is no confirmation that all affected customers suffered permanent financial losses. Access to the accounts was frozen, and the government began returning insured deposits several days later.

## Why Did Ueno Bank Collapse?

On November 2, 2026, Paraguay's central bank decided to initiate a bank resolution procedure for Ueno Bank. The decision was publicly announced on November 3. Following adjustments to the bank's financial statements, regulators determined that its solvency ratio had fallen below half the legally required minimum.

In practical terms, the bank no longer maintained sufficient capital to meet regulatory requirements and absorb the risks associated with its operations.

Investigators are also examining possible irregularities involving loans to companies connected to the bank's controlling shareholder group, as well as relationships between the lender and senior political officials. These allegations remain under investigation and should not be treated as established wrongdoing.

The central bank has emphasized that the resolution process is intended to protect depositors and preserve confidence in the wider financial system.

## President Tried to Resign but Lost His Political Immunity

Ueno Bank held more than private customer deposits. Significant amounts of public money were also deposited with the lender, including funds connected to government institutions and Paraguay's pension system. Media reports estimate that approximately $800 million to $860 million in public and pension-related funds were associated with the bank. Investigators are examining how those funds were placed there and whether the decisions involved conflicts of interest.

Particular attention has focused on President Santiago Peña's previous financial connections to Ueno Bank. He held shares in a company associated with the bank's ownership structure until 2025. Prosecutors suspect that Peña may have used his presidential influence to encourage government agencies and state-owned institutions to maintain substantial deposits at the lender despite potential conflicts of interest.

Peña denies any wrongdoing and maintains that the bank's problems were a regulatory matter.

On November 5, Peña submitted his resignation, saying he wanted to help calm the political crisis. However, Congress did not complete the process of accepting it. A parliamentary session lost the required quorum, and lawmakers proceeded with impeachment proceedings instead.

The distinction was far more than a procedural technicality.

Under Paraguay's Constitution, democratically elected former presidents can receive lifetime seats in the Senate, together with parliamentary protections, unless they have been removed through impeachment.

Had Congress accepted Peña's resignation, he could have retained that special political status. By removing him through impeachment, lawmakers prevented him from obtaining the lifetime Senate seat and the associated immunity.

This opened the way for criminal proceedings without first requiring the removal of parliamentary immunity.

On November 6, Paraguay's Senate unanimously voted to remove Peña from office. All 44 senators present supported his impeachment.

Shortly afterward, prosecutors issued an arrest warrant, and police officers arrived at his residence in Asunción. The former president was initially placed under house arrest.

The extraordinary political transition produced a striking scene on Paraguayan television.

As Pedro Alliana was sworn in as the country's new president, the national anthem played during the ceremony inside Congress. At the same time, a split-screen television broadcast showed police officers surrounding the residence of his predecessor.

While Paraguay welcomed a new president to the sound of its national anthem, the man who had held the office only moments earlier was being placed under arrest.

The anthem accompanied the inauguration ceremony, not the arrest itself.

On November 7, Peña appeared before prosecutors, who filed charges related to breach of trust and using public office for personal benefit. They also requested pretrial detention.

Peña rejects the allegations. His removal from office, arrest and criminal charges do not establish guilt. The investigation and judicial proceedings remain ongoing, and the presumption of innocence applies.

## Protests, Business Disruption and Fears Over Pension Funds

The collapse triggered protests in Asunción and other parts of Paraguay. Thousands of citizens demanded access to their savings and accountability from bank executives, regulators and political leaders.

Some demonstrations escalated into clashes with police.

The disruption also affected retailers and small businesses. With customers unable to use their Ueno Bank accounts, some merchants reported dramatic declines in sales.

Many workers struggled to access their salaries or pay for essential goods, illustrating how rapidly a banking crisis can spread into the wider economy when a large share of the population depends on a single financial institution.

Concerns over public pension funds added another dimension to the crisis.

Paraguay's largest teachers' union announced plans for a strike beginning November 9, demanding greater protection for pension savings and public funds.

The presence of government and pension-related deposits at Ueno Bank does not automatically mean pension payments have stopped. However, it raises serious questions about the concentration of public money in one institution and the adequacy of financial oversight.

## How Much Money Have Depositors Recovered?

Paraguay operates a deposit guarantee system intended to protect customers when a financial institution fails.

According to the central bank, eligible deposits are insured up to 228.3 million Paraguayan guaraníes per depositor and financial institution, equivalent to 75 minimum monthly wages.

Emergency legislation introduced following Ueno Bank's collapse authorized accelerated advance payments of up to 40 minimum wages.

The first round of reimbursements began on November 5 and initially focused on customers with smaller deposits who met the required conditions. Additional groups of depositors were subsequently included.

By November 7, approximately 1.2 million savers had received partial or full reimbursement of their insured funds.

According to Reuters, around $160 million had already been distributed through the deposit guarantee scheme.

Payments are continuing through other banks and financial institutions, allowing affected customers to regain access to at least part of their savings.

However, the situation remains less certain for depositors whose balances exceed the statutory insurance limit. The eventual recovery of amounts above that threshold may depend on the bank's remaining assets and the outcome of the resolution process.

Deposit insurance provides important financial protection, but it does not guarantee immediate access to funds during a banking failure.

Even when customers ultimately recover their money, several days without functioning payment services can create serious difficulties for households and businesses.

## Could the Crisis Spread to Other Banks?

Paraguayan authorities maintain that the financial problems identified so far are limited to Ueno Bank. Regulators are seeking to prevent panic and preserve confidence in the rest of the banking system.

There is currently no confirmation that other banks in Paraguay have become insolvent as a result of Ueno's collapse.

Nevertheless, the sudden demand for alternative banking services created operational pressure across the financial sector.

Large numbers of affected customers began opening accounts with other institutions, while increased demand temporarily disrupted some digital banking services.

The crisis highlights a broader risk associated with the concentration of everyday financial activity in a single institution.

Digital banks can expand rapidly by offering simple account opening, convenient mobile applications and competitive customer benefits. This growth can improve financial inclusion, particularly among people who previously had limited access to traditional banking.

However, rapid expansion must be accompanied by adequate capital reserves, effective risk management and strong regulatory supervision.

When millions of people rely on one institution for salaries, savings and daily payments, its sudden failure can become a national economic problem.

The Ueno Bank collapse does not prove that digital banks are inherently unsafe. It demonstrates that the basic principles of banking stability remain essential regardless of whether customers access their money through a mobile application or a traditional branch.
