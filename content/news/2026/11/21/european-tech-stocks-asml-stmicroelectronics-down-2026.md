**European stock markets ended their toughest week in more than a year, with semiconductor companies bearing the brunt of the decline. On Friday, November 20, shares in ASML and STMicroelectronics each lost more than 9%, while the European technology sector fell around 5.4%. The pan-European STOXX 600 ended the session down 2% and lost more than 5.7% over the week. The sell-off shows that doubts about the returns on enormous artificial intelligence investments are no longer confined to Wall Street.**

## Europe ends a fifth consecutive session in the red

The STOXX 600 closed Friday at approximately 625.65 points, its lowest closing level in six weeks. It marked a fifth consecutive negative session, and the weekly performance was the weakest since March 2025. Investors reduced exposure not only to companies directly linked to AI, but also to industrial and other cyclical stocks. Some selling reflected profit-taking after earlier gains, while other trades reflected a reassessment of future risks.

The decline in Europe's technology sector is especially significant because many of its companies supply the global semiconductor industry. Their market valuations depend not only on current results but also on expectations of large investments in data centers and new computing infrastructure over the coming years.

## Why does ASML matter so much?

Dutch company ASML makes sophisticated lithography equipment used to manufacture the most advanced chips. Its position in the supply chain means that decisions by major semiconductor manufacturers to expand capacity can strongly affect future orders. STMicroelectronics has a broader product range, with chips for industrial applications, vehicles and electronics, and is therefore exposed to different sources of demand. Even so, both companies suffer when investors begin to cut technology-sector risk.

ASML does not primarily manufacture chips itself. Instead, it produces the lithography systems that enable manufacturers such as TSMC and Samsung to make advanced semiconductors. STMicroelectronics, by contrast, makes semiconductor components themselves: microcontrollers, sensors and power-management chips used in vehicles, industrial automation, energy systems and consumer electronics.

A one-day share-price decline of more than 9% does not automatically mean the business deteriorated by the same amount on that day. Stock prices reflect expectations, the value of future earnings, liquidity and investor sentiment. That is why a sudden sell-off can hit companies even when they have not announced new negative business developments.

## The AI boom faces questions about investment returns

The dramatic change in sentiment followed months during which investors expected almost unlimited growth in demand for chips. Major technology firms announced vast spending on data centers, electricity and specialized processors. Now the market is increasingly asking how quickly those investments will generate actual revenue. Even strong interest in newly listed AI companies failed to stop the broader selling wave.

For European equipment suppliers, the key risk is that customers might postpone investment decisions. If construction of a data center is pushed back by several quarters, expected orders across a whole supply chain may change, from semiconductor manufacturing equipment to energy infrastructure.

## Additional geopolitical pressure on Europe

Europe's semiconductor industry is also exposed to the relationship between the United States and China. US controls on exports of advanced computing equipment, along with Chinese decisions about critical raw materials, can affect supply chains and sales opportunities. Export policy is particularly important for ASML because its equipment is strategically sensitive. It is essential, however, to distinguish the demonstrated impact of measures already adopted from the possible consequences of future political decisions.

## What comes next for European markets?

A short-term rebound is possible after such a steep weekly fall, but one positive session would not establish that the correction is over. Investors will watch US technology shares, earnings from major chipmakers and whether orders for manufacturing equipment hold up. For the European economy, the question is whether weaker sentiment stays within equity markets or leads businesses to delay real industrial projects.

## Our take (Metaadvisor)

- The AI sell-off has become a European story: ASML and STMicroelectronics show how closely European manufacturers are tied to the global investment cycle.
- A falling stock price is not the same as deteriorating operations. Market sentiment must be distinguished from confirmed changes in orders.
- ASML occupies a strategic position in semiconductor manufacturing but remains exposed to its customers' investment decisions.
- Export controls and US-China relations add uncertainty for the European technology industry.
- The next important test is real demand for AI infrastructure, not just the short-term direction of equity indexes.

Europe's decline shows that investment in artificial intelligence is not a story limited to a single country or company. When markets begin to question the returns on major investments, the consequences quickly spread across the technology supply chain, including European industry.
