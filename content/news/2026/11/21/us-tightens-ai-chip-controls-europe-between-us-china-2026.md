**The United States has introduced new rules restricting access to advanced computer chips and their processing power through overseas data centers. The Commerce Department regulation took effect on November 19, 2026, and aims to prevent sensitive computing resources from being accessed through intermediaries and cloud services. For European industry, especially equipment suppliers such as Dutch company ASML, the rules are another sign that the technology rivalry between the United States and China is increasingly becoming a struggle over global supply chains.**

## Washington also wants to control remote access

Until now, export controls were most commonly associated with the physical shipment of advanced processors and manufacturing equipment. But the development of artificial intelligence has created another route to computing power: a company can rent capacity in a data center in another country and use advanced chips remotely without buying them directly.

A new interim final rule published by the US Bureau of Industry and Security introduces additional controls when advanced chips are shipped to data centers that are known to provide significant computing capacity to entities headquartered in countries on a designated US security-restrictions list or in Macau. It adds requirements to verify end users and ownership relationships, and certain transactions require export licenses.

## Five additional markets face new requirements

The regulation also introduces extra requirements for deliveries of certain advanced semiconductors to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. US authorities identify these markets as carrying an increased risk that sensitive technology could be redirected to prohibited users. The new rules do not amount to a complete ban on all trade with buyers in these countries: exemptions apply, including for some deliveries involving US-headquartered companies, subject to specific reporting obligations.

For businesses, this means more complex administration and closer checks. Data-center operators, exporters and intermediaries must understand who will actually use the computing power, not merely who formally places an order for the chips.

## Why does this change matter for artificial intelligence?

Training large AI models requires enormous computing capacity, but direct ownership of processors is no longer necessary. Companies can rent cloud resources, use several data centers and distribute workloads across countries. As a result, technology controls are gradually expanding beyond the manufacture of physical chips to cover access to computing services, user identity and infrastructure security.

This approach may make it harder to evade export restrictions, but it also raises compliance costs. International operators serving large numbers of clients across multiple jurisdictions are particularly exposed.

## Europe is caught between two large markets

Europe's semiconductor industry cannot be viewed separately from global supply chains. Dutch company ASML produces lithography equipment essential for the most advanced chips, while European makers of electronics, vehicles and industrial devices rely on components and raw materials sourced worldwide. The United States exerts considerable influence over chip design and access rules for advanced technology, while China plays an important role in processing some critical minerals and in industrial manufacturing.

The new US requirements do not mean that every European business will immediately lose access to a market or need to change its operations. They do, however, underline the importance of geopolitical conditions when planning manufacturing, cross-border deliveries and data-center investments. Europe needs to maintain access to American technology, Asian markets and its own industrial capabilities at the same time.

## January 2027 remains a key trade deadline

Investors are also watching January 2027, when certain temporary trade measures between the United States and China are due to expire. Their expiration does not automatically mean another escalation. Extensions, changes or new agreements are possible, but the uncertainty already affects companies planning long-term projects.

That is why the debate over strategic resilience matters for Europe: how can it develop its own capabilities without giving up the benefits of international cooperation? Complete independence in producing the most advanced chips cannot be achieved quickly, because the current system relies on specialization across many countries.

## Our take (Metaadvisor)

- US oversight is no longer limited to the physical shipment of chips; it also addresses remote use of advanced computing capacity for AI systems.
- Five additional markets face tighter requirements, not a blanket prohibition on trade.
- Data-center operators will need more detailed checks of users and ownership connections.
- European companies depend on stable global supply chains and the political decisions of major powers.
- For Europe, improving long-term resilience without retreating from global markets is essential.

The new rule demonstrates that competition in artificial intelligence increasingly revolves around control over computing power. Developing world-class technology alone is no longer enough for European industry: companies also need to adapt to more complex conditions governing its international sale and use.
