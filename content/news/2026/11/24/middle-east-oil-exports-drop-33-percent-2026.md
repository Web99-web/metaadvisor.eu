**Middle Eastern crude oil exports have fallen 33% in five weeks, at least 12 vessels have been struck in November, and oil prices are still declining. At the same time, the International Energy Agency expects the global oil market to return to surplus. The apparent contradiction comes down to earlier shipments still crossing the oceans, October's strong supply rebound and demand recovering more slowly than production.**

Kpler tanker-tracking data analyzed by Reuters on November 24, 2026 show that weekly crude exports from the Middle East fell from **20.86 million barrels per day** in the week beginning October 12 to **13.96 million** in the week beginning November 16. That is a **33.1%** decline from an unusually high October week, not a 33% fall in global production. Meanwhile, Brent crude retreated from an October peak near $118 a barrel toward $89. The market appears to be pricing abundant future supply even as fewer new cargoes leave one of its most important exporting regions.

## Strait of Hormuz: at least 12 vessels struck in November

Shipping security is a critical concern. According to Joint Maritime Information Centre (JMIC) figures reported by Reuters, at least **12 vessels** have been struck in November, following 17 in October. Attacks and higher risks in and around the Strait of Hormuz can affect tanker availability, insurance costs and shipowners' willingness to use dangerous routes.

The rise in attacks coincides with declining exports, but that does not prove every missing barrel was caused by a strike. Changes in export routes and loading schedules, along with gaps in tracking data when vessels switch off identification systems, may influence the totals. Still, reduced shipments combined with uncertain navigation show how fragile the recent recovery in regional flows remains.

## Why is there talk of an oil surplus while exports are falling?

Global production is not the same as one region's exports in a given week. The IEA's official November 13 report says worldwide oil supply climbed by 2.3 million barrels a day in October to around **105.4 million barrels per day**. It estimates average world oil demand for 2026 at **102.3 million barrels per day**, following a sharp annual decline driven by high energy prices and economic disruption.

Those figures cannot simply be subtracted to establish an October surplus: one is a monthly supply figure, the other an annual demand average. But the direction is clear. After a substantial supply rebound in September and October, the IEA expects global supply to move back above demand in the final quarter of 2026. High prices, weaker activity in some industries and more efficient vehicles are constraining consumption growth.

## Much of the oil is still at sea

This is the most revealing part of the paradox. A tanker loaded in October may not reach its destination refinery that month. Much of the Persian Gulf's crude heads to Asia, which receives roughly 80% of Middle Eastern crude exports. The strong October cargo flow is therefore still feeding supplies for November and December, even though the volume of new shipments has been shrinking.

The IEA recorded a **52-million-barrel rise in observed global oil inventories in October**. Yet oil held or transported on water grew by **86 million barrels**, reaching its highest level since January, while on-land stocks continued to decline to their lowest levels since at least 2016. Oil on water is part of the total observed inventory picture, so these figures must not be added together.

Oil at sea is not necessarily unsold or freely available. Much of it already belongs to buyers and is en route to specific refineries; some is stored on ships. That means global supply can look plentiful even while particular locations remain short. **Earlier shipments are currently providing a buffer, but that protection could fade if new cargoes keep declining.**

## Diesel shortage: plenty of crude, not enough finished fuel

A surplus of crude oil does not automatically mean a surplus of diesel. Crude must first be processed in refineries, and disruptions to Middle Eastern refining infrastructure alongside attacks on Russian energy facilities have constrained supplies of particular fuels. The IEA identifies diesel availability as one of the most pressing remaining problems in the energy market.

According to a Reuters analysis published on November 23, US inventories of middle distillates, including diesel and heating oil, remained about **15% below their five-year average**, despite a recent improvement. Gasoline stocks, by contrast, had recovered above their usual levels. Europe, a major diesel importer, continued to face wholesale diesel prices far above those at the beginning of the year, even after they eased from autumn highs.

Refineries cannot produce diesel without also yielding other products such as gasoline. Running harder to capture high diesel margins therefore adds gasoline to a market that may already be adequately supplied, putting further pressure on gasoline prices. **Crude oil can get cheaper while diesel remains comparatively scarce and expensive.**

## Why are oil prices still falling?

Oil markets price expectations for the coming weeks and months, not just the number of barrels leaving port today. Strong September and October shipments are still reaching buyers, production has recovered and demand remains weak. Many traders also expect diplomacy to reduce geopolitical risks and restore safer passage through Hormuz.

But the latest export trend challenges the assumption that current supplies will remain comfortable. If departures stay near November's lower levels, earlier shipments will eventually be unloaded without an equivalent number of new cargoes behind them. Inventories may then start falling again. Alternatively, improved maritime security and a further supply recovery could continue to weigh on prices. Neither outcome is guaranteed.

## Our take

- **The 33% figure needs context:** It compares two individual weeks, not total annual global oil production.
- **Twelve struck vessels:** Maritime risks are serious, but attacks have not been proven to explain the entire export decline.
- **The surplus is a global forecast:** Recovering supply and weaker demand can coexist with disruptions in one export region.
- **Oil is already on the way:** October cargoes are arriving in November and December, so fewer new departures affect the market with a delay.
- **Inventories are unevenly distributed:** Oil on water has increased while on-land stocks remain unusually depleted.
- **Diesel is a separate problem:** Limited refining and product exports can keep diesel expensive even as crude prices fall.

**Today's oil-market paradox is that prices are declining partly because of barrels already produced and shipped, while fresh exports from the Middle East keep weakening. If that pattern persists, the crucial question will be how many barrels arrive after the tankers currently at sea have finished unloading.**
