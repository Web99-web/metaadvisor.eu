**227,700 followers. A gold verification badge. A familiar logo, professionally designed graphics and convincing text. Would you suspect anything was wrong?**

On November 25, 2026, we came across a post on X announcing the start of the third season of Hyperliquid's user rewards programme. According to the post, no fewer than 38.8 million HYPE tokens are reserved for the community, with users voting on the direction of their distribution. At first glance, everything looks like an official announcement. But take a closer look at the profile name.

The brain practically corrects the misspelled name on its own. We do not always read every individual letter; instead, we recognise the word we expect to see. When we also see hundreds of thousands of followers, a gold verification badge and familiar branding, it is easy to automatically assume that the account is official. And that is precisely the most dangerous part of this story.

Users searching for Hyperliquid, Hylpreliquid or @Hyperliquid on X should pay particular attention to the exact account handle. The project's official account is @HyperliquidX, while the profile in our screenshot uses the almost identical display name Hylpreliquid and the handle @Hylprerliquid.

## Scammers no longer target greed alone

This post does not promise a 1,000 percent return or offer millions of dollars to whoever clicks a link first. Instead, it offers something that sounds perfectly logical for Hyperliquid: community voting and token distribution.

The alleged 38.8 million HYPE tokens would be distributed among four groups:

- Perpetual futures traders.
- Liquidity providers and HLP users.
- HyperEVM applications.
- Spot and HIP-3 markets.

Everything is presented as a legitimate activity within an existing community. The post even emphasises that no group will be left without tokens, regardless of the voting results.

![Fake Hyperliquid Season 3 announcement](/images/hack/Hyperliquid-Fake-Season-3-Post.jpg)

What makes this even more insidious is that the accompanying text addresses users' security concerns in advance, claiming that voting neither transfers nor locks funds. This attempts to remove any remaining doubts before the user takes the next step.

Scammers no longer target greed alone. They target a sense of security, belonging to a community and trust in a familiar brand. **This is sophisticated social engineering, not just a rudimentary crypto scam.**

## What does a gold verification badge actually guarantee?

Users of X associate the gold verification badge with official business and organisational accounts. But how did a profile impersonating Hyperliquid obtain such a badge? How did it accumulate 227,700 followers? Were the followers purchased? Was a business account taken over and subsequently given a different name and visual identity?

We cannot confirm this at present. Nor do we know how many users saw the post, how many believed its claims or whether anyone lost money because of it. However, this case raises serious questions about the reliability of verification systems on social media. How many users continue checking an account's authenticity after seeing a gold badge and more than 227,000 followers?

## This is not just a cryptocurrency problem

Imagine someone impersonating any of the following tomorrow using the same approach:

- Your bank introducing a new security system.
- Microsoft requesting account verification.
- The tax authority announcing a tax refund.
- A delivery service requesting address confirmation.
- A well-known online shop launching a rewards programme.
- A media organisation publishing a fake breaking news story.
- A government institution requesting updated personal information.
- A public figure promoting a new product or service.

And each of these profiles has hundreds of thousands of followers, a verification badge, a recognisable logo and professionally written posts. How many people would suspect anything was wrong?

This form of impersonation can be used to steal personal information, access user accounts, commit financial fraud, spread disinformation or manipulate public opinion. **Anyone can be a target.**

## How to protect yourself

Follower counts, verification badges and visual identity are not sufficient proof of authenticity. Before entering personal information, passwords or bank card details, connecting a crypto wallet or authorising a financial transaction:

- Check every letter of the account name and handle.
- Open the organisation's official website independently of the post.
- Check whether the official website links to that profile.
- Check whether the same information appears on other official channels.
- Do not use a link from a suspicious post as the starting point for verifying its authenticity.

Even when a post comes from an authentic, official account, remember that such accounts can also be compromised.

## Our take

- Follower counts, verification badges and professional branding are no longer sufficient proof of authenticity.
- Scammers target not only greed but also a sense of security, community belonging and trust in familiar brands.
- The most dangerous scams may be precisely those that appear perfectly normal and raise no initial suspicion.
- This form of social engineering is not limited to cryptocurrencies. Anyone can be a target.
- Social media verification systems raise serious questions about the reliability of digital identity.

**Because sometimes the difference between trust and fraud is just a few letters.**
