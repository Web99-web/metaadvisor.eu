**Apple and Meta have joined a wave of major companies opposing the trading of tokenized versions of their shares on the planned OKX–ICE marketplace. According to reports published on November 6, 56 of the 62 companies originally selected for the platform have objected, a rejection rate of roughly 90%. That presents a major obstacle for a venture aiming to bring stock trading onto blockchain infrastructure.**

## What are OKX and ICE building?

Crypto exchange OKX and Intercontinental Exchange, the parent company of the New York Stock Exchange, are developing a venue for tokenized US stocks. The proposal involves blockchain-based representations of shares traded through automated liquidity pools, with supported stablecoins providing payment infrastructure.

The design could extend trading beyond conventional exchange hours. But turning shares into tokens raises questions beyond market technology: who can create the tokens, what rights do holders receive, and how are the underlying issuers involved?

## Why are Apple and Meta objecting?

Apple, Meta and Palantir are among prominent companies recorded as objecting to the planned venue. The overall objection count reportedly reached 67 issuers, including businesses outside the original group of 62. Of that initial group, 56 had filed objections.

Importantly, the companies are objecting to a particular venue's proposed third-party tokenization of their shares. That should not be described as a blanket rejection of blockchain technology or every potential model of tokenized securities. The precise motivations of individual issuers also differ and are not all publicly documented.

## What has the SEC actually authorized?

In September, the US Securities and Exchange Commission introduced a temporary, conditional innovation exemption for qualifying tokenized securities venues. The exemption is designed to run for up to five years and includes conditions covering trading limits, investor rights and issuer notices.

For shares tokenized by a third party unaffiliated with the original issuer, companies must have an opportunity to object. The SEC explains that a timely issuer objection prevents the specified tokenized stock from trading on that venue. This is a conditional pathway, not an unrestricted license to list any company's shares as tokens.

## Which companies have not publicly objected?

As of the November 6 report, six firms from the original selection had no publicly disclosed objection: Strategy, Coinbase, Circle, Robinhood, Securitize and BitGo. Their ties to crypto and digital finance make the remaining group especially notable.

Still, no public objection does not establish consent, endorsement or a firm commitment to participate. Responses and venue disclosures can change as the process continues.

## Is tokenized stock trading already in trouble?

The objection wave is a setback for this particular venue, not necessarily the end of tokenized equities. Issuers could pursue other structures, including arrangements they sponsor or approve directly. The dispute highlights a core tension between demand for around-the-clock blockchain markets and the need to preserve issuer rights, investor protections and orderly trading.

For investors, the crucial questions involve whether a token delivers rights equivalent to the underlying stock, how settlement works, how liquid the market may become and what happens if a smart contract or intermediary fails.
