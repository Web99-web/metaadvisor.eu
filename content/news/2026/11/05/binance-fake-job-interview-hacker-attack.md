**A fake job interview, a malicious coding assignment and several compromised employee computers were part of a months-long operation that ended with approximately $6.34 billion in cryptocurrency being stolen from Binance. According to preliminary findings published by the company on November 3, 2026, the attackers did not simply break through a single security barrier. They gradually gathered information about employees, exploited their trust and eventually used an internal artificial intelligence tool to expand their access to the company's systems. The incident demonstrates how dangerous the combination of social engineering, compromised software and automated business tools can become.**

## First attack detected back in June

According to Binance's report, the first known compromise attempt occurred in June 2026, almost four months before the actual theft. One of the engineers, identified in the report as Employee A, attempted to install an AI-assisted coding tool. While searching online, the engineer encountered a tampered installation method associated with the popular Homebrew software package manager.

The attackers attempted to exploit the installation process to place malicious code on the employee's computer. Binance's security systems detected the suspicious activity, after which the computer was replaced and the associated credentials were rotated. At the time, the company believed the threat had been eliminated, but the subsequent investigation showed that the incident had not been without consequences.

Although the immediate compromise was stopped, the attackers managed to gather information about other employees and their responsibilities during the operation. They were particularly interested in members of teams involved in cryptocurrency management and internal infrastructure. Those details were later used to carry out a much more precisely targeted attack.

## Fake job offer became an entry point into Binance's systems

In September, a second person, identified as Employee B, received a professionally presented job offer through a legitimate recruitment platform. The individual who initiated contact claimed to be a project director, and the communication resembled a routine recruitment process for a technology position.

The attackers organized several stages designed to make the offer appear credible. The employee participated in an alleged video interview with a human resources representative, followed by another conversation with someone impersonating the company's chief executive. The entire process was structured to gradually build the candidate's trust in the supposed employer.

After the interviews, the employee received a coding assignment to complete as part of the recruitment process. Such requests are common for software developers, as many technology companies ask applicants to demonstrate their skills through practical exercises. However, the code repository the employee was instructed to download and run contained malicious software.

That moment proved crucial. The attackers did not need to persuade the employee to reveal a password or directly disclose login credentials. They only needed to convince the employee to execute code that appeared to be a legitimate part of a job interview.

## Malicious software bypassed security defenses

According to Binance's preliminary findings, the malicious code exploited a vulnerability that was not known to security systems at the time of the attack. Such vulnerabilities are commonly described as *zero-day vulnerabilities* because the software vendor or security community has not yet provided an appropriate defense.

As a result, the compromise was not immediately detected. The attackers gained access to Employee B's work computer and were able to explore the internal environment. Because the employee had legitimate access to certain business tools, the attackers could operate within the system using that person's existing identity and permissions.

This aspect of the attack matters far beyond the cryptocurrency industry. Increasing numbers of developers rely on public code repositories, AI tools and external software packages, while downloading third-party code is part of their everyday work. Attackers can turn these routine activities into entry points into an organization, particularly when malicious content is delivered through an apparently trustworthy professional contact.

## Internal AI tool exploited to expand the attack

Binance stated that on October 20, after establishing access to the compromised computer, the attackers also exploited an internal artificial intelligence tool. According to the company's reconstruction, the tool became part of a broader process through which the attackers obtained access to additional devices and permissions.

During this phase, the computers of Employees A and C were compromised or brought under the attackers' control. The attackers also discovered an access token associated with an internal software container deployment system. Such a token can enable operations within an infrastructure without requiring credentials to be manually entered again.

It is important to distinguish the role of artificial intelligence from the actual cause of the attack. The available findings do not show that an AI system independently decided to steal funds or initiate a criminal operation. Instead, the attackers exploited the internal tool's functionality and access privileges to extend their control over the infrastructure.

Nevertheless, the incident raises serious questions about the security of AI agents in business environments. When an artificial intelligence tool can execute commands, access internal data or communicate with other systems, compromising an authorized user may have consequences far beyond gaining access to a single computer.

## How did the attackers reach billions of dollars on October 29?

The final phase of the attack took place on October 29. According to Binance, the attackers combined control over Employee C's device, credentials associated with Employee A and the capabilities of the compromised infrastructure to execute a malicious procedure targeting the cryptocurrency transaction signing system.

Transaction signing systems are among the most sensitive components of a cryptocurrency platform's infrastructure because they authorize transfers of digital assets. If an attacker manages to influence such a system, the consequences can be far more serious than an ordinary leak of customer data.

The total value of the stolen assets was estimated at approximately **$6.34 billion**, with the attack involving several cryptocurrencies, including Bitcoin, Ethereum, XRP, Solana and other tokens. Binance also stated that the attackers used mechanisms associated with Ethereum's EIP-7702 standard, making certain activities more difficult to stop.

The investigation showed that the theft was not the result of one accidental click immediately before the incident. The attackers had spent months preparing the operation, gathering information about employees with the necessary privileges and gradually building access to the infrastructure required for the final attack.

## Binance promises to compensate affected users

Following the incident, Binance announced that it had taken measures to block and trace stolen assets. According to its preliminary November 3 report, approximately **$2.85 billion** had been frozen or intercepted, while the company had secured funds to cover customer losses.

Binance stated that it held compensation reserves exceeding the estimated amount required to reimburse affected users. By the time the report was published, approximately 98% of the affected tokens had been replenished or otherwise secured, according to the company's figures. These numbers represent Binance's preliminary disclosures and do not mean that all stolen assets had been recovered from the attackers.

The US Federal Bureau of Investigation linked the attack to actors associated with North Korea. That attribution is consistent with previously documented cryptocurrency theft operations connected to North Korean groups, which have also used fake professional contacts, job offers and compromised coding assignments.

However, an official attribution should be distinguished from a final judicial determination of responsibility. Binance explicitly described its report as preliminary, meaning that additional investigative findings could change some details of the reconstructed sequence of events.

## Why is this attack particularly important for the AI industry?

The traditional image of a cyberattack often involves a hacker attempting to guess a password, exploit a security vulnerability or send a fraudulent email. In this case, the attackers combined several different approaches while carefully studying the everyday working practices of the employees they targeted.

They first targeted a developer installing an AI tool, then another employee through a fake recruitment process, and subsequently exploited compromised devices and an internal AI system. Each stage increased their capabilities for the next, until the attackers reached infrastructure associated with authorizing financial transactions.

What makes the case especially concerning is that job interviews and practical coding assignments are entirely normal in the technology industry. Candidates frequently receive links to GitHub repositories or instructions to install development tools, and they may have limited ability to determine whether the supplied code is safe.

The development of AI agents adds another layer of complexity to cybersecurity defenses. Organizations increasingly give automated tools permission to read files, execute commands and connect to internal systems. Unless those permissions are carefully restricted, an attacker who compromises one user may gain access to substantially more functionality than would have been available in a traditional business environment.

For this reason, the incident is not simply a story about cryptocurrency security. It highlights how rapidly the boundaries between human decisions, automated processes and access to critical infrastructure are changing.

## Our Take (Metaadvisor)

* **The most dangerous part of the attack was not a single security flaw but months of preparation.** After the first detected attempt, the attackers continued gathering information about employees and adapting their strategy. Removing malicious software therefore does not necessarily mean that a broader operation has ended.
* **Fake job interviews are becoming a serious security risk for technology professionals.** Developers are accustomed to downloading code and completing practical assignments during recruitment. That makes this technique potentially more effective than conventional messages directly requesting passwords.
* **AI tools increase productivity, but they can also magnify the consequences of compromised access.** Artificial intelligence did not independently organize this attack, yet an internal AI tool formed part of the chain through which the attackers expanded their control. AI agent security therefore requires strict access limits, monitoring of executed commands and a clear separation of privileges.
* **Compensating customers does not eliminate questions about infrastructure security.** Binance's reimbursement announcement is important for user confidence, but it is equally important to understand how a months-long operation managed to reach transaction signing systems.
* **The central lesson concerns trust within digital workflows.** The attackers did not need to directly overcome every security barrier. They exploited legitimate working habits, professional contacts and existing employee permissions. As AI tools take on more responsibilities, identity verification, code provenance and clearly defined access boundaries become increasingly important.

This incident demonstrates that the future of cybersecurity will not depend solely on stronger passwords, antivirus software and network protection. Equally important will be organizations' ability to recognize when an apparently legitimate business activity is becoming part of a carefully prepared attack.
