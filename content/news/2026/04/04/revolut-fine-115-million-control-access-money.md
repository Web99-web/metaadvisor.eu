## When access to money is no longer secure

The Italian regulator fined Revolut €11.5 million over the way it communicates with users and manages certain account functions. At first glance, this looks like just another regulatory fine in the fintech sector. But behind this decision lies a much deeper issue that is increasingly affecting both users and small businesses — who actually controls access to money today?

According to the authority’s findings, some users faced account restrictions without clear explanations, combined with delays in communication and limited access to support. In some cases, users did not know why the restriction was applied, how long it would last, or what they needed to do to regain access to their own funds.

Revolut disagrees with the fine and has announced an appeal, stating that it operates in line with regulations and that such measures are part of protecting users and the financial system. This defense is not unusual, as financial institutions today rely heavily on automated systems for fraud detection and anti-money laundering.

But this is where the core issue emerges. The problem is not that accounts are being restricted — it is how this is done, and what happens afterward. If a user loses access to funds without a clear explanation and without timely communication, this is no longer just a security measure — it becomes total control over access.

For large companies, a €11.5 million fine may be almost negligible — a cost of doing business. But for a user whose account is restricted, the consequences are immediate and very real. For small businesses, this can mean halted operations, inability to pay suppliers or employees, and even a complete shutdown.

## “Not your keys, not your coins” — and beyond

The most well-known phrase in crypto is: **“Not your keys, not your coins.”** It means that if you do not control the private keys of your wallet, you do not truly control your money. If your funds are held on a platform, they are technically not under your direct control.

It is important not to confuse private keys with a password — they are not the same. A password grants access to an account, while a private key represents actual ownership and control of the funds.

A similar principle exists in traditional banking. Money held in a bank is not physically “your money” in a strict legal sense, but a claim against the bank. In other words, the bank is your debtor — it owes you that amount.

## AI moderation and the disappearance of digital identity

This story does not exist in isolation. A similar pattern is already visible on social networks, where AI moderation can shut down user accounts without warning, explanation, or a real chance to appeal. In that moment, years of work, contacts, and communication disappear.

When AI moderation shuts down an account, Facebook, Instagram, and Messenger are often all disabled at once. You don’t just lose posts — you lose clients, contacts, and communication channels… years of hard work.

No explanation. No human support. No real appeal.

The difference between access to money and access to a digital profile is becoming smaller. In both cases, what is lost is not just a tool — but access to your business.

The question remains the same: who controls access — and who is accountable when it is taken away?
