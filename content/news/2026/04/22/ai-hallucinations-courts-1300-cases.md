# AI hallucinations in courts: a problem that is no longer theoretical

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being integrated into professional workflows, but recent data shows how risky that can be. **More than 1300 cases of AI hallucinations in court proceedings** have been recorded, with **over 900 in the United States alone**. This is no longer a series of isolated mistakes, but a pattern pointing to a systemic issue.

A recent example comes from the prominent US law firm **Sullivan & Cromwell**, which had to formally apologize to a court after submitting a filing containing around **40 incorrect citations and legal references**. Although internal AI usage policies existed, they were not followed in this case.

The result was a document that appeared credible, but was built on information that simply did not exist.

## How AI “hallucinates” and why it matters

The problem is not just inaccuracy, but how these errors are generated. AI produces outputs that are **structurally correct, logical and convincing**, even when they are entirely wrong. This combination makes them particularly dangerous, especially in environments like the legal system where precision is critical.

Another issue is that it is often **unclear which specific tool was used**, but previous cases have shown that generative AI systems, when not properly verified, can easily fabricate case law, citations and arguments.

AI does not signal uncertainty like humans do. When it makes mistakes, it does so confidently, increasing the risk that those errors go unnoticed, especially in professional environments.

## AI hallucinations and moderation: when algorithms erase people

AI hallucinations are not a new topic. For months, we have been tracking how this issue extends beyond the legal system — from deleted emails and broken workflows to unintended changes in code. However, one of the most sensitive areas is **AI moderation on social platforms**, where decisions are increasingly made without human oversight.

In such systems, so-called **“false positive” decisions** occur when AI incorrectly flags users or harmless content as problematic. The result is often account suspension or deletion without clear explanation.

According to available data, around **63,000 users** have signed a petition claiming that their accounts on platforms like Meta (Facebook and Instagram) were suddenly disabled since 2025 — without explanation, without valid reason, and without access to real human support.

The consequences are severe. While AI may generate a wrong citation in court, on social media it can **erase long-standing digital identities** — accounts built over 10 or even 20 years. **Access to communication, business operations and income can disappear instantly.** Even more concerning, affected users often **lose the ability to appeal**, effectively becoming invisible in the digital space.

## The bigger picture: trust in AI is being tested

This trend raises a critical question: **how much can we trust systems that do not know when they are wrong?** Media outlets such as Reuters and Bloomberg Law have already emphasized that responsibility must remain with humans, regardless of technological progress.

In practice, however, the opposite is often happening — people are increasingly trusting AI while verifying it less.

In theory, solutions exist: internal controls, verification processes and clear policies. But in reality, they are often not consistently applied — whether due to time pressure, efficiency demands or overconfidence in the technology.

In a world where AI can generate legal documents, moderate content and make decisions that directly impact people’s lives, this is no longer just a technical issue. It is a matter of responsibility, trust and control.
