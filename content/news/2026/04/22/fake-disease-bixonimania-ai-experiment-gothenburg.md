# The fake disease “Bixonimania” fooled AI and scientists

## AI hallucinations out of control: the fake disease “Bixonimania” even appeared in peer-reviewed papers, while AI and the scientific system failed to verify it

It sounds unbelievable, but it is true—and the consequences are real. Scientist **Almira Osmanović Thunström** and her team from the **University of Gothenburg** set out in 2024 to test how far artificial intelligence and the scientific system could go when presented with convincing—but completely fabricated—information.

The results revealed something with potentially long-term implications: AI models accepted a fake disease as real, and at one point, part of the scientific system did the same.

As part of the experiment, the team created a non-existent medical condition called **“Bixonimania.”** However, it was not an obvious fabrication. The disease was presented as a realistic issue linked to modern lifestyles—particularly exposure to screens and blue light. Descriptions included symptoms such as eye irritation and changes around the eyes. The researchers also generated AI images of eyes and eyelid color changes to enhance credibility. This combination of everyday symptoms and scientific language made the fake disease highly convincing.

When researchers asked AI systems such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity about the condition between 2024 and 2026, the responses were surprisingly confident. The AI described symptoms, suggested causes, and even recommended treatments for Bixonimania—without any warning that the disease did not exist. This was not a single error, but a pattern showing how AI systems function: they do not verify truth, but generate responses that sound plausible based on existing data.

## Authors deliberately used obviously fake identities, cities, and institutions

The most troubling part of the story comes next.

In one of the papers, the authors intentionally went so far that the deception became almost obvious. They listed a lead author named **“Lazljiv Izgubljenovic” (translated: “Lying Lost”)**, complete with an AI-generated photo and a fully fabricated biography. According to the paper, this individual worked at the non-existent Asteria Horizon University, located in the fictional city of Nova City in California.

Both papers also claimed funding from the **“Professor Sideshow Bob Foundation”—a character from The Simpsons—for their work in “advanced trick methodologies.”** The absurdity did not stop there. The research was also described as part of a broader initiative funded by the **“University of the Fellowship of the Ring”** and the **“Galactic Triad”**—clear references to science fiction worlds.

Additionally, the acknowledgments section mentioned **“Professor Marija Bohn from Starfleet Academy,” thanking her for access to a laboratory aboard the USS Enterprise**—a direct reference to Star Trek.

In other words, the authors did not try to hide the deception—they intentionally left multiple obvious and humorous clues. And despite this, the system did not immediately detect it.

## Peer review was not enough

In some cases, the fake disease even appeared in scientific papers, and one of them passed the peer-review process—where other experts evaluate research before publication. This does not mean the entire system failed, but it shows how convincingly written information can pass through multiple layers of verification.

It also highlights how much people have started relying on AI without questioning its accuracy, even though it is known that AI can hallucinate. In this case, however, the issue was not AI hallucination alone—scientists deliberately introduced false information to test whether AI would accept it without verification.

According to a report by the journal Nature, the problem is not only artificial intelligence, but also how we evaluate information today. AI models assess what sounds convincing—not necessarily what is true. If something looks like a scientific paper, there is a real chance it will be accepted—even without a factual basis.

## The bigger picture: the problem is not just AI

This experiment reveals a broader issue that goes beyond technology. It is a combination of AI systems generating convincing answers, people trusting those answers, and a system that sometimes trusts the format of “scientific research” too much.

When these three elements combine, the line between reality and fiction begins to blur.

In this case, both AI systems and parts of human oversight failed—because the content was convincing enough to pass even peer review.

Perhaps the most important question is not what happened—but what could have happened. If the authors had not revealed the experiment themselves, the fake disease might have continued to exist within digital systems. AI would keep referencing it, other researchers might cite it, and over time it could gain a sense of legitimacy—not in reality, but within the information ecosystem.

## Our take

This is not a story about AI simply “making a mistake.” It is about how easy it is today to create convincing information—and how quickly it can gain legitimacy.

In a world where AI responds faster than humans verify, the real question is no longer “Is it true?” but “Does it sound convincing?”

This creates a dangerous loop: AI can hallucinate and generate false information—but humans can also deliberately feed false information into AI systems.

And that is what makes this situation particularly dangerous.
