# Why does a 15–20 year old Facebook profile have no reputation credit?

It appears that among the profiles permanently banned or removed from the Meta Platforms Facebook platform, the most common cases are not new or suspicious accounts — but exactly the opposite: long-term users, the pioneers of the platform.

These are people who joined early, explored new features, invited others to join, and built networks and communities. People who created and managed groups, moderated discussions, invested time, and produced content that fueled the platform for years. These are the profiles that are now disappearing — without a clear explanation, without a written response, and without a real possibility of appeal.

👉 **If Facebook is a “social network” — why is it removing the very foundations of that society from the network?**

![](/images/Meta/Meta-wrongfully-disabling-accounts-Facebook-Instagram.png)

In real life, reputation is built over years. If someone has acted correctly for 15 or 20 years and has tens of thousands or even millions of interactions without incidents, we expect the system to trust them more than someone who appeared yesterday. On Facebook, however, that logic seems not to exist. A profile that is two decades old can be treated the same as a profile that is three days old. And this is where the inconsistency begins.

The platform owned by Mark Zuckerberg has for years promoted “community standards” and safety. But safety without context turns into automated rigidity. If a long-term user writes a single sentence that triggers a certain “risk pattern,” the system reacts regardless of 15–20 years of clean history. Thousands of posts carry no weight; one formulation does.

## Why the algorithm ignores behavioral history

This raises a key question: why does the algorithm not take into account the percentage of behavior? In the world of data, calculating a reputation ratio is trivial. If 99.999% of posts complied with the rules, it is logical that a single exception should not carry the same weight as it would for a completely new profile. Yet the system is designed to react to events, not history. In other words, it does not ask “who you were for 20 years,” but “what you just wrote.”

## AI moderation and “false positives” — what is it?

Another issue that is rarely discussed is the so-called “false positive” — a situation where the system flags something as a violation even though it actually isn’t.

In other words, it is possible that the user did nothing wrong at all. The AI moderation simply failed to understand the content.

Algorithms often fail to understand context, irony, tone, or the nature of a discussion. This leads to incorrect labeling — for example, users may be flagged as promoting drugs even when they are discussing the topic in a warning or analytical context, or as offering loans and financial services even though their posts never mention money, interest rates, or any kind of offer.

In such situations, the problem is not user behavior — but system interpretation. AI does not “understand” content on a human level; it classifies it based on patterns. When those patterns misfire, the result can be serious consequences: labeling, restrictions, or even permanent account removal without a real reason.

This further confirms how dangerous it is to rely solely on automated moderation without human oversight — because a misinterpretation can have real and long-lasting consequences for real people.

## A new pattern: mass bans of long-term users

In recent months, another pattern has emerged: mass removals of users from the United States, Canada, and the Philippines. What is concerning is not just the scale — but the structure of affected users. These are people who were active, who built communities, and who were visible. In other words, exactly those who contributed the most to the platform.

## A system without a real appeal process

The problem is further amplified by the fact that the system does not offer a real path for appeal. In theory, there are protective mechanisms and a European framework such as the Digital Services Act, as well as institutions like the Appeals Centre Europe. In practice, many users do not receive any written response for months and cannot reach human support. The automated decision remains final. Users from the United States report that they have not received any response since 2025, when they were suddenly removed from Facebook.

![](/images/Meta/Disabled-Facebook-Messenger.png)

## AI moderation without accountability

Supporters of strict automation argue that reputation credit could open the door to abuse. But this argument is incomplete. Introducing reputation does not mean immunity. It can be a factor that reduces system sensitivity or introduces an additional human review before sanctions are applied. That layer is currently missing.

It is especially problematic that algorithms do not understand tone, irony, or the context of public discussion. Criticism, sarcasm, or pointing out irregularities can trigger the same signal as actual violations. At that point, a long-term user has no advantage. Behavioral history seems to not exist.

## The platform is penalizing what it once encouraged

This brings us to a crucial point: the platform has actively encouraged this exact behavior for years — users built communities, managed groups, moderated discussions, and developed networks around shared interests. They were allowed to manage multiple pages under one profile, share different types of content, and actively participate in content distribution. This activity was the foundation of the platform’s growth.

At the same time, these users were not only content creators — they were also the audience. For years, they were exposed to thousands of ads, purchased products and services, and participated in the platform’s economy through their attention and engagement. In other words, they did not just build the network — they were an active part of it.

Today, however, these same behavior patterns — especially among users who post more and are more active — can be interpreted as risky.

## The question of responsibility

This leads to the question of responsibility. AI moderation did not emerge on its own. Someone designed it, someone implemented it, and someone approved it. If a system systematically removes long-term users without clear explanation and without a functional appeal process, then this is no longer a technical issue — but a governance issue.

## Conclusion

In the end, a fundamental question remains: is equal treatment of all profiles truly fair? Treating an anonymous account and a 20-year-old profile the same may appear neutral, but in practice it ignores context and continuity. A system that does not recognize reputation sends a clear message: long-term responsibility has no value.

If the goal is a stable and healthy community, reputation must have a role. Not as immunity from sanctions, but as a signal of trust. Otherwise, long-term users are justified in asking: why did we build a history for years if it has no value at the moment when the algorithm decides?

This is not a sentimental question. This is a question of system logic.

If data exists, if history exists, and if statistics are measurable — why are they not used? Do they only want silent users and ad consumers? Or AI bots?

In a digital space governed by technology, ignoring reputation is not a limitation.

It is a decision.

And every such decision has consequences. We want Facebook to remain a social network in which society actively participates.
