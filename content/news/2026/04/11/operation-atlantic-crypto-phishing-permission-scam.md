# Operation Atlantic: $12 million frozen and 20,000 victims — a new crypto scam that doesn’t ask for money, but permission

The international operation known as Operation Atlantic has exposed the scale of a new generation of crypto scams affecting tens of thousands of users across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada. In the coordinated effort, authorities managed to freeze more than $12 million, while estimates suggest that total losses may exceed $45 million.

Unlike traditional scams, where attackers attempt to convince users to send funds directly, this operation revealed a more sophisticated model. Victims were not tricked into sending money but instead unknowingly granted permissions that allowed attackers to access their wallets.

A key element of the operation was the involvement of the private sector. Binance also participated, assisting in identifying suspicious transactions and helping freeze part of the funds, highlighting how cooperation between institutions and the crypto industry has become essential in tackling these threats.

## How a scam that doesn’t look like a scam works

Unlike older phishing models that rely on suspicious emails or direct messages, victims in this case often encountered attackers through websites that appeared entirely legitimate. These included replicas of well-known DeFi platforms, NFT projects, or investment applications.

On these sites, users were presented with standard options such as “Connect wallet,” “Approve access,” or “Claim rewards.” Everything appeared to be part of normal crypto usage, with no obvious warning signs.

At that moment, the critical mistake occurred. By signing an approval transaction, users believed they were performing a harmless authorization. In reality, they were granting attackers permission to manage specific tokens within their wallets, allowing funds to be drained gradually without further interaction.

## A fraud network operating at scale

The investigation showed that this was not an isolated incident but part of a broader network of coordinated scams. More than 20,000 victims were identified, with numerous fraudulent websites operating simultaneously.

These operations increasingly function as organized systems, where tools, designs, and infrastructure are shared among different groups. As a result, phishing has evolved from individual attempts into a scalable global industry.

## Why this type of scam is more dangerous

The biggest issue with these attacks is that they do not appear to be scams. There is no pressure, no obvious threat, and no request to send funds. Everything looks like a legitimate interaction with a crypto application.

Users are accustomed to confirming transactions and permissions quickly, often without reviewing the details. This behavior becomes the main vulnerability. Once permission is granted, blockchain systems treat it as legitimate, with no easy way to reverse the action.

This is what makes these attacks so effective. The line between legitimate use and fraud has nearly disappeared, and security now depends heavily on user awareness.

## What has changed in fighting crypto fraud

Operation Atlantic also demonstrates a shift in how crypto crime is being addressed. Instead of reacting after funds are lost, authorities are increasingly attempting to intervene while attacks are still ongoing.

Through blockchain analysis and cooperation with exchanges, including Binance, compromised wallets were identified, and in some cases, users were warned before losing funds. This marks a transition toward proactive protection.

## Conclusion

Operation Atlantic makes it clear that crypto scams are entering a new phase. Attackers no longer need users to send money — they only need permission.

For users, this means security is no longer just about who they trust, but also about what they sign. In decentralized finance, the most expensive mistake is no longer a wrong transaction, but a wrong authorization.
