# US opens compensation for OneCoin victims: only a fraction of billions will be returned

The US Department of Justice (DOJ – Department of Justice, federal law enforcement authority) has launched a compensation process for victims of one of the largest financial frauds in modern history — OneCoin. Official estimates suggest at least **$4 billion** was lost, while some analyses place total damages as high as **$19 billion**.

Currently, around **$40 million** is available for compensation — only a small fraction of the total losses suffered by millions of investors worldwide. As Metaadvisor highlights, this case remains one of the clearest examples of how large-scale financial fraud can operate globally.

## A scam marketed as a “Bitcoin killer”

OneCoin was launched in **2014 in Bulgaria as a supposed cryptocurrency designed to surpass Bitcoin**. The project was led by **Ruja Ignatova** and **Karl Sebastian Greenwood**, with a later key role played by her brother **Konstantin Ignatov**.

Ignatova, widely known as the **“Cryptoqueen”**, became the public face of the project. However, in **2017 she disappeared without a trace** and has not been seen since. After her disappearance, her brother Konstantin Ignatov took over parts of the operation, was later arrested in the US and pleaded guilty, while Greenwood was sentenced to **20 years in prison**.

## A global Ponzi scheme with no real technology

Despite its promises, what makes this case particularly striking is that **OneCoin never had a real blockchain or functional technology behind it**. The value of its “coin” was not market-driven but internally controlled.

The system operated as a classic Ponzi scheme, where new investors funded payouts to earlier participants. Revenue was generated through the sale of so-called educational packages and aggressive recruitment strategies.

## Millions of victims across more than 100 countries

It is estimated that more than three million people across over 100 countries were affected. The majority of victims were in Europe, Asia, and Africa, but investors also came from the United States.

Official figures point to more than $4 billion in losses, while some analyses suggest damages could reach up to $19 billion, making this one of the largest fraud cases ever recorded. As suspicions about the legitimacy of the project grew, central banks and regulators worldwide began issuing warnings, followed by large-scale international investigations.

US authorities, including the Department of Justice, launched criminal cases against key individuals. During these proceedings, assets linked to the fraud were seized — including bank accounts, real estate, and other holdings. These seized assets now form the basis for compensation payments.

## The brother’s role and the collapse

After Ruja Ignatova disappeared in 2017, her brother Konstantin Ignatov took over leadership of the project and became one of the central figures during its final phase.

Ignatov was arrested in 2019 in the United States, pleaded guilty, and cooperated with authorities, which led to a significantly reduced sentence compared to other participants.

## Compensation begins — but remains limited

The Department of Justice states that **anyone who purchased OneCoin between 2014 and 2019 and suffered losses is eligible to file a claim**. Prosecutors emphasize that this is an important step toward justice, but acknowledge that no compensation can fully recover the scale of the damage.

The funds available for distribution come from seized assets connected to the scheme. However, most of the money was never recovered or has been permanently lost.

The available $40 million represents only a symbolic portion of the total losses, meaning that most victims will recover only a small part — if any — of their original investment.

## Conclusion

OneCoin remains one of the clearest examples of how marketing, trust, and lack of regulation can combine into a global financial disaster.

The compensation process has begun — but for most victims, the majority of lost funds will likely never be recovered. As Metaadvisor continues to follow such cases, the lesson remains clear: understanding technology is essential before investing.
