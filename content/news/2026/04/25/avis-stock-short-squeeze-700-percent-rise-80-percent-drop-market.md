# AVIS +700% then -80%: how a car rental company became a market extreme

## It wasn’t business growth — it was a market collision

At first glance, this looks like another story of an explosive stock rally. But behind Avis’ price movement, there was no technological breakthrough, no business turnaround, and no surprising earnings. It is a classic car rental company with no fundamental change in its operations — which makes the situation even more unusual.

That is exactly why this story is attracting attention: the price surge was not driven by value creation, but by an extreme imbalance within the market itself.

## The combination that creates extremes

According to available data, two large institutional investors — RS Investment and Pentwater Capital — controlled more than **71% of the freely traded float**. At the same time, approximately **86% of that same float was shorted**, meaning it was used for bets on a price decline.

Such a structure creates a highly unstable environment. A large number of market participants depend on buying shares to close their positions, while the supply of those shares is limited. At that point, the market stops functioning as a traditional price discovery mechanism and enters a zone of extreme pressure.

## What actually happened

In such conditions, even a relatively small price move can trigger a chain reaction. As the price began to rise, short sellers were forced to close their positions by buying shares on the market. That additional demand pushed the price even higher, forcing more participants to buy.

The result was dramatic: the stock jumped from around **$107 to over $850**, representing a gain of more than **700%** in a short period. This rise did not reflect a change in the company’s business — it was driven by market mechanics known as a short squeeze.

## And then came the drop

Once the pressure from forced buying began to fade and large players started exiting their positions, the market quickly reversed. Shares that had previously risen almost vertically began to fall sharply.

Within just a few days, the stock dropped by approximately **80%**, wiping out a large portion of the previous gains. Investors who entered late, driven by price momentum and headlines, were left trapped at the top.

## What this story shows

This case clearly demonstrates that, under certain conditions, markets can stop reflecting a company’s real value and instead become the result of pure mathematical interaction between supply and demand.

When a small number of players control most of the supply, and the other side of the market is forced to buy, prices can escalate without any connection to fundamentals. In such conditions, the market does not act as a valuation system — it becomes a pressure system.

## Our view (Metaadvisor)

* the 700% rise was driven by market structure, not business performance
* the 80% drop shows how quickly such extremes can collapse
* high short interest combined with concentrated supply creates instability
* these situations are not necessarily manipulation, but they carry high risk
* the key factor is not information, but positioning in the market
