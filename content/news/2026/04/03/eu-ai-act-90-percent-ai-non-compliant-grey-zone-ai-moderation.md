# Up to 90% of AI systems in Europe may already be breaking the law

**The EU AI Act does not apply only to European companies — but to all those whose AI systems affect users within the European Union, regardless of where they are registered.** That is why estimates suggesting that up to 90% of AI applications being developed in Europe today may already be non-compliant with the EU AI Act carry additional weight.

The problem starts very early. **The moment AI processes data, interacts with users, or influences decisions, it enters the legal framework.** This means that even simple tools such as chatbots, automations, or AI features within applications may have regulatory obligations — regardless of how “small” they appear.

## Where most companies fail

In practice, most organizations lack the basic elements required for compliance. AI systems are used daily, but without documentation, without clear oversight, and without records that could explain their decisions.

Most teams:

* don’t know their risk classification  
* don’t have technical documentation (Art. 11)  
* don’t have logging & traceability (Art. 12)  
* don’t implement human oversight (Art. 14)  
* can’t prove compliance  

This means that when a regulator asks a simple question — “how did the AI make this decision?” — many companies have no answer.

This is where the key question emerges: **will regulation focus on smaller, easier-to-control systems, while missing the “forest” — large platforms whose AI systems, especially AI moderation, already make decisions affecting millions of users, often without explanation and without adequate human support?**

## A law without borders — it applies to all companies affecting the EU

One of the key parts of the EU AI Act clearly states:

> **“This Regulation applies to providers placing on the market or putting into service AI systems… irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within the Union or in a third country.”**

> **“This Regulation also applies to… providers or deployers located in a third country, if the output… is used in the Union.”**

In other words — the law follows impact, not location.

## AI in recruitment: decisions without explanation

One of the most visible areas is recruitment. AI systems increasingly filter CVs, rank candidates, and decide who even gets the chance for a human interview. These decisions directly affect people’s lives, yet often come without explanation, without the possibility of review, and without human involvement.

That is why the EU AI Act classifies such systems as high-risk. Alongside recruitment, the law also targets areas such as credit scoring and healthcare — sectors where AI can have a direct and measurable impact on individuals. In these cases, clear documentation, logging, and explainability are required.

## What happens outside those sectors?

While the focus of regulation is clear — recruitment, credit, healthcare — the real-world use of AI is already expanding far beyond those areas. This is where the problem begins, as the law does not yet fully cover these use cases.

AI is now used in systems that may not formally decide on careers or loans, but still significantly affect everyday life — visibility, communication, access to information, and business activity. And this often happens without the transparency standards required in high-risk categories.

---

## AI moderation: a growing problem without clear rules

**Since 2025 and 2026, more and more users worldwide have been reporting problems with AI moderation.** This includes accounts that are 15–20 years old, with thousands or tens of thousands of interactions. Systems increasingly flag content incorrectly (“false positives”), then remove posts or disable entire accounts without prior warning.

Platforms like Meta Platforms, through Facebook, Instagram, and Messenger, rely heavily on automated systems that make decisions without human review. In practice, this means that a user can lose access to all accounts at once — with no warning and no explanation.

The problem becomes even more severe afterward. Once removed from the system, users often have no direct access to human support. All attempts remain within automated systems, where users receive only pre-generated responses. Real answers are missing, as banned users can no longer reach human support. Even requests submitted through official EU channels such as **https://www.appealscentre.eu/** are delayed for weeks — if they arrive at all.

## Only 180 days to recover — or total loss of digital identity

**In many cases, users have around 180 days to recover their accounts. But without access to support, this becomes a paradox — like asking a wall: “Why was I thrown out?”** Without a clear reason for the ban and without a real appeal process involving human support, this deadline often has no real value.

This raises a critical question: if the law requires logging, transparency, and accountability for AI systems in business — why do the same standards not apply to systems that erase long-standing digital identities, businesses, and communication?

Why do large systems respond so slowly? Why are there no enforced deadlines for responses?

Today’s platforms are global infrastructures — and removing users effectively cuts them off from a large part of modern information flow.

---

## Conclusion

Regulation is trying to set rules, but there is a risk that it misses the forest for the trees. While focus is placed on individual, controllable use cases, systems with the greatest impact on millions of people remain without real transparency.

---

## MetaAdvisor take

The biggest issue with AI today is not just the technology, but the imbalance in accountability. If regulation is applied selectively — strictly to smaller systems and more loosely to large platforms — then the real risk will remain exactly where the impact is greatest.
