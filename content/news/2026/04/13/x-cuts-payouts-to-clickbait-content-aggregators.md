# X cuts payouts to clickbait content aggregators

The platform X (formerly Twitter) has started reducing payouts to accounts that flood the timeline with clickbait content and rapid news aggregation, signaling a clear shift in its monetization strategy. The move was confirmed by Head of Product Nikita Bier, who stated that aggregators have already seen payouts reduced to around 60% in the current cycle, with further cuts expected in the next one.

This change follows a period in which exactly this model was encouraged. Monetization based on views and engagement allowed some accounts to generate significant income almost entirely through content volume, without the need for original work or deeper analysis. This situation increasingly frustrated original creators and more serious content producers.

## A model that created its own problem

In practice, clickbait aggregators functioned as continuous streams of information. They published dozens, sometimes hundreds of reposts daily, often using videos and news that had already circulated widely. In many cases, the content was simply taken and reposted without additional context or commentary.

Let’s be honest — part of this content is still enjoyed by audiences. Many videos that have circulated online for years still manage to trigger reactions such as laughter, shock, or disbelief. Those reactions are what make content go viral, and virality was the key driver of monetization.

In that sense, the relationship between the platform and content aggregators worked as a kind of “win-win”: the platform gained traffic and engagement, while creators increased their chances of earning by posting at scale. The problem emerged when the volume of such content began to dominate the feed, pushing original creators into the background and lowering overall content quality.

## “STOLEN” as a signal and a quiet protest

As payouts started to decline, reactions from affected accounts quickly became visible. Some began labeling their posts with “STOLEN” or “STOLEN NEWS,” but this move appears to be more than just an attempt at transparency.

It increasingly looks like a form of quiet protest.

Creators are effectively saying that their method hasn’t changed — they are still posting the same type of content — but the platform has changed the rules overnight. In other words, what was rewarded yesterday is now being penalized.

This position is clearly reflected in statements from some accounts. One argues that anyone sharing media clips is essentially an aggregator and that this model has long been a fundamental part of the platform. According to this view, rewarding only original content would drastically limit who can participate in monetization.

![Example of a content aggregator post on X](/images/news/X-clickbait.png)

In another post, Dom Lucre emphasizes that mainstream media frequently pick up his content, arguing that the issue is not clickbait itself, but the way information is presented to capture attention.

![Dom Lucre explaining his content model](/images/news/X-clickbait-Dom-Lucre.png)

## Changing the rules mid-game

The key issue is not that the platform deliberately chose this model, but that it emerged naturally. Users quickly realized they didn’t need to create original content to succeed — redistributing existing content at scale was enough to gain reach.

During growth phases, platforms need activity and engagement, and this type of content delivered exactly that. The logic was simple: out of many posts, one would always go viral — and that one would generate most of the income.

According to available information and creator statements, such models could generate earnings of up to $55,000 per year. At the same time, attention-grabbing labels like “BREAKING” proved highly effective for years without major restrictions.

Now, as X attempts to limit this approach, a clear tension is emerging between the platform and creators. While X maintains that it will not restrict reach or freedom of expression, it is also making it clear that it will no longer financially reward system manipulation.

## The bigger picture: monetization, not visibility

This shift primarily raises questions about how content is monetized, rather than what users see. X continues to operate through an algorithm that distributes content based on user interests, but it is now adjusting how that content is financially rewarded.

Reducing payouts to clickbait aggregators does not mean such content will disappear — it simply means it will be less financially incentivized. At the same time, it remains unclear how effectively the system can distinguish between high-quality content and content optimized purely for attention.

The line between viral and valuable content remains blurred. The real test is still ahead: will the algorithm successfully recognize and reward original creators, or will it simply redefine the rules without changing the outcome?

## A new reality for creators

For many accounts that relied on speed and volume, this shift represents a significant drop in income. At the same time, it opens the door to a different approach — one focused more on originality, context, and real value.

However, a key question remains: will quality truly be rewarded, or will the system simply rebalance itself without greater transparency?
