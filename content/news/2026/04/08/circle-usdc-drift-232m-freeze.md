# Circle and USDC under scrutiny — when speed becomes the problem

After the Drift Protocol exploit shook the DeFi sector, the focus is no longer only on the attack itself, but increasingly on the response — or the lack of it. At the center of attention is Circle, the issuer of the USDC stablecoin, through which a significant portion of the compromised funds passed.

According to available on-chain data and analyses within the crypto community, around $232 million in USDC linked to the Drift incident moved through the system within a relatively short period of time — without being frozen in time. This time window is becoming crucial for understanding the entire case.

## Six hours that raised a major question

Analyses show that attackers executed more than one hundred transactions over approximately six hours, converting USDC into Ethereum. This pattern is not unusual in attacks — ETH is often used because it allows greater flexibility for further transfers and reduces the risk of funds being frozen.

For that reason, many in the industry are asking: if the transactions were visible and intervention was possible, why was the response not faster? This is not about a single transaction or a few seconds — it is about hours of activity happening publicly on the blockchain.

## Is it even possible to monitor everything

It is important to understand the technical reality. Systems like USDC process an enormous number of transactions — often millions per day. It is not possible to automatically classify every transaction in real time as legitimate or illicit without risking errors.

Circle and similar companies use blockchain analytics tools, track suspicious patterns and collaborate with researchers and institutions. However, many transactions initially appear normal — only later does it become clear that they were part of an attack.

## Where technology ends and responsibility begins

Critics, however, emphasize that this case is not a typical “gray zone.” According to analyses circulating within the community, there were moments when it was clear that the funds were compromised. It is precisely in those moments that a response is expected.

This leads to the key question: if a centralized stablecoin has the ability to freeze funds, at what point does that ability become an obligation? Because USDC is not a fully decentralized system — it includes control, and control implies responsibility.

{{< support2 >}}

## The paradox of stablecoins

USDC is often presented as a stable and secure bridge between traditional finance and crypto. Yet this combination of decentralized infrastructure and centralized control creates a paradox.

If funds can be frozen, users expect protection. If they are not frozen — trust is weakened. At the same time, a reaction that is too fast or incorrect can block legitimate users and create legal risks.

Balancing speed, accuracy and responsibility is becoming increasingly difficult.

## The bigger picture: more than a single incident

The Drift case is not isolated. Similar situations have occurred before, further intensifying the debate about the role of centralized actors in crypto. According to analyses within the community, Circle has failed to freeze a total of around $420 million linked to various hacks and fraud cases since 2022, placing this issue in a much broader context than a single incident. As more capital moves through blockchain systems, pressure on such companies continues to grow.

At the same time, part of these flows has been linked to organized crime and even actors associated with states such as North Korea. In that context, the issue of freezing funds moves beyond a technical question and into the realm of security and geopolitics.

## What comes next

In the coming period, further analysis and potential industry responses are expected. Circle has not yet provided a detailed response for each individual case, but the discussion within the community is already intensifying.

Incidents like this are becoming a test of maturity for the entire crypto system. It is no longer enough to have functional technology — it is necessary to clearly define who takes responsibility and when.

* Centralized systems offer control — but with it comes responsibility
* Perfect detection is unrealistic, but a response in clear cases is expected
* Trust in stablecoins will depend on how such incidents are handled
* The key question: where is the line between technical capability and real responsibility?
