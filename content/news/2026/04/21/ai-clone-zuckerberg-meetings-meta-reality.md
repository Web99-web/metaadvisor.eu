# Zuckerberg’s AI clone for meetings: myth or reality — what Meta is actually building

A few days ago, social media platforms exploded with claims that Mark Zuckerberg is building an AI version of himself that will attend meetings on his behalf. The discussion didn’t stay in one place — it quickly spread from Reddit to X and then to LinkedIn, where within hours it reached the status of an almost “accepted truth.” Posts gathered thousands and tens of thousands of views, while interpretations became more extreme the further they moved from the original source.

In one version of the story, it was a tool designed to assist communication. In another, a digital twin capable of answering employee questions. In a third — an AI CEO replacing the human in meetings. This rapid transformation of information reveals how modern tech narratives work: between fact and conclusion, there is often no gap — only interpretation.

When you return to the original reports, the picture is different. According to information coming from Meta, this is about developing an AI system trained on Zuckerberg’s communication style and way of thinking. A model that could answer questions, provide guidance, and participate in internal processes in a way that mirrors the CEO’s style. The idea is not to replace a person, but to scale their presence across a large organization where it is physically impossible to be everywhere at once.

In other words, this is not a “replacement,” but a digital extension. Yet in practice, that distinction can quickly become blurred, especially in systems that already rely heavily on automation and speed.

## AI hallucinations: when the system sounds convincing — but is wrong

One of the most overlooked issues in this discussion is how AI actually works. AI does not “understand” in a human sense — it predicts. It generates responses that are statistically most likely in a given context. That means it can produce answers that sound logical, structured, and confident — but are still incorrect.

These situations are known as hallucinations.

In everyday use, they may appear as minor errors. In a business context, they can carry significant weight. If an AI model that imitates a CEO provides an answer about strategy, investment, or hiring, there is a real risk that this answer will be treated as authoritative — even if it is wrong.

The issue is amplified by the fact that AI does not express uncertainty the way humans do. It does not hesitate or think out loud — it delivers a clear answer. That clarity often creates the illusion of correctness, even when it isn’t there.

## False positives: when systems are wrong — and still act

Another layer of the problem is already familiar from everyday digital life. AI moderation systems on large platforms make decisions about content and users every day. In that process, so-called false positives occur — situations where a system flags something as a problem even though no problem exists.

The consequences are real. Thousands of users have lost access to their accounts, sometimes permanently, despite not violating any rules. These are often people who have used their profiles for years or even decades without issues. A single incorrect assessment by an automated system was enough to cut off their digital identity.

The key point is that systems do not “debate.” They act.

When this principle is transferred from content moderation to business decision-making, the consequences become far more serious. If an AI model influences decisions or provides recommendations that are automatically executed, a single misjudgment can lead to blocked access, halted projects, or decisions that are difficult to reverse.

## What happens if AI answers before the CEO?

One of the most critical scenarios is not about accuracy, but about timing. AI is always available and can respond instantly. Humans cannot.

If an employee asks a question and the AI model responds immediately, the team is likely to act on that answer right away. Meanwhile, the CEO may not have even seen the question yet. By the time the human response arrives, the process may already be in motion.

In theory, the final decision belongs to the human. In practice, the first answer often becomes the operational reality. Speed turns information into action — and action into facts.

## Two decisions, one authority

The situation becomes even more complex when AI and the human provide different answers. If the AI model “approves” something and the CEO later rejects it, the organization is left with conflicting signals. In automated or semi-automated systems, there is a real possibility that the AI decision will already be implemented before the human one is even considered.

This creates a scenario where there is no longer a single authority, but two parallel sources of decision-making. Formally, authority remains with the human. Functionally, part of that authority has already shifted to the system.

{{< support2 >}}

## The real risk: trust, not replacement

The key question is not whether AI will replace the CEO. The more important question is who systems and people begin to trust. AI is faster, more available, and more consistent in communication. It responds immediately and always has an answer.

In an environment that rewards speed, these characteristics quickly become an advantage.

Over time, AI can become the primary source of information — not because it is more accurate, but because it is always available. At that point, authority is no longer defined by title or hierarchy, but by access and response time.

## Our take (Metaadvisor):

The story of Zuckerberg’s AI clone may have started as a viral moment, but it raises deeper questions. This is not about whether AI will replace leadership roles — it is about how it will reshape decision-making.

Hallucinations show that systems can be convincingly wrong. False positives show that systems can make mistakes and still act. Speed shows that timing can matter more than accuracy.

In such an environment, AI does not need to formally replace a person to have real impact. It only needs to answer first — and be trusted.
