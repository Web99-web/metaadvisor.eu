## A brief drop that lasted as long as the headline itself

At the moment news of a possible ceasefire broke, the market reacted almost instantly. Brent crude fell from levels above 100 USD to around 91–97 USD, while WTI (West Texas Intermediate) dropped even further, approaching the 90–96 USD per barrel range.

But that decline lasted only as long as confidence in the ceasefire. As soon as the first doubts about its durability appeared, the market reversed sharply. Prices started rising again as if nothing had happened, confirming just how sensitive today’s markets are to political signals — even when they are unconfirmed.

Within the next 24 hours, as uncertainty around the ceasefire grew, prices recovered again. Brent moved back toward 97 USD, while WTI traded in the 97–99 USD range, erasing much of the earlier drop. At the time of writing, Brent is around 91 USD, while WTI is near 92 USD, further highlighting how fast and unpredictable these swings have become.

## The market no longer reacts to economics — but to headlines

What makes this event particularly striking is not just the volatility, but the speed of reaction. In the past, oil prices were primarily tied to supply and demand, industrial output, and global growth. Today, it increasingly appears that markets react first and foremost to headlines.

Geopolitical developments, especially in key energy regions, have an immediate impact. A single statement, a diplomatic signal, or even an unverified report can trigger billions of dollars in market movement within hours. This means the market no longer waits for confirmation — it reacts to expectations.

## Strait of Hormuz: a bottleneck for the global economy

At the center of the story is one of the world’s most critical energy chokepoints — the Strait of Hormuz. Roughly 20% of the world’s oil supply passes through it, making it a vital artery for global energy flows.

Any threat of disruption or restriction in this passage instantly creates upward pressure on prices. Even without an actual supply interruption, the mere possibility is enough to move the market. That is why every piece of news from the region carries disproportionate weight.

## Brent vs. WTI: differences that emerge in times of stress

Although often discussed together, Brent and WTI are not identical. Brent serves as the global benchmark, reflecting international markets, while WTI is more closely tied to the U.S. market and domestic conditions.

In times of stress, these differences become more visible. Short-term supply disruptions can create unusual situations where one benchmark reacts faster or more aggressively than the other. This adds another layer of complexity and shows how fragmented the market really is.

## Volatility as the new normal

A sharp drop followed by a rapid recovery within 24 hours is no longer an exception — it is becoming the rule. The oil market is starting to resemble high-volatility environments like crypto, where sudden moves are expected and stability is rare.

This shift has broader implications. Energy prices influence inflation, transportation costs, production, and ultimately consumer prices. When a core input like oil becomes unstable, the entire economic system becomes more sensitive.

## What does this mean going forward?

If current trends continue, oil prices will increasingly depend on political developments rather than traditional economic indicators. That makes forecasting more difficult and risk significantly higher.

For investors and businesses, this introduces a new level of uncertainty. Planning becomes harder, and reactions must be faster. In such an environment, access to information — and the ability to interpret it quickly — becomes a key advantage.
