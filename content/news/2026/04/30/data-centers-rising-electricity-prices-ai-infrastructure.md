## Data Centers and Rising Electricity Prices: What’s Really Behind the AI Boom?

As artificial intelligence rapidly expands into every part of the digital world, a much quieter — yet potentially far more serious — story is unfolding behind the scenes: the battle for energy. Data centers, especially those built for AI, are becoming the largest single consumers of electricity in the modern economy. What used to be a purely technological issue is now turning into a social, political, and economic one.

Large hyperscale data centers today consume between 20 and 100 megawatts continuously, while the newest AI-driven facilities exceed 500 megawatts and can even reach 1 gigawatt. At that scale, they can no longer be viewed as “just another industrial facility” — they are systems that rival entire cities in power consumption.

In theory, data centers bring investment, infrastructure development, and digital transformation. In practice, however, more and more local communities are asking a critical question: who actually pays for that transformation?

Not indirectly. Not theoretically.

## ⚡ When technology makes electricity more expensive

In states like Virginia, Georgia, and Arizona, a pattern is already emerging — one that is now being closely watched in Europe.

Data centers require massive investments in power infrastructure — new substations, high-voltage transmission lines, and stable energy sources that can operate 24/7. These investments are often carried out by energy companies such as Dominion Energy or Georgia Power, but the cost does not stop there.

Part of these costs is built into regulated electricity prices. In other words, the infrastructure built to support a large data center is often partially financed through the bills of all consumers.

At the same time, data centers receive tax incentives and favorable conditions to attract investment. Local governments gain projects, but the financial burden can be distributed across the wider population.

The result is a paradox: a technology meant to drive progress can, in some cases, increase the basic cost of living.

---

## 💸 How the cost is transferred to citizens

The electricity system is designed so that large infrastructure investments are distributed through regulated tariffs. This means grid expansion is not paid upfront, but over time — through the bills of all users.

➡️ **WHEN THE GRID IS EXPANDED FOR A DATA CENTER — THE COST IS SHARED BY EVERYONE.**

👉 **CITIZENS AND BUSINESSES CO-FINANCE THE INFRASTRUCTURE THROUGH THEIR ELECTRICITY BILLS.**

---

## ⚡ Subsidies: the hidden layer

To attract large investments, governments often offer tax breaks, lower energy prices, and infrastructure support.

On paper, this looks like an economic win. In reality:

👉 **SOMEONE HAS TO COVER THAT “DISCOUNT.”**

➡️ **MOST OFTEN — THE ENERGY SYSTEM.**
➡️ **AND THE SYSTEM IS FUNDED BY CITIZENS AND BUSINESSES.**

---

## 🔌 Energy has to come from somewhere

A large data center cannot run on unstable energy sources. It requires constant, reliable supply.

That means:

* building new power plants
* or purchasing more expensive energy on the market

👉 **THIS COST DOES NOT DISAPPEAR.**

➡️ **IT IS BUILT INTO ELECTRICITY PRICES FOR EVERYONE.**

---

## 📈 Demand effect: the more we use, the more we pay

When a massive consumer enters the system:

* overall demand increases
* the grid approaches its limits

👉 **WHEN DEMAND RISES — PRICES RISE.**

This is not specific to one country. It is the fundamental logic of energy markets.

---

## 💧 Water, noise, and land — hidden costs

Electricity is not the only resource. Large data centers use enormous amounts of water for cooling, often millions of liters per day. In dry regions like Arizona, this has already become a serious social issue.

There is also constant noise, increased traffic, and significant land usage. Despite the scale of investment, the number of jobs remains relatively low due to high automation.

All of this reinforces a key question: is the local community truly benefiting?

---

## 🇭🇷 Pantheon in Topusko: opportunity or risk?

In this context, the widely discussed Pantheon AI data center project stands out — potentially the largest private investment in Croatia’s history.

A planned capacity of 1 gigawatt means annual consumption of about 8.7 terawatt-hours, representing approximately **40–50% of Croatia’s total annual electricity consumption**.

This means:

👉 **ONE PROJECT ≈ HALF A COUNTRY.**

Such a project does not just affect one region — it reshapes the entire national energy balance.

The investor plans to build new infrastructure: hundreds of kilometers of transmission lines, substations, solar capacity, and large battery systems. On paper, it appears modern and sustainable.

However, the core challenge remains the same as in the United States: how to ensure stable, continuous energy supply in a system that already lacks significant surplus capacity.

Solar energy depends on weather and time of day. Batteries can help, but they cannot replace continuous generation. Without additional production — whether gas, nuclear, or large-scale renewables — such a project inevitably relies on the existing grid.

And once it relies on the grid, the price question becomes unavoidable.

---

## ⚖️ Who ultimately pays?

Experience from the United States shows that the answer is rarely simple. In the best-case scenario, the investor funds most of the infrastructure and the project drives development without significantly affecting prices.

In a more realistic scenario, costs are shared between investors, energy companies, and the state. A portion of that cost — often invisibly — ends up on citizens’ bills.

In the worst case, a data center becomes an “energy magnet,” pulling resources from the system, forcing new power plant construction, and increasing electricity prices for everyone in the long term.

---

## The new reality of the digital age

Data centers are the backbone of the modern economy. Without them, there is no artificial intelligence, no cloud services, and no digital infrastructure we increasingly depend on.

But as their size and consumption grow, their role is changing. They are no longer just technical facilities — they are becoming a key factor in energy policy and society.

That is why the discussion is no longer limited to engineers, but now includes regulators, policymakers, and citizens.

---

## Our take

Data centers are not the problem by themselves. The problem arises when their growth is not aligned with the energy reality of the systems they depend on.

Pantheon could be a development opportunity for Croatia, especially for a region that has been neglected for decades. But without a clear and transparent energy plan, the project carries significant risks — not only for the power system, but also for end users.

The key question is not whether the project will be built, but under what conditions — and who will ultimately bear the cost.

Because in the era of artificial intelligence, energy is becoming the new currency.

And its price is no longer something that can be ignored.
