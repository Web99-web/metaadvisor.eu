# A law that promises clarity — but deepens the divide

The U.S. **Clarity Act** has been presented as a long-awaited effort to finally bring order to the crypto market. After years of regulatory ambiguity, court battles, and ad hoc enforcement actions, lawmakers say they want to draw a clear boundary between the traditional banking system and digital assets.

Yet as the debate moves closer to a final outcome, it is becoming increasingly clear that the Clarity Act is not merely a technical framework. **It exposes a deep conflict of interest between banks and the crypto sector — while largely sidelining the real-world experiences of everyday investors.**

**At the heart of the bill is not crypto innovation, but fear of losing bank deposits.**

## Why stablecoins have become the main target

Behind the language of financial stability lies a simple economic reality: **money that is not held in banks does not generate bank profits.**  
For years, stablecoins were marketed as the “safe harbor” of crypto — digital tokens pegged to the U.S. dollar or other fiat currencies, enabling fast payments, value transfers, and participation in decentralized finance without the extreme volatility of Bitcoin or altcoins.

Recently, however, stablecoins have become the primary focus of regulators and banking lobbyists alike. The reason is straightforward: **stablecoins are beginning to offer what banks consider their exclusive domain — yield and interest on money.**

Banks argue that stablecoins paying rewards or passive yield effectively function like deposits, but without the same regulatory oversight, capital requirements, or consumer safeguards. From their perspective, this creates a “parallel banking system” that threatens financial stability.

**The Clarity Act seeks to draw a firm line: a stablecoin must not resemble a bank deposit.**

## But who protects users when the system collapses?

While lawmakers debate definitions and interest mechanisms, far less attention is paid to what happens when crypto platforms fail. Recent history provides sobering answers.

The bankruptcies of **FTX** and **Celsius** revealed just how ill-prepared existing legal frameworks are for digital assets. Hundreds of thousands of users knowingly invested in crypto, accepted market volatility, and embraced the long-term nature of the asset class.

When those platforms collapsed, however, **their crypto holdings were legally converted into U.S. dollar claims — fixed at the value on the day bankruptcy proceedings began.**

Everything that followed — including Bitcoin’s sharp recovery and the broader market rebound — had no bearing on their payouts. **They did not participate in the subsequent rise of crypto prices.**

**Investors absorbed the full crypto risk, but were denied the crypto upside.**

## The USD logic inside a crypto system

This raises a fundamental question that the Clarity Act does not address at all:  
If regulators continue to treat crypto as a USD-denominated claim in times of crisis, **why is there such intense fear that stablecoins might “imitate” banks?**

At the moment of collapse, crypto ceases to be crypto. It becomes a dollar figure frozen in the past. This effectively nullifies the logic of long-term investment in digital assets.

**This is not a neutral legal choice — it structurally favors the traditional financial system.**

## Banks, legislation, and the fight for control

Although the Clarity Act is framed as a consumer-protection and financial-stability measure, its emphasis tells a different story. Banks are not only worried about risk — **they are worried about losing control over money itself.**

Stablecoins allow users to hold value outside the banking system, without opening hours, without traditional intermediaries, and often with greater transparency. Every dollar held in a stablecoin is a dollar not sitting in a bank account.

That is why the argument becomes paradoxical: stablecoins are treated as banks when it comes to restrictions, but **when they fail, they are not treated like banks at all — they receive no special protection.**

In this light, regulation becomes less about adaptation and more about preserving an existing hierarchy.

## Our view (MetaAdvisor)

**In an open market, choice must exist.**  
Some will always prefer the relative safety of the banking system. Others will accept the risks of crypto in exchange for freedom and potential growth.

But that choice only makes sense if the rules are consistent.

If investors bear the full downside risk of crypto, they should also retain exposure to its upside — even in cases of bankruptcy. Anything else creates a system where risk is privatized, while returns are selectively capped.

**The Clarity Act may offer clarity to banks — but for crypto investors, it raises more questions than answers.**
