# Why Google’s AI tool increasingly cites YouTube for health information

A study conducted in Germany, analyzing more than 50,000 health-related search queries, has raised serious concerns about how Google’s AI-generated search summaries present medical information. While Google claims its AI responses rely on reputable sources, the findings suggest a different reality: YouTube is cited more frequently than hospitals, government health portals, or medical institutions.

This is particularly significant because Google’s AI summaries are seen by approximately **2 billion people each month** and often appear above traditional search results, shaping first impressions and user trust.

## What is Google’s AI search tool?

Google’s AI tool generates automated summary boxes at the top of search results, offering quick and confident answers powered by generative artificial intelligence. The goal is convenience — reducing the need for users to click through multiple sources.

However, this format introduces a critical issue: the AI presents information with a **strong sense of authority**, regardless of how reliable or verified the underlying sources may be.

## YouTube as a dominant health source

According to research by SE Ranking, YouTube accounts for **4.43% of all citations** in AI-generated summaries for health-related queries. No hospital network, government health agency, or academic medical institution comes close to this level of representation.

The issue lies in YouTube’s nature as a platform:
- it is not a medical publisher  
- it lacks formal editorial or scientific review  
- anyone can publish content  

While YouTube hosts high-quality videos from licensed medical professionals and institutions, it also features content from influencers, wellness coaches, and creators with no medical training.

## Why this is risky for public health

Health information requires accuracy, context, and professional oversight. When AI-generated summaries elevate unverified content with authoritative language, the risks become tangible:

- misinformation may be perceived as medical fact  
- users may make health decisions based on flawed advice  
- the distinction between expert knowledge and opinion content becomes blurred  

In healthcare, misinformation is not just inconvenient — it can have real-world consequences.

## Google’s response and the structural problem

Google maintains that its AI tool prioritizes high-quality content regardless of format and points out that many credible medical professionals publish on YouTube. However, the study suggests the system does not clearly distinguish between **institutional authority and general content popularity**.

Instead, factors such as accessibility, engagement metrics, and technical suitability for AI summaries appear to play a larger role. This points to a **structural design issue**, not a temporary malfunction.

## Trust, authority, and the future of search

As AI increasingly becomes the primary gateway to information, the key question shifts from *“Is this accurate?”* to *“Why does this feel authoritative?”*. In health-related contexts, that shift is especially concerning.

If AI systems become the first — and sometimes only — point of reference, the responsibility for source selection becomes a matter of public trust, not just engineering.
