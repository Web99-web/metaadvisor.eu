## How the new “taxi tax” was meant to work

At the start of 2026, changes to UK tax rules were introduced with the aim of reshaping how value-added tax (VAT) applies to ride-hailing platforms. The intention was to align companies like Uber with traditional taxi operators and ensure a level playing field.

Under the new framework, the standard 20% VAT was expected to apply to the full fare paid by passengers, not just to the platform’s commission. The UK government estimated this would generate around £700 million in additional annual tax revenue.

## Uber’s contractual pivot: from provider to agent

Before the new rules could fully take effect, Uber adjusted its contracts with drivers outside London. Under the revised structure, Uber no longer positions itself as the provider of transport services but as an agent that connects drivers and passengers.

The transport contract is now formed directly between the driver and the rider, while Uber charges a commission for facilitating the match. The tax consequence is significant: VAT is applied only to Uber’s commission, not to the entire fare.

Since most drivers remain below the VAT registration threshold, the majority of rides outside London effectively avoid the additional tax burden.

## Why London is treated differently

London remains an exception. Local transport regulations do not allow the agency model, meaning Uber must continue to act as the formal service provider. As a result, VAT is charged on the full fare for London trips.

This creates an unusual situation in which the same platform operates under two different tax models within a single country, depending on local regulatory rules.

## A broader challenge for governments

Uber’s maneuver highlights how quickly digital platforms can adapt their legal and contractual structures in response to regulatory changes. While the law has technically been tightened, the real fiscal impact may fall well short of policymakers’ expectations.

For governments, the case serves as a warning: without precise legal definitions of who the actual service provider is, ambitious tax reforms can be neutralized through contractual restructuring.

## Our take (MetaAdvisor)

- Uber’s strategy is legally sound but undermines the policy goal of the tax reform.  
- Digital platforms continue to stay one step ahead of regulators.  
- The split between London and the rest of the UK exposes weaknesses in fragmented regulation.  
- Similar approaches are likely to be adopted by other gig-economy platforms.  
- Over time, lawmakers will need clearer rules defining who truly provides the service.
