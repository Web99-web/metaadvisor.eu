# Cheaper gas, cheaper attacks: how Ethereum’s Fusaka upgrade opened the door to a new wave of scams

Ethereum’s **Fusaka upgrade**, implemented in early December, delivered what users and developers had long been waiting for: **significantly lower network fees** and improved throughput. But only weeks later, it became clear that this optimization came with an unintended side effect — a surge in **low-cost, large-scale attacks** that exploit human behavior rather than technical flaws.

## Lower fees as the trigger

Following Fusaka, average Ethereum transaction fees dropped by more than **60%**, dramatically reducing the cost of all onchain activity. DeFi usage picked up, NFT minting became cheaper and overall network traffic increased. At the same time, the same drop in fees made it **economically viable for attackers** to send thousands of transactions at scale.

In blockchain systems, where every action once carried a meaningful financial cost, lowering that barrier inevitably increases the risk of abuse.

## What is address poisoning?

One of the most widespread attack methods after the Fusaka upgrade is so-called **address poisoning**. Scammers send minimal token amounts from wallet addresses that visually resemble legitimate ones. The goal is not immediate theft, but deliberate confusion. When users later copy an address from their transaction history, they may unknowingly select a **fake address** and send a significantly larger amount directly to the attacker. The attack does not rely on smart contract vulnerabilities, but on routine, speed and everyday wallet habits — a pattern that led to one of the biggest single crypto mistakes of 2025, when a user

   style="color:#1e40af; font-weight:700; text-decoration:none;">
copied an address — and lost $50 million
</a>.

## The illusion of healthy network growth

Onchain metrics showed a sharp rise in new addresses and daily transactions, but security researchers warn that part of this growth is driven by **automated spam activity**. In some weeks, the number of new addresses surged by as much as **170% above historical averages**, without reflecting genuine user adoption.

In other words, some “positive” network indicators are masking an increase in **malicious activity**.

## Real losses, no protocol breach

These attacks are estimated to have already caused losses of **hundreds of thousands of dollars**, without compromising wallet software or the Ethereum protocol itself. The technology remained secure — users did not.

This reinforces a long-standing lesson in crypto: **the weakest link is often not the code, but the human behind it**.

## Security as a prerequisite for scaling

Researchers warn that network scalability cannot be pursued in isolation from user security. Without clearer address visualization, warnings for suspicious transfers and stronger user education at the wallet level, address poisoning and similar attacks will remain a persistent threat.

Ethereum’s Fusaka upgrade has shown that every optimization has a cost. **Cheaper gas enables more innovation — but also cheaper attacks.**

## Our take (MetaAdvisor)

- Lower fees changed the economics of attacks, not just network usage.
- Address poisoning exploits user behavior rather than technical weaknesses.
- Rising onchain metrics do not automatically indicate a healthy network.
- Scaling without stronger user protection introduces hidden systemic risks.
