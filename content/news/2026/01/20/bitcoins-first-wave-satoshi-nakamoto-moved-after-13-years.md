# Bitcoins from the first wave, from the time of Satoshi Nakamoto, moved after 13 years

The blockchain has recorded a rare event: a Bitcoin wallet from the so-called **first wave**, created during the period when **Satoshi Nakamoto** was still actively involved in the project, has moved **909 BTC** after more than **13 years of complete inactivity**. At current market prices, this amount is valued at approximately **$85 million**.

Such movements almost always trigger speculation about potential selling and market impact. However, a closer look at the broader context suggests that the reasons may be far more complex.

## How much were these bitcoins worth then — and how much are they worth now?

When these bitcoins were accumulated, roughly between **2011 and 2013**, the price of Bitcoin ranged from about **$10 to $200**. At the time, the total value of the holdings was **well below $100,000**, and in some phases significantly less.

Today, with Bitcoin trading above **$90,000 per BTC**, the same holdings represent wealth worth **tens of millions of dollars**, making this case one of the most striking examples of long-term value appreciation in financial history.

## What does the “first wave” of Bitcoin mean?

The first wave refers to Bitcoin’s early years, when:
- there was no institutional participation,
- modern exchanges as we know them today did not yet exist,
- security practices were experimental,
- very few participants could imagine today’s valuations.

Wallets from this period carry particular significance, as they represent a direct link to Bitcoin’s original vision.

## Paper wallets and the risk of physical degradation

At the time these wallets were created:
- private keys were often printed on paper (paper wallets),
- seed phrases were written down by hand,
- data was stored on USB drives, CDs, or old hard disks,
- backups were minimal or nonexistent.

Over time, these storage methods are vulnerable to **physical degradation**. Paper fades, ink disappears, and storage media fail. Moving funds to a new, modern address is therefore often a matter of **basic security**, not a trading decision.

## Inheritance as an overlooked factor

Another realistic scenario is that **heirs have taken control** of the wallet. In such cases:
- funds are typically moved to a new address first,
- immediate selling is often avoided,
- the priority is preservation rather than speculation.

These actions are frequently misinterpreted by the market.

## Why the market remains calm

Despite the impressive size of the transfer:
- there are no deposits to exchanges,
- there is no fragmentation typical of a sell-off,
- there is no visible downward price pressure.

As a result, the move is widely seen as a **technical or security-related action**, rather than the beginning of a broader liquidation.

## Our take (MetaAdvisor)

- The gap between the original and current value explains why such wallets draw intense attention.
- Paper wallets and legacy storage methods pose real security risks.
- Inheritance is a common but under-discussed factor.
- Without transfers to exchanges, there is little reason for market panic.
