# CertiK Traces Fund Flows After Major Crypto Hack

Following one of the larger crypto hacks earlier this year, in which approximately **$282 million** worth of assets were compromised, blockchain security firm CertiK released a detailed analysis of the subsequent fund movements. According to the investigation, at least **$63 million** was linked to transactions involving **Tornado Cash**, a well-known privacy-focused mixing protocol.

While this represents only a portion of the total stolen funds, the transaction patterns provide a clear picture of how attackers attempt to obscure trails after a successful breach.

## Cross-Chain Transfers and Fund Fragmentation

CertiK reports that attackers initially moved part of the stolen Bitcoin holdings across chains using cross-chain swaps. Roughly **686 BTC** was bridged to the Ethereum network and converted into approximately **19,600 ETH**.

Instead of consolidating the funds in a single address, the assets were then split into numerous smaller amounts and distributed across multiple wallets. This fragmentation is a standard step in crypto money laundering strategies.

## Tornado Cash as a Privacy and Obfuscation Tool

A key part of the trail leads to Tornado Cash. The protocol allows users to break the on-chain link between deposit and withdrawal addresses, significantly complicating forensic analysis. CertiK estimates that around **$63 million** flowed through Tornado Cash in this case.

While Tornado Cash itself is not inherently illegal, it is frequently used in criminal contexts due to its ability to obscure transaction histories. As a result, it remains under close scrutiny from regulators and security analysts.

## How the Victim Was Tricked Into Signing

According to available information, the incident did not involve a compromised seed phrase or malware infection. Instead, the victim was manipulated into signing a **malicious transaction or message** that granted broad permissions over their wallet.

Such signatures often appear harmless — framed as verification steps, logins, or technical confirmations — but can silently provide attackers with lasting control. The actual draining of funds may occur later, making detection and response more difficult.

## Why These Attacks Are Hard to Prevent

What makes this attack vector particularly dangerous is that the signatures involved are **technically valid**. From the blockchain’s perspective, they are legitimate authorizations. This makes social engineering one of the most effective tools for attackers, a trend CertiK and other security firms have repeatedly highlighted.

## Transparency — and Its Limits

Blockchain technology enables public tracking of transactions, but tools like mixers and cross-chain bridges introduce significant limitations. At the same time, on-chain transparency is what allows firms like CertiK to reconstruct events and identify recurring attack patterns.

This case underscores the importance of understanding the difference between a simple transaction and a signature that grants ongoing permissions.
