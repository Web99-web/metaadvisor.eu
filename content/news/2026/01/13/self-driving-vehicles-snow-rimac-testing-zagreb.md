# Can self-driving vehicles operate in snow?

Mate Rimac’s announcement that autonomous robotaxis under the **Verne** project could begin testing in **Zagreb**, the capital of **Croatia** and a member of the **European Union**, as early as **spring 2026**, has reignited a debate that extends far beyond local headlines. The issue is no longer whether autonomous vehicles are technologically possible, but how they behave under real-world conditions — particularly during European winters.

Autonomous driving is often associated with sunny test zones in California or carefully controlled urban areas in Asia. Zagreb, however, presents a very different scenario. The city combines older infrastructure, dense traffic, trams, heavy pedestrian flows, and frequent improvised traffic situations — all of which become more challenging once winter weather sets in.

## Winter in Zagreb as a real-world stress test

Winter conditions in Zagreb are not limited to snowfall. Snow frequently covers lane markings and crosswalks, while wind-driven snow sticks to traffic signs and traffic lights, making them temporarily unreadable. At the same time, heavy use of road salt creates a mixture of water, salt, and slush that quickly coats vehicles.

Drivers are familiar with the experience: after just ten to fifteen minutes of driving, windshields, headlights, and cameras can become heavily soiled, significantly reducing visibility. For autonomous vehicles, this creates a critical challenge. The system must determine whether reduced perception is caused by dirty sensors or by an actual obstacle — and decide whether to continue, slow down, or disengage entirely.

A particularly dangerous phenomenon is **black ice** — a thin, nearly invisible layer of ice that poses a risk even to experienced drivers. In practice, black ice cannot be reliably detected in advance by human vision or current sensor systems. It is usually discovered only once traction is lost. While autonomous systems can react very quickly to skidding, reliably identifying black ice before it causes a loss of control remains one of the largest unresolved challenges in autonomous driving worldwide.

## Electric vehicles and cold temperatures

Because robotaxis and most autonomous vehicles are electric, winter conditions further affect their performance. At temperatures around –10 to –12 degrees Celsius, which are not uncommon in Zagreb, electric vehicles experience a significant reduction in driving range. Cold weather slows battery chemistry, while additional energy is required to heat the cabin, sensors, and onboard computing systems.

In real-world operation, this results in shorter driving intervals between charging sessions and a greater need for careful route and fleet planning — especially important for autonomous vehicles expected to provide consistent and safe service.

## What exactly did Rimac announce?

Mate Rimac has stated that **testing of Verne’s autonomous robotaxis in Zagreb is expected to begin in spring 2026**. These tests are intended to be gradual and carefully controlled. The initial phase will involve limited routes, lower speeds, and continuous human supervision — not full autonomous operation available to the general public.

This approach aligns with European safety standards and highlights an important reality: autonomous driving is not a switch that can simply be turned on, but a long-term process of adapting technology to complex, real-world traffic environments.

## Europe is not a laboratory

Unlike many U.S. test regions, European cities feature denser urban layouts, more complex traffic interactions, and far more variable weather conditions. For that reason, testing in Zagreb carries broader significance. It reflects conditions common across much of Europe rather than idealized environments.

If autonomous vehicles can operate safely under these circumstances, it would be a strong signal of technological maturity. If they cannot, that insight is equally valuable for regulators, industry leaders, and the public.

## Conclusion

Self-driving vehicles can operate in snow — but not at all times, not everywhere, and not without limitations. **Zagreb, Croatia, and the European Union** present a demanding environment for autonomous mobility, particularly in winter, when snow, road salt, and black ice quickly expose the limits of current systems.

Rimac’s announcement of testing planned for spring 2026 is therefore more than a local development. It represents an important European real-world experiment. The future of autonomous mobility will not be decided under perfect conditions, but precisely when even human drivers struggle to stay in control.
