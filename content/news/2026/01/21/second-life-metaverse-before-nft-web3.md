# Who Remembers Second Life – Gaming, Living, and Real Money in the Metaverse Before NFTs and Web3

Before terms like NFTs, Web3, and the metaverse became part of everyday tech vocabulary, there was already a virtual world where **real money moved in the millions**. Virtual land parcels sold for **100,000 USD**, companies invested **tens of thousands of dollars per campaign**, and individual users built virtual empires valued at **over 1,000,000 USD**, with the ability to convert those assets into real money and withdraw them to a bank account.

That world was called Second Life.

## The first major metaverse before we called it that

Second Life launched in 2003 and reached its peak between 2005 and 2008. During that period, it was not perceived as a traditional game but as a parallel digital world where people could live, work, and do business. Users bought virtual land, built houses and shops, organized events, and developed business models that generated real revenue.

A key difference from today’s metaverse projects was that Second Life already had a functioning economy. The Linden Dollar, the platform’s internal currency, had an official exchange rate and could be legally converted into U.S. dollars.

## Virtual millionaires and early digital entrepreneurs

The most famous example from that era was Ailin Graef, known by the avatar name **Anshe Chung**, who became the first publicly recognized “virtual millionaire” in 2006. **Her assets were valued at more than one million U.S. dollars**, based on virtual land holdings, constructed properties, and ongoing rental income.

It is important to note that this wealth represented a combination of **actually realized cash and the valuation of digital assets** at the market’s peak. Second Life was not a system where everyone made money — but it did enable a genuine form of digital entrepreneurship for those who understood how the ecosystem worked.

## When major corporations entered the virtual world

At the height of its popularity, major global corporations entered Second Life. Technology companies, media organizations, and automotive and fashion brands built virtual offices, showrooms, and marketing campaigns inside the platform.

Advertising did not rely on banners but on **immersive experiences**. Companies had to buy or lease virtual land, pay for 3D design, develop interactive content, and maintain an ongoing presence. Serious campaigns typically cost **between 10,000 and 50,000 USD**, while larger projects went far beyond that. At one point, the overall virtual economy of Second Life was measured in **hundreds of millions of dollars per year**.

## Mass adoption, hype, and rapid mental fatigue

Second Life became widely popular for a period and experienced a strong wave of hype. User numbers grew quickly, interest was genuine, and it felt as though a new digital continent was emerging. Yet despite real money, major brands, and intense media attention, that mass enthusiasm did not last long.

All interactions took place entirely within the virtual world. Even simple actions required time, movement, and constant attention. It often took several minutes of real time just to walk from one area to another, without any concrete outcome or reward. For many users, this quickly became mentally exhausting.

## Success or failure — a debate that remains open

The question remains whether Second Life should be viewed as a successful or failed experiment, or as the first major attempt whose limitations only became visible once people truly began to use it. It was real enough to move serious money, large enough to attract corporations, and durable enough to leave lasting lessons behind.

- It demonstrated that virtual economies with real money can function

- The real value of Second Life lies in its lessons, not in nostalgia
