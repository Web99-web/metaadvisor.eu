# The Netherlands and the unrealized gains tax: Why should investors pay tax on something they haven’t sold?

The Netherlands has opened a heated debate about introducing a **tax on unrealized gains**, a measure that would apply to stocks, bonds, and cryptocurrencies. Under this proposal, citizens would **pay tax on increases in asset value even if they never sell those assets**, raising serious concerns among investors and the broader public.

Supporters frame the change as a way to stabilize government revenues, but critics argue that the consequences for small and medium-sized investors could be severe — especially in highly volatile markets.

## What does an unrealized gains tax actually mean?

Unlike a traditional capital gains tax, which is due **only when an asset is sold**, an unrealized gains tax targets the **annual increase in an asset’s value**, regardless of whether that gain is ever converted into cash.

**In other words: the potential value of an asset may rise — but only “on paper.”**

- **Why only on paper?** Because there is no sale.  
- **No sale means no cash.**  
- **What does that mean in practice?** The tax must be paid **out of your own pocket**.

As a result, taxation becomes detached from citizens’ real financial situations and, **instead of rewarding saving and long-term investing, turns into a mechanism that gradually erodes household wealth**.

## A practical example: taxing gains that were never cashed in

Imagine an investor who puts **€20,000** into stocks and cryptocurrencies as a long-term investment. Over several years, the portfolio grows to **€70,000**, yet the investor **does not sell anything**. Despite the lack of cash income, annual taxes on **unrealized gains** must be paid **in cash from personal funds**, totaling roughly **€15,000** over time.

Later, a market downturn cuts the portfolio value in half, to **€35,000**, at which point the investor decides to sell.

**The result: a real gain of €0**, despite years of growth and continuous tax payments made from personal savings. **The state is the only guaranteed winner**, while the investor bears all market risk and may end up with nothing — or even a loss.

## Why this system undermines public trust

The core issue is **asymmetric risk sharing**. When markets rise, the government collects taxes. When markets fall, **citizens absorb the losses alone**.

For ordinary people, this does not feel like a technical tax reform. It feels like being **penalized for trying to save and invest responsibly**, creating a deep sense of unfairness and distrust.

## A particular risk for crypto and long-term saving

Cryptocurrencies and financial markets are known for **high volatility**. Long-term investors accept this risk as part of the deal. An unrealized gains tax, however, transforms volatility into a **permanent tax burden**.

The consequences are clear:
- patience is punished  
- short-term trading is encouraged  
- long-term saving becomes unattractive  
- the risk of capital flight increases  

## Our view (MetaAdvisor)

- **Unrealized gains are not real gains** and should not be taxed.  
- This model guarantees **stable revenue for the state** while shifting all market risk onto citizens.  
- High volatility makes such a tax especially unfair for small investors.  
- Instead of encouraging financial responsibility, the system **penalizes saving and long-term planning**.  
- Over time, this approach risks **loss of trust and capital outflows**.
