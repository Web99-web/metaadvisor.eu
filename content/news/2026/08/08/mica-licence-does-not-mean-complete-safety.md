# A MiCA Licence Does Not Mean Complete Safety

A MiCA licence has become an important marker for crypto platforms that want to provide services in the European Union. Authorised providers must meet organisational, capital, governance and security requirements, protect client assets, handle complaints and clearly inform users about costs and risks.

This is a major step forward compared with a market in which many platforms operated for years without a common European framework. However, a licence does not mean that every cryptocurrency available on a platform is safe, that users cannot lose money or that every product displayed by a licensed company inside the same app is covered by MiCA rules.

The European Securities and Markets Authority, ESMA, has therefore warned about the so-called halo effect. A user sees that a platform is regulated and then assumes that every token, product and service it offers is automatically regulated and safe. That may not be the case.

## What Does a MiCA Licence Actually Confirm?

MiCA, the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, introduces common rules for issuers of certain crypto-assets and providers of crypto-asset services in the European Union. A platform authorised as a Crypto-Asset Service Provider, or CASP, must have an appropriate governance structure, internal controls, sufficient capital, client-protection procedures and a complaints-handling system.

Authorisation means that the relevant national authority has reviewed the legal entity, its management, business plan, asset-protection arrangements, IT systems and other required elements. An authorisation issued in one member state can then allow the provider to offer its approved services in other EU countries through the European passporting system.

Users therefore no longer need to rely only on a platform’s marketing claims. They can check whether the relevant legal entity appears in the official ESMA register and which services it has been authorised to provide.

However, regulatory authorisation primarily confirms the status of the service provider. It does not confirm that a particular token will increase in value, that a project has no technical vulnerability or that every product shown in the app is suitable for every user.

## A Licence Does Not Guarantee a Token’s Value

MiCA does not remove market risk. Bitcoin, ether, altcoins and other tokens can still lose value sharply, regardless of whether they were purchased through a licensed platform.

A platform may execute an order correctly, safeguard assets according to the applicable rules and display all required risk warnings, while the purchased token still falls by 50, 80 or almost 100 percent.

The regulator does not assess whether an investment will be profitable. MiCA authorisation is not a quality seal for every token offered on a platform and it is not a recommendation to buy. European supervisory authorities continue to warn that crypto-assets can be highly risky and speculative, and that the level of protection depends on the specific asset and service involved.

## Not Every Product in the Same App Is Covered by MiCA

One of the biggest potential sources of confusion arises when a licensed platform offers products outside the MiCA framework alongside regulated crypto services.

Inside the same app, users may see spot cryptocurrency purchases, custody, staking, lending, perpetual futures, derivatives, structured products or other services. The fact that a particular legal entity is authorised under MiCA does not mean that every one of those products falls under the same regulation or offers the same level of protection.

ESMA has specifically warned about the halo effect that can arise when an authorised CASP offers unregulated products alongside regulated services. Users may then wrongly assume that the regulatory approval applies to the entire offering simply because all products appear under the same brand and inside the same app.

It is therefore not enough to check whether a platform has MiCA authorisation. Users also need to identify which legal entity holds that authorisation and whether it covers the specific service they intend to use.

## MiCA Does Not Cover Every Token and Service in the Same Way

The regulation covers a large part of the crypto-asset market that was not previously governed by other European financial rules, including special provisions for asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens.

However, not all digital products are classified in the same way. Certain assets may fall under other European financial legislation, while some fully decentralised activities and particular services may remain outside the full MiCA framework, depending on their actual structure.

For example, ESMA clarified in June 2026 that MiCA does not directly regulate crypto-asset lending and borrowing. This is another reason users should not assume that every product offered by a well-known and regulated platform is automatically protected under MiCA.

Even when a token falls within MiCA, that does not mean its software code has been verified as completely secure, that the project cannot fail or that its value will remain stable. The rules create obligations, accountability and transparency, but they do not eliminate business, technical or market risk.

## Crypto-Assets Are Not Insured Like Bank Deposits

Users often assume that a regulated crypto platform offers protection similar to a bank. That is not necessarily the case.

Money held in a bank account in the European Union may be protected by a deposit-guarantee scheme up to the legally defined amount. Crypto-assets held on a platform do not automatically benefit from the same type of state-backed guarantee.

MiCA requires providers to apply appropriate custody arrangements and separate client assets, while also providing for provider liability in certain circumstances. However, this is not the same as a guarantee that every user will recover the full amount lost in the event of insolvency, a hack or a dispute.

The outcome may depend on the type of service, how the assets were held, the cause of the loss, the contractual terms, the provider’s liability and the insolvency process.

## A Regulated Platform Can Still Be Hacked

A MiCA-authorised provider must maintain security systems, risk management, internal controls and operational procedures, but no information system is completely invulnerable.

Attackers may target the platform’s infrastructure, employees, external suppliers, user accounts or smart contracts connected to a particular product. A licence reduces the likelihood that a provider operates without basic security controls, but it cannot guarantee that no incident will ever occur.

Users should therefore continue to protect themselves with a unique and strong password, two-factor authentication, careful verification of withdrawal addresses and caution around fake messages and websites.

MiCA also cannot prevent a user from entering login details on a fake website, revealing a seed phrase or approving a malicious transaction.

## A Licence Does Not Eliminate the Risk of Company Failure

Regulated providers must hold a certain amount of own funds and meet prudential requirements. This increases business resilience, but it does not guarantee that a company can never become illiquid or insolvent.

Poor management, falling revenue, major operating losses, litigation, internal fraud or a serious security incident can still threaten the business.

Regulation should improve transparency, accountability and oversight, but it does not turn a private company into an institution that cannot fail. Users should therefore understand who actually holds their assets, how those assets are separated from the platform’s own property and what the contractual terms provide in the event of insolvency.

## How Can Users Recognise Genuine MiCA Authorisation?

Terms such as “MiCA compliant”, “MiCA ready” or “MiCA aligned” may mean that a company claims to have adapted its operations to the regulation’s requirements. However, those phrases alone do not confirm that the relevant regulator has completed the process and granted official authorisation.

When a platform has actually received MiCA authorisation, it should clearly state that it is “authorised under MiCA”, “MiCA-licensed” or “authorised as a Crypto-Asset Service Provider”, meaning that it has been approved as a CASP.

The most legally precise wording is that a specific legal entity is authorised as a crypto-asset service provider under MiCA. The phrase “MiCA-licensed” is widely understood and often used in public communication, but the official status comes from the regulatory authorisation granted to the specific legal entity.

The platform should identify its full legal name, the member state in which the authorisation was granted, the relevant regulator and the scope of services it is permitted to provide. Even then, a statement on the platform’s own website is not the final confirmation. The most reliable proof is the legal entity’s entry in the official ESMA register of authorised CASPs.

The familiar name of an app is not always the same as the legal entity providing a particular service. Large international groups may have several related companies, while different products may be supplied by different entities within the same group.

## A Platform Without MiCA Authorisation Is Not Necessarily Illegal

The absence of MiCA authorisation does not automatically mean that a platform operates illegally in every country. A company may be properly registered, licensed or regulated in the United Kingdom, the United States, Switzerland, the United Arab Emirates or another jurisdiction and legally provide services there.

It also does not necessarily mean that the platform has never attempted to obtain MiCA authorisation. It may not have applied, its application may still be pending, it may have withdrawn its application or it may have chosen not to target the European Union market.

However, a licence or registration outside the European Union does not replace MiCA authorisation for actively providing crypto services to clients in the EU. As a general rule, a third-country provider may not actively target, advertise or market its crypto services to users in the European Union without the appropriate authorisation.

There is a very narrow exception known as reverse solicitation. It may apply when an EU user requests a particular service from a third-country provider entirely on their own initiative and without previous advertising, solicitation or a targeted offer from the platform.

ESMA stresses that this exception must be interpreted restrictively. A platform may not use it as a disguised way to conduct regular business with EU users without MiCA authorisation.

This does not mean that an EU resident can never technically access a platform without a MiCA licence. However, the mere availability of a website or the ability to open an app does not mean that the platform is allowed to actively offer its services in the European market or that the user receives the protection available to clients of authorised CASPs.

## Authorisation From One Member State Applies Across the EU

MiCA allows an authorised company to provide its approved services throughout the European Union from one member state. This makes cross-border business easier and creates a more consistent regulatory framework.

However, a user may have a contract with a legal entity registered in another member state. In the event of a complaint or dispute, it is important to know which company is the actual service provider, which authority granted the authorisation and where the complaint should be submitted.

The name of the app, its logo and a well-known global brand are not enough to determine its regulatory status. Users need to open the terms of service and verify which legal entity actually provides services to clients in the European Union.

## MiCA Does Not Protect Users From a Bad Investment Decision

Regulation can require clearer information, risk warnings and fairer treatment of clients. It cannot prevent a user from buying an overvalued token, using excessive leverage or investing money they cannot afford to lose.

Even accurate and complete documentation is not useful if an investor does not read or understand it. A white paper is not a guarantee that a project will succeed, just as a licensed exchange does not guarantee that every token it lists will retain its value.

MiCA increases institutional accountability, but the user still remains responsible for assessing market and technical risk.

## What Should Users Check Before Using a Platform?

The first step is the official ESMA register. Users should confirm that the relevant legal entity is listed, identify which country granted the authorisation and check which crypto-asset services the company is permitted to provide.

They should then determine whether MiCA protection applies to the particular product they intend to use. Spot trading, custody, staking, lending and derivatives are not the same, even when they all appear inside the same app.

It is also important to read who holds the assets, whether they are separated from the platform’s own property, what happens in the event of insolvency, whether withdrawal restrictions apply and which institution handles complaints.

Users should also assess the token itself, the issuer, liquidity, ownership concentration, technical risks and the possibility of a complete loss of value. A platform licence does not replace that due diligence.

## MiCA Is a Layer of Protection, Not a Guarantee

MiCA is an important development because crypto-asset service providers can no longer simply offer the same services throughout the Union without common rules on authorisation, governance, capital, client protection and transparency.

For users, a platform authorised under MiCA is generally a better starting point than an anonymous provider with no verifiable headquarters, legal entity or regulatory oversight.

However, there is a major difference between “regulated” and “risk-free.” MiCA authorisation confirms that a provider must operate within a defined legal framework. It does not guarantee a token’s price, the security of every smart contract, the recovery of every lost amount or the protection of every product shown inside the same app.

Likewise, the absence of a MiCA licence does not automatically mean that a platform is illegal everywhere. It may operate lawfully in other jurisdictions, but without the appropriate European authorisation it may not routinely and actively target users in the European Union.

The most reasonable approach is therefore not to ignore a MiCA licence, but to understand what it actually confirms, which services it covers and where its protection ends.
