# Coldcard Attack Worth $89 Million Hit 4,585 Bitcoin Wallets

The attack linked to vulnerable seeds generated by certain Coldcard firmware versions is no longer limited to the first wave, in which nearly 1,200 Bitcoin addresses were drained in only 41 minutes.

According to new estimates from Galaxy Research, a total of 4,585 addresses were drained across three identified attack waves. Approximately 1,367 BTC, worth nearly $89 million, was stolen.

The latest wave shows that the attacker is no longer targeting only larger balances. Wallets holding just a few thousand dollars are now also being systematically drained.

The attack continued for almost three days after the first major transactions, while the method used to move the stolen funds became more complex and harder to trace.

## Third Wave Hit Another 1,912 Addresses

In the third identified attack wave, approximately 208 BTC was stolen from 1,912 addresses.

The average amount per affected address is now slightly above 0.1 BTC. That is significantly lower than in the first wave, when roughly 1,083 BTC was withdrawn from 1,196 addresses in 41 minutes, or almost one bitcoin per address.

The falling average balance suggests that the largest and most profitable wallets within the vulnerable key space may already have been drained. The attacker is now moving toward smaller amounts that may still be financially worthwhile.

This is especially concerning for users who may have believed that a relatively small wallet balance would make them unattractive to an attacker.

## The Attacker Changed Tactics

The first two attack waves were comparatively easy to connect because the stolen bitcoin was moved to a smaller number of shared addresses.

In the third wave, funds from individual affected addresses were sent to separate destinations. The transactions were also batched, with an average of around six victims included in each sweep.

The funds were moved into pay-to-witness-script-hash outputs, which can support more complex conditions such as multisignature requirements or timelocks. Earlier waves more often used simpler outputs associated with a single key.

In the latest wave, the attacker searched only the default derivation path, the standard branch of the key tree that a wallet normally checks first.

## One Attacker or Several Independent Operators?

The change in behavior could mean that the same attacker adjusted the system after the first destination addresses were publicly identified.

It is also possible that a second attacker is independently searching the same vulnerable key space.

The blockchain alone cannot show whether all three waves were coordinated. Galaxy Research believes each individual wave was likely carried out by a single operator, but it has not connected all three waves to the same person or group.

The latest change in tactics nevertheless suggests that those exploiting the vulnerability are following public analyses and adapting how they move stolen funds.

## How Was Theft Possible Without Access to the Device?

The problem did not arise because attackers physically stole Coldcard devices, connected to them or intercepted users’ seed phrases online.

The vulnerability was in the way random data required for seed generation was produced.

A change introduced during a firmware migration in March 2021 caused seed generation in certain versions to rely on a software-based random number generator instead of the intended hardware source of randomness.

On affected Mk2 and Mk3 devices, the effective searchable space may have been approximately 40 bits, according to the manufacturer’s preliminary estimate, rather than the expected security level.

Later models including Mk4, Q and Mk5 included additional entropy from secure elements, but the manufacturer estimated that some seeds may have contained around 72 bits of entropy, still below the intended 128 bits.

## Private Keys Could Be Reconstructed Offline

The reduced entropy limited the number of possible seeds the vulnerable firmware could generate.

An attacker with sufficient computing power and knowledge of the vulnerability could therefore recreate possible seeds offline, derive private keys from them and check whether the corresponding Bitcoin addresses held funds.

The attacker did not need to touch the Coldcard device, know the user’s PIN or obtain a physical copy of the seed phrase.

The device could remain powered off and disconnected from the internet while the funds were still exposed, because the attack did not target communication with the device.

It targeted the predictable space of possible private keys.

## A Firmware Update Cannot Save an Old Seed

Coinkite released corrected firmware versions for affected models and warned users not to generate a new seed before installing the appropriate update.

However, updating the firmware cannot repair a seed that was already generated using a vulnerable version.

Users must:

* install the corrected firmware
* generate a completely new seed
* carefully record and verify the new backup
* verify the new address on the device screen
* first send a small test transaction
* move the remaining funds only after the test is confirmed

Coinkite warned users not to rush the migration because mistakes when recording the seed, checking addresses or transferring funds could create an additional and more immediate risk of loss.

## Which Models and Versions May Be Affected?

According to the manufacturer’s security warning, the issue affects seeds generated on Mk2 and Mk3 devices running firmware versions from 4.0.1 through 4.1.9.

Seeds generated on Mk4, Mk5 and Q devices may also be affected if they were created before corrected firmware versions were released for the relevant model and release track.

Corrected versions listed by the manufacturer include:

* Mk2 and Mk3: version 4.2.0 or later
* Mk4 and Mk5 Standard: version 5.6.0 or later
* Q Standard: version 1.5.0Q or later
* Mk4 and Mk5 Edge: version 6.6.0X or later
* Q Edge: version 6.6.0QX or later

TAPSIGNER, OPENDIME and SATSCARD are not affected because they use different codebases.

Users should not assume they are safe simply because they updated their device after the warning. The key question is which firmware version was used when the existing seed was originally generated.

## Dice Rolls and a Strong Passphrase May Reduce Risk

Coinkite stated that a seed may not be exposed solely by this RNG vulnerability if the user added at least 50 fair, independent and private dice rolls during the original seed-generation process.

That manually added entropy increases the number of possible seeds an attacker would need to search.

A strong and unique BIP-39 passphrase also creates an additional barrier.

However, a short, common, reused or predictable passphrase does not provide sufficient protection. A passphrase is also not the same as the PIN used to unlock the Coldcard device.

Even users with a strong passphrase are advised to migrate to a completely new seed because the passphrase only reduces immediate exposure. It does not repair the vulnerable seed from which the addresses were derived.

## Users Are Sending Bitcoin Back to Exchanges

The incident caused behavior opposite to what was observed after the collapse of FTX.

After the centralized exchange failed, users withdrew cryptocurrencies in large volumes to private wallets and hardware devices.

Now some bitcoin holders are sending funds back to exchanges because they temporarily see them as safer than potentially affected Coldcard addresses.

CryptoQuant data showed a sharp rise in smaller Bitcoin transfers and exchange deposits. Some of those transactions may belong to users moving funds to newly generated wallets, while others may be sending bitcoin temporarily to centralized platforms.

That does not mean centralized exchanges are generally safer than self-custody.

The incident shows that security does not depend only on whether a private key is stored offline. It also depends on whether the seed from which the key was created was generated with sufficiently strong randomness.

## Where Is the Stolen Bitcoin Now?

According to the blockchain analyses available so far, most of the stolen bitcoin remains on the Bitcoin network at addresses controlled by the attackers.

During the earlier waves, part of the funds was consolidated into a smaller number of addresses.

In the third wave, the funds were distributed across more separate destinations, making them harder to present as one large balance and harder to connect directly with the earlier waves.

There is no confirmation that the stolen BTC was sent directly to an identified centralized exchange, converted into fiat currency or exchanged for ETH and moved through Tornado Cash.

Native bitcoin cannot be sent directly through Tornado Cash because the protocol does not operate on the Bitcoin network. The attacker would first need to exchange BTC for an asset on a supported EVM blockchain, creating additional blockchain traces and potential points where the funds could be identified or frozen.

## An Air-Gapped Device Cannot Protect a Weak Seed

Coldcard devices are known for being usable without a direct internet connection.

An air gap can reduce the risk of attacks during communication, computer connectivity and transaction signing.

However, it cannot protect funds if the private key was derived from a predictable seed.

In this case, the device can remain completely offline while the attacker still accesses the funds because the attacker is not targeting the device itself. Instead, the attacker is searching the limited set of keys the vulnerable firmware may have generated.

That makes the incident an important warning for the entire hardware-wallet industry.

Security depends on more than physical device protection, secure elements, a PIN and an air-gapped workflow. The foundation remains high-quality and genuinely unpredictable entropy when generating the private key.

## The Attack Expanded to Smaller Wallets

The Coldcard incident developed from one rapid attack into a multi-day and systematic draining of thousands of Bitcoin addresses.

The identified losses have now risen to approximately 1,367 BTC, nearly $89 million and 4,585 affected addresses.

The third wave shows that attackers are no longer targeting only large wallets. As the largest balances are drained, the search continues toward addresses holding smaller amounts.

Users should not assume they are safe simply because a potentially vulnerable address does not contain a large bitcoin balance.

The most important step is not only updating the firmware, but generating a completely new seed on corrected firmware and moving funds to addresses derived from that new seed.
