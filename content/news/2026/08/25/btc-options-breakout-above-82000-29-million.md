# $2.9 million on Bitcoin above $82,000

One or more large traders paid almost **$2.9 million** for a short-term position designed to benefit if Bitcoin continues its strong rise.

According to data from Laevitas cited by CoinDesk, **2,000 Bitcoin call options with an $82,000 strike price and a September 4 expiry** were bought on August 24. The purchase came after a strong multi-day Bitcoin rally toward the $80,000 area.

At first glance, the story seems simple: someone put $2.9 million on Bitcoin rising above $82,000. But options work differently from simply buying BTC or opening a futures long. The trader **does not even have to wait for Bitcoin to cross $82,000** to make a profit on the position.

## What was actually bought?

The traders bought **call options**. A call option gives the buyer exposure to a rise in the price of an underlying asset — in this case Bitcoin — relative to a predetermined price known as the **strike**.

The strike on these options is **$82,000**, and the expiry date is **September 4, 2026**.

With Deribit BTC options, one contract represents **1 BTC**, so 2,000 purchased contracts represent options exposure linked to 2,000 Bitcoin. At the $82,000 strike price, that corresponds to a nominal value of approximately **$164 million**.

That does not mean someone invested $164 million. The buyer paid around **$2.9 million in option premium**. The premium is the price of the option itself and is paid upfront.

Dividing $2.9 million by 2,000 contracts gives an average dollar-equivalent premium of approximately **$1,450 per option**, with the important caveat that Deribit's inverse BTC options are actually quoted and settled in BTC.

## Why options instead of futures?

The trader could have used the same $2.9 million as margin for a large leveraged Bitcoin futures position. Futures and purchased call options, however, have very different risk profiles.

If a futures long is opened with high leverage, a sufficiently large temporary Bitcoin decline can consume the margin and lead to forced liquidation. A trader can correctly predict the eventual direction of the market and still lose the position because Bitcoin **moved the wrong way first**. Imagine a path where Bitcoin falls from **$79,000 to $76,000**, then several days later jumps to **$85,000**. A highly leveraged futures long could be liquidated during the initial decline. The later rally would no longer help because the position would already be gone.

The buyer of a call option has already paid the premium. The market value of the option can fall sharply when BTC falls, but a standard purchased call does not have the same forced-liquidation mechanism as a leveraged futures long. As long as the contract has not expired and the trader has not sold it, the option still exists. If Bitcoin then recovers sharply, its value can rise again.

## The option can be sold before expiry

Even more importantly, the trader **does not have to wait until September 4** and does not have to wait until Bitcoin actually crosses $82,000.

An option is a financial derivative with **its own market price**. It is not a stock and does not represent an ownership stake in a company, but in one important respect it behaves like other tradable market instruments: it can be bought, its price can rise or fall, and its owner can sell it again.

If the trader paid the equivalent of around **$1,450** for an option and Bitcoin then rapidly moves closer to the $82,000 strike several days later, the market value of that option can increase substantially.

For example, an option bought for $1,450 could, under favorable market conditions, be worth **$2,300** a few days later. The trader could then sell it to another buyer and make approximately **$850 profit per option**, even if Bitcoin had not yet reached $82,000.

The $2,300 figure is only an illustrative example. The actual market value of an option depends on Bitcoin's price, time remaining until expiry, implied volatility, demand and other market variables.

## How much can an option rise?

Historical examples show that the value of short-dated BTC options can change much faster than the price of Bitcoin itself.

Deribit has previously described cases in which Bitcoin call options multiplied in value after BTC moved rapidly toward the strike, even though Bitcoin itself had not yet reached that strike price.

In one example from 2022, a large trader bought Bitcoin calls with a $45,000 strike while BTC was trading around $39,000. When Bitcoin later climbed toward $42,000, the premium value of those calls approximately doubled even though Bitcoin never reached the $45,000 strike.

In another Deribit educational example, a call buyer paid **0.10 BTC** for an option. Several days later, the option was worth **0.18 BTC**. Selling before expiry would have produced a gain of 0.08 BTC, or around **80% on the premium paid**.

That explains why the word **"rapid"** matters so much in CoinDesk's description of the current trade. The trader may not simply expect Bitcoin to be above $82,000 at some point. They may expect BTC to **approach that level very quickly**, which could cause the options themselves to rise sharply before expiry.

## What does the $82,000 strike mean?

The strike is the predetermined price written into the options contract. For these calls, it is **$82,000**.

If the trader holds the option all the way to expiry, the option begins to have intrinsic value above the strike price. For example, if Bitcoin is worth $85,000 at expiry, an $82,000 call has approximately $3,000 of intrinsic value per BTC before accounting for the premium previously paid.

But **the strike is not the same as the break-even price**.

The buyer has already paid for the option. In a simplified dollar calculation, if the premium was around $1,450 per contract, the break-even at expiry would be approximately **$83,450**. Only above that level does the total value of the option cover the premium that was paid.

If BTC were worth **$85,000 at expiry**, the simplified calculation would look approximately like this:

**$85,000 − $82,000 = $3,000 intrinsic value**

Subtract around **$1,450 in premium paid**, leaving approximately **$1,550 net profit per option**.

Across 2,000 options, that would amount to approximately **$3.1 million in profit**, before costs and using the simplified dollar calculation.

Deribit BTC options are inverse contracts and are actually settled in BTC, so the exact result is not literally equal to simple dollar addition and subtraction. However, the example illustrates the basic economics of the trade.

## Why would someone later pay more for the same option?

Suppose the first trader bought the option for approximately **$1,450**, Bitcoin then quickly rises to $85,000, and several days remain before expiry.

The option would already have substantial intrinsic value, but it could also retain additional **time value**. A new buyer might believe Bitcoin will not stop at $85,000 but will climb to $88,000, $90,000 or higher before expiry.

That is why someone might be willing to pay, for example, **$3,500 or $4,000** for an option that the first trader bought for $1,450.

The original trader could then sell and lock in a substantial profit on the option itself.

The new buyer, however, has a completely different entry price and a new break-even level. If they pay $4,000 for the option, it is no longer enough for Bitcoin simply to remain at $85,000 at expiry. They are paying the higher price because they expect further upside or because they believe they can sell the option even more expensively to another buyer before expiry.

## Where are Bitcoin options traded?

One of the world's largest markets for crypto options is **Deribit**, a platform specializing in Bitcoin and other crypto derivatives.

Traders can choose between options with different expiry dates and different strike prices. In this case, the trade involved call options with an $82,000 strike and a September 4 expiry.

The market works through buy and sell orders. When one trader buys an option, there is a seller on the other side. If the owner wants to exit the position several days later, the option can be sold to another market participant at the prevailing market price.

Deribit is the marketplace that matches these orders. The trader does not have to wait for the formal expiry of the option to close the position.

## Can users in the EU trade crypto options?

Access to crypto options from the EU is more complex than simply buying Bitcoin on the spot market.

Options and futures are **derivative financial instruments**, and such products can fall under MiFID II rules and other regulations governing financial instruments and retail investor protection. Availability therefore depends on more than whether a user can technically open an account on a particular platform.

Deribit requires user identification and a KYC process, and clients are classified according to their status. Availability of individual derivatives depends on jurisdiction, regulatory rules, client classification and the platform's current rules.

The European Union as a whole is not one simple category in which every retail user automatically has access to every crypto derivative. Before trading, a user therefore needs to verify whether they are permitted to access such a product under the rules of their jurisdiction and the platform.

## While some buy calls, others buy protection

Perhaps the most interesting part of the current story is that the rest of the options market is not showing the same level of optimism.

While one or more traders paid almost $2.9 million for call options positioned for upside, the broader short-term options market is simultaneously showing increased demand for **put options**, which are often used as protection against falling prices.

According to Laevitas data cited by CoinDesk, Bitcoin's seven-day options skew moved from **+2.36% to -5.17%**, which in this metric indicates a rise in the relative price of and demand for downside protection.

In other words, two very different bets are being made in the same market at the same time.

One side of the capital is saying: **I don't want to miss a possible continuation of the rally.**

The other is saying: **the rise has been so fast that I want insurance in case the market suddenly reverses.**

## What does the $2.9 million trade really tell us?

Public data does not reveal the identity of the buyer, nor is it confirmed whether this was one trader or several. We also cannot see the entire portfolio.

A call option can be a straightforward bullish bet, part of a hedge strategy or simply one element of a much more complex derivatives position. For that reason, one trade alone does not prove that a large trader simply believes Bitcoin will definitely finish above $82,000.

What we do know is that someone was willing to **pay $2.9 million upfront** for short-term exposure to a possible new Bitcoin surge.

Choosing an option instead of a highly leveraged futures long also means that a temporary BTC decline will not automatically liquidate the purchased call. And if Bitcoin moves toward the strike quickly enough, the trader can sell the options before expiry and profit from the rise in the price of the options themselves.

The real message of this trade may therefore not be:

**"Bitcoin will be above $82,000 on September 4."**

It is closer to:

**"Someone believes there is a large enough chance of another rapid Bitcoin surge over the next roughly ten days to justify risking almost $2.9 million upfront."**
