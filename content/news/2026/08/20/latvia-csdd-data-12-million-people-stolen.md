# Latvia: Data of 1.2 Million People Stolen

Latvia’s Road Traffic Safety Directorate, CSDD, has confirmed one of the largest cybersecurity incidents to hit a state institution in the Baltics in recent years. Attackers extracted data linked to more than 1.2 million individuals as well as around 200,000 companies and other legal entities. Latvia has just over 1.8 million residents, meaning the number of people affected by the incident is equivalent to roughly two-thirds of the country’s current population.

## Data of 1.2 Million People Stolen

CSDD disclosed the full scale of the incident on August 18. The attack took place over the weekend of August 8 and 9, while the public was first informed about it on August 13. The attackers managed to gain partial access to CSDD’s information system and unlawfully extract historical data from payment receipts related to the agency’s services.

The compromised information includes personal identification numbers of citizens or registration numbers of legal entities, first and last names or company names, payment amounts and dates, vehicle registration plates, and addresses recorded at the time a service was provided. CSDD says users’ phone numbers and email addresses were not compromised, while usernames and passwords were also not affected according to the information released so far.

## Some of the Data Goes Back to 2008

The time span of the compromised records is particularly notable. CSDD confirmed that the stolen historical data from payment receipts goes back as far as 2008, meaning the incident does not affect only recent users of the system but also part of the data collected over nearly two decades of CSDD operations.

The fact that some of the data is old does not by itself explain why it was stored, nor does it mean there was no legal or operational reason to retain it. What has been confirmed is that data from that period was among the information the attackers were able to access.

## CERT.LV Warns of Targeted Scams

Latvia’s CERT described the incident as a targeted cyberattack that had been prepared in advance. According to CERT.LV, both the nature of the attack and the methods used point to a technically capable attacker. One of the biggest risks now is the possible secondary use of the stolen data.

The combination of a person’s name, personal identification number, vehicle registration plate, address and information about previous payments can allow criminals to create far more convincing phishing messages, SMS scams or fraudulent phone calls. Instead of sending a generic message, an attacker could mention genuine personal details or information linked to a victim’s vehicle and previous payments. This is why CERT.LV is warning citizens about scam attempts that may impersonate CSDD communications.

A similar case has also emerged in Croatia in recent days, where a database containing around 88,000 records of Croatian citizens was published online, including first and last names, OIB and JMBG identification numbers, and dates of birth. Croatia’s Ministry of Justice, Public Administration and Digital Transformation is checking the origin and authenticity of the published data.

## The Attack Became a Political Issue

The scale of the incident in Latvia quickly went beyond a purely technical security problem. Latvian President Edgars Rinkēvičs described the mass data breach as a significant threat to national security and said that CSDD’s reputation and public trust had been seriously damaged.

CSDD’s supervisory board subsequently resigned, while CSDD head Aivars Aksenoks also said he was prepared to step down after helping complete the investigation and manage the consequences of the attack. Latvian police opened a criminal investigation, while cybersecurity and data protection authorities continue their inquiries.

According to information CERT.LV provided to Latvia’s public broadcaster, the attackers exploited a vulnerability in a CSDD system accessible from the internet. It was also established that several mandatory cybersecurity requirements had not been met.

## CSDD Strengthened Security After the Attack

Following the incident, CSDD worked with CERT.LV to block the methods and channels used by the attackers and introduced additional security improvements. The agency also restricted the ability of unauthorised users to process vehicle data and obtain additional information such as make and model using only a vehicle registration plate.

CSDD was also targeted by another attempted cyberattack after the first incident, but the agency said that attempt was stopped thanks to security changes introduced after the initial breach. Regular CSDD services were not interrupted, and both in-person and electronic services remained available.

## What Should Citizens Watch For Now?

For citizens whose data may have been affected, the immediate risk is not only that someone gained access to old information, but also how those details can be combined in future fraud attempts. Particular caution is needed with messages, phone calls or emails that appear to come from CSDD, banks, insurers or other institutions and contain accurate personal information.

The fact that a message includes the correct name, address or vehicle registration plate is no longer proof that it really comes from an institution that legitimately knows those details. This is one reason why large data breaches can remain a security risk long after the original cyberattack is over.

### Data of 88,000 Citizens Also Leaked in Croatia

The Latvia case comes only days after personal data belonging to around **88,000 Croatian citizens** was published online. Hacker group INF Grupa claims the data was taken from Croatia’s justice system, while the published database contains first and last names, OIB and JMBG identification numbers, and dates of birth, and can be downloaded for free from the internet. Croatia’s Ministry of Justice, Public Administration and Digital Transformation is carrying out a technical review to establish the authenticity of the data and determine whether this is a new security incident or partly a republication of previously available records.
