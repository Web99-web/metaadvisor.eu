# EU Introduces AI Labels: What Must Be Disclosed?

Since August 2, 2026, new transparency obligations under Article 50 of the EU AI Act have applied across the European Union. Their purpose is to help people recognize when they are interacting with artificial intelligence or viewing content that has been generated or significantly altered by AI.

That does not mean every email, translation, business proposal or article created with the help of ChatGPT must automatically carry a large “written by AI” label.

The obligations depend on the type of AI system, the content it produces, how the content is published and whether it could mislead users.

## A Chatbot Must Disclose That It Is Not Human

Providers of interactive AI systems must inform users that they are communicating with artificial intelligence, unless this is already obvious from the circumstances and context of use.

This can apply to customer support chatbots, virtual assistants, AI agents and other systems that communicate directly with people.

The aim is to prevent situations in which a user believes they are speaking with a real employee, adviser or another person, while the responses are actually being generated by an AI system.

The disclosure must be clear and provided no later than the user’s first interaction with the system.

## AI Content Must Be Technically Detectable

Providers of generative AI systems must design their solutions so that synthetic audio, images, video and text can be identified as artificially generated or manipulated.

Machine-readable labels, metadata, digital signatures and other technical methods may be used to verify the origin of the content.

Such labeling does not always need to be clearly visible to an ordinary user. Its purpose is to allow other systems, platforms and verification tools to determine whether content was created with AI.

This obligation primarily applies to companies that develop and provide generative AI systems, not only to an individual who creates a single image or text.

## Deepfakes Must Be Clearly Labeled

A person or organization publishing a deepfake image, audio recording or video must disclose that the content was artificially generated or manipulated.

A deepfake is content that uses AI to convincingly depict real or fictional people, events, places or objects in a way that could be mistaken for authentic material.

Examples include a fake video of a politician, an artificially generated voice of a public figure or an altered recording of an event that never happened.

For artistic, satirical, creative and fictional works, a more appropriate form of disclosure may be used so that the audience’s experience is not unnecessarily disrupted. However, it must still be clear that the content is not an authentic recording of reality.

## What About AI-Generated Texts and Articles?

Special rules apply to AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public about matters of public interest.

Such content must, in certain circumstances, be disclosed as artificially generated or manipulated.

However, the AI Act provides an important exception where the text has undergone human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person assumes editorial responsibility for the publication.

It is therefore not the same to generate an article automatically and publish it without review, or to use AI for research, structuring, writing and translation while a newsroom later assumes responsibility for the final content.

The fact that AI contributed to the creation of a text does not automatically mean that every labeling obligation under Article 50 applies.

## Not Every Business Document Must Be Labeled

The new rules do not introduce a general obligation to label every piece of content created with the assistance of an AI tool.

An internal email, draft contract, business proposal, meeting summary or translation does not automatically need a public AI label simply because the author used ChatGPT or a similar tool.

The following questions should be considered:

* Is the content being published to the public?
* Does it concern a matter of public interest?
* Could it mislead users?
* Is it deepfake content?
* Has it undergone human review and editorial oversight?
* Is a person directly interacting with an AI system?

The claim that every text written with ChatGPT must be labeled from August 2 is therefore incorrect.

## Emotion Recognition and Biometric Categorization

Transparency obligations also apply to certain emotion-recognition and biometric-categorization systems.

People exposed to such systems must be informed that the technology is being used.

This can include systems that attempt to assess a person’s emotional state or categorize them based on voice, face, movement or other biometric data.

Such a notice does not mean every use of the system is automatically lawful. The GDPR and other relevant rules still apply to the processing of personal and biometric data.

## The EU Has Also Prepared Standardized Labels

The European Union has prepared visual labels that may be used to identify different types of AI-generated content.

Use of those specific icons is not mandatory. They are intended as a voluntary and standardized way to help users understand what type of AI content they are viewing.

The Code of Practice on the Transparency of AI-Generated Content is also voluntary. However, the legal obligations under Article 50 are not.

Companies that do not join the Code must still demonstrate through other appropriate methods that they comply with labeling and transparency requirements.

## Is “Created in Collaboration With AI” Enough?

The phrase “Created in collaboration with AI” is not an official label expressly required by the AI Act.

However, it can be a useful voluntary form of transparency because it tells readers that AI contributed to the creation of the content without claiming that AI was the sole author.

Such broad wording may be suitable for publishers, authors and small newsrooms that use AI for research, drafting, language editing, translation or content organization.

The disclosure does not replace specific obligations that apply to deepfakes, chatbots, emotion-recognition systems or certain fully AI-generated publications on matters of public interest.

## Penalties Will Not Necessarily Begin on the Same Day

The transparency obligations have applied since August 2, 2026. Their practical enforcement will nevertheless depend on the competent authorities, the specific circumstances and how an organization attempted to comply with the rules.

A limited transitional period applies to certain generative AI systems that were placed on the market before Article 50 began to apply. It relates to technical labeling and the detectability of AI-generated content.

That transitional period should not be understood as a general postponement of all transparency obligations.

Chatbots, deepfake content and other covered uses must be assessed separately according to their specific function and the rules that apply to them.

## Our Take

* The transparency obligations under Article 50 of the EU AI Act have applied since August 2, 2026.
* Users must be informed when they directly interact with an AI system, unless this is obvious.
* Generative AI systems must enable the technical detection of AI-generated or altered content.
* Deepfake images, video and audio must be clearly disclosed as artificially generated or manipulated.
* Special rules apply to AI-generated texts concerning matters of public interest.
* An exception exists where the text has undergone human review or editorial control and someone assumes editorial responsibility.
* Not every email, translation or business document created with AI assistance must automatically be labeled.
* People must be informed when they are exposed to certain emotion-recognition or biometric-categorization systems.
* Standardized EU icons and the Code of Practice may help with compliance, but the use of specific icons is not mandatory.
* The phrase “Created in collaboration with AI” can be a useful voluntary form of transparency.
* Such wording does not replace specific legal obligations where they apply.
* The key distinction is between ordinary AI assistance and content that may mislead the public about its origin or authenticity.
