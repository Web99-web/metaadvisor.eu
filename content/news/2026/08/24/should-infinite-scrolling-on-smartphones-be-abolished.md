# Should infinite scrolling on smartphones be abolished?

Instagram. One video, then another, then a third, and then the next. After an hour or two, the app is closed, but the smartphone remains in the user's hand. TikTok opens and the whole process begins again. Then perhaps Snapchat, followed by Instagram again, where new content has appeared in the meantime. What looks like the use of several different apps can in reality amount to hours of almost uninterrupted repetition of the same activity: scrolling.

In some families, this pattern can take up a large part of the day. Add together scrolling in bed first thing in the morning, social media throughout the day and returning to the same apps in the evening, and total use can approach eight hours. If school performance declines at the same time, it would be wrong to automatically claim that the smartphone is the only cause. But it is equally difficult to ignore the question of how much time, sleep and concentration remain for school, studying and other activities after several hours a day spent in a continuous stream of short-form content.

Parents are not in a simple position either. Many households no longer have a landline, which means the smartphone is simultaneously a means of communication, safety, school access, transport coordination and everyday arrangements. Taking the phone away completely is therefore often unrealistic, while constantly monitoring every app is not something most parents can do all day.

## Infinite scroll removed the moment when we stop

People spent hours online long before social media existed. They could read one newspaper, then a second, third and fourth, research a particular topic or look for information for work. The difference was that an article ended, a page had a bottom, and continuing required another conscious action.

Infinite scroll removed precisely those small moments of interruption. When one video ends, the next is already waiting. When one post disappears from the screen, another is already underneath it. The user does not have to decide to continue. The user has to decide to stop.

It is a small design change with a very large consequence.

During 2026, the European Commission placed exactly these kinds of features at the centre of its investigations into TikTok, Instagram and Facebook. Infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and highly personalised recommendations are no longer being viewed only as convenient features, but as possible elements of design that can encourage compulsive use.

## The problem does not end when one app is closed

The biggest weakness of today's limits is that usage time is mostly measured app by app. If a user spends an hour on TikTok, then an hour on Instagram and later opens TikTok again, the phone usually presents these as several separate activities.

For the user's attention, however, it may be one multi-hour session.

Android and iOS could therefore begin recognising the **type of use**, rather than only the name of the app. TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and similar feeds could be grouped into one category of endless or short-form content. Instead of simply saying that a user spent 52 minutes on Instagram and 48 minutes on TikTok, the phone could display something more meaningful, such as: **“Today you spent 7 hours and 40 minutes continuously scrolling.”**

Moving from one app to another should not be treated as a break either. If a user closes TikTok and opens Instagram a few seconds later, the scrolling has effectively continued.

## A ten-minute break is not a real break

Today's warnings often offer only a short interruption, or users can simply dismiss them. But ten or fifteen minutes can hardly interrupt a pattern that has continued for hours. During that time, someone may go to the bathroom, get something to eat or answer a few messages, then continue almost exactly where they left off.

If the goal is to genuinely interrupt the cycle, the break should be much more substantial — **90 to 120 minutes without endless feeds**.

That does not mean blocking the entire phone. Calls, messages, school apps, navigation, music and other normal functions would remain available. Only the category of endless feeds would be temporarily locked.

An hour and a half or two hours is long enough for someone to do something completely different. They may go outside, start studying, exercise, talk to someone or simply give their eyes and attention a proper break.

Even more importantly, knowing that such a break is coming could change behaviour before it begins. A user who knows that a long scrolling session will be followed by two hours without any endless feeds may stop earlier and reduce the amount of time spent inside an individual app.

## A feed does not have to be endless

Another possible solution would be to bring back the end of the page. After a certain number of posts or videos, the feed could simply stop and ask the user whether they want to load another block of content. Continuing would still be possible, but it would no longer happen completely automatically.

After several such continuations and a certain amount of total usage, a shared 90- to 120-minute break could begin. Autoplay could also be turned off by default, so that when one video ends, the user has to decide whether to start the next one.

The goal is not to ban social media, but to restore the moment in which the user has to consciously decide whether to continue.

## Rules for children should be much stricter

Adults can be given more choice. An adult can decide whether certain limits should be turned on or off.

For children and teenagers, that is not enough.

Accounts belonging to minors should have much stricter default settings for endless feeds, autoplay and shared limits. After a certain amount of scrolling, there should be a mandatory 90- to 120-minute break across all apps using the same type of feed, without the possibility of simply moving from TikTok to Instagram and continuing.

Parents already have various monitoring apps and parental-control systems, but that does not solve the basic problem. Parents do not have the time or energy to spend the entire day checking how long a child has been using one app, when they moved to another and whether they later reopened the first one. A technology system capable of precisely recommending content in order to keep users on a screen for as long as possible should also be capable of recognising hours of scrolling and automatically limiting it.

The responsibility therefore cannot be shifted almost entirely onto parents.

## Eight hours is one third of the day

If someone scrolls for eight hours a day, that is one third of the entire day. After eight hours of sleep, only another eight hours remain for school, studying, commuting, meals, exercise, friends, family and everything else.

The problem is that those eight hours rarely feel like one enormous session. The time is broken up throughout the day: Instagram in the morning, TikTok later, Instagram again, something else, and then the entire cycle starts over in the evening.

Usage statistics should therefore become clearer as well. Instead of showing only individual apps, a phone should show how much total time was spent in endless feeds and how many continuous sessions took place without a genuine break.

## Our view

* **The problem is no longer one app, but the entire cycle.** TikTok, Instagram and other feeds can together become one multi-hour scrolling session.
* **Limits for individual apps are not enough.** The operating system should add together time spent across all apps with endless feeds.
* **Short warnings achieve too little.** After prolonged scrolling, a real break should last **90 to 120 minutes**, while the phone's other functions continue to work normally.
* **A feed does not have to be infinite.** After a certain amount of content, users should consciously decide whether they want to continue, while autoplay should not be the default setting.
* **Rules for children and teenagers need to be much stricter.** Parents cannot monitor every app all day, so protection needs to be built into devices and platforms themselves.
* **There is no need to ban the internet or smartphones.** What needs to be limited is a design that allows users to scroll for hours without ever reaching a natural stopping point.
