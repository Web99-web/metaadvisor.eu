# MiCA Moves Toward DeFi — But First It Has to Find Someone to Regulate

Brussels is once again opening the question of where the boundaries of European crypto regulation should lie. This time, the focus is not on exchanges, stablecoins or token issuers, but on an area that MiCA largely left outside its original framework: decentralized finance, crypto lending and borrowing.

On May 20, 2026, the European Commission opened a targeted consultation on the review of MiCA. Among the areas explicitly being considered are DeFi, staking, lending and borrowing, and the Commission is asking a very simple question with potentially major consequences: should the lending and borrowing of crypto-assets be regulated?

The problem is that this question is much easier to answer than the next one: if the answer is yes — who exactly should be regulated?

## The problem starts with the word “decentralized”

MiCA already recognizes a distinction between more traditional crypto services and fully decentralized systems. Recital 22 states that crypto-asset services provided in a fully decentralized manner without any intermediary should not fall within the scope of the regulation.

But this is exactly where Brussels now runs into a problem. What does “fully decentralized” actually mean?

In its new consultation, the Commission is seeking views on criteria that could be used to determine whether a particular DeFi application is genuinely fully decentralized. Possible criteria include the existence of an identifiable intermediary or group of persons, control over important protocol functions through admin keys, concentration of governance power, custody of user assets, whether the code is open, and even whether an identifiable person or company is responsible for marketing the protocol.

In other words, calling something “DeFi” may no longer be enough. A protocol can run on a blockchain and use smart contracts while still having people or organizations that decide where capital is deployed, change risk parameters, approve new components or control access to certain functions.

And that is where the regulatory question begins: is this still just software, or is it already a financial service being provided by someone?

## Vaults make the issue even more complicated

DeFi lending vaults are a particularly interesting example. To users, they can appear very simple: deposit USDC, ETH or another asset, and the vault then attempts to generate yield by allocating capital across different lending markets. Economically, the result can resemble a financial product that accepts user capital, deploys it according to a particular strategy and generates returns.

But there does not necessarily have to be a traditional bank, fund or single company behind it performing all of those functions.

Morpho Vault V2 is a good example of this problem. Its architecture divides authority among at least four different roles: Owner, Curator, Allocator and Sentinel. The Owner controls the highest permission levels, the Curator sets risk configurations, adapters, limits and fees, the Allocator actively deploys capital, while the Sentinel can intervene when risk needs to be reduced.

Morpho describes its Vault V2 architecture as a non-custodial system in which no single party has complete control over users’ assets. The contracts use timelocks, separated permissions and mechanisms that allow users to exit individual positions.

That creates a very different problem for regulators than a traditional financial institution. If one person manages risk, another allocates capital, a smart contract executes transactions, governance approves certain components and the assets remain non-custodial — who is actually providing the financial service?

The Curator? The Owner? The smart contract developer? The DAO? The frontend that gives users access to the protocol? Or none of them individually?

## Brussels is also considering DeFi certification

An even more interesting part of the European consultation shows that the Commission is not thinking only about the traditional licensing model. One of the questions concerns possible certification of DeFi protocols and smart contracts. The Commission asks whether such certification should also cover services that MiCA currently does not regulate, including lending and borrowing.

It is even considering whether DeFi protocols that are not fully decentralized could be required to obtain some form of certification instead of a full CASP license, while also integrating certain compliance mechanisms directly into the design of the protocol.

But Brussels goes one step further. The consultation asks whether even fully decentralized protocols should be required to obtain certification once they reach a certain level of importance, for example based on total value locked — TVL.

An even more far-reaching possibility would be to regulate access to DeFi through intermediaries that the EU can already identify. The Commission asks whether CASPs should conduct due diligence on DeFi protocols to which they connect their clients, and even whether they should be prohibited from connecting users to protocols that are not certified.

## If you cannot regulate the protocol, regulate the gateway

This could become one of the most important questions in the future of European DeFi regulation. A fully decentralized smart contract can be very difficult to place under traditional regulatory supervision. The code can be publicly available, execute autonomously and remain accessible to users around the world.

But a European exchange, wallet provider or other regulated crypto service is not an anonymous smart contract. It has a legal entity, employees, a license and a regulator.

If Brussels concludes that it is not practical to regulate every decentralized protocol directly, it could instead try to regulate the way European users access them. Regulatory pressure could then shift away from DeFi protocols themselves and toward centralized exchanges, wallets and other services that act as bridges between users and the blockchain.

For users, this could mean that a particular protocol technically continues to exist and operate on the blockchain, while a regulated European service is no longer allowed to provide easy access to it.

## Lending is currently outside MiCA

It is important to emphasize that crypto lending and borrowing are currently not specifically regulated under MiCA. The European Commission explicitly states this in its consultation document and is therefore asking stakeholders whether these activities should be regulated and, if so, which regulatory requirements should apply.

This could become increasingly important as the line between an ordinary crypto wallet and a financial platform continues to blur. A user can hold a stablecoin in a wallet, deposit it into a lending protocol a few clicks later, earn yield, use a vault that automatically allocates funds, and potentially have no idea which smart contracts and protocols are being used in the background.

From the user's perspective, the experience can look almost identical to a traditional savings or investment product. From the blockchain's perspective, it is a series of transactions between smart contracts. And from the regulator's perspective, the main question remains: who is responsible when something goes wrong?

What happens if a smart contract contains a bug? What if the Curator chooses an excessively risky strategy? What if an oracle fails? What if users suffer major losses? Who has to explain the risks to users, and who can users complain to?

## MiCA has not yet been extended to DeFi

Still, it is important not to go one step too far: the European Commission has not yet decided to extend MiCA to DeFi lending, nor has it presented a final legislative proposal.

The document is an official consultation. The Commission itself stresses that it does not represent a final policy position or a formal legislative proposal. The results of the consultation are intended to help the Commission assess whether the existing framework should be amended or supplemented. The current deadline for responses is September 30, 2026.

But the questions Brussels is asking already show the direction in which the debate is moving. In its first version, MiCA largely regulated actors that are relatively easy to identify: token issuers, stablecoins and crypto-asset service providers.

DeFi challenges exactly that assumption.

Financial activity worth billions can exist, users can earn returns and take on credit risk, while there may be no single bank, no single director and not even one company behind the product to which a regulator can send a licensing requirement.

That is why the biggest problem facing future European DeFi regulation may be much simpler than the technology, blockchain or smart contracts themselves.

Before MiCA can regulate DeFi, Brussels first has to determine who it is actually regulating.

## Our take

* **DeFi is no longer outside Brussels’ regulatory focus.** The European Commission is officially considering whether the MiCA framework should be extended to lending, borrowing and other DeFi services.
* **The biggest problem is not only what to regulate, but who.** In decentralized protocols, responsibility can be distributed among developers, governance, curators, frontend operators and smart contracts.
* **Vaults particularly blur the line between software and a financial product.** To users, they can look like a simple way to earn yield, while management behind the scenes is divided among several different roles.
* **Brussels is also considering certification of protocols and smart contracts.** For parts of the DeFi market, this could become an alternative to a traditional CASP license.
* **Regulation could shift toward access points.** If the EU cannot practically regulate a decentralized smart contract, it may instead try to regulate exchanges, wallets and other services through which users access DeFi.
* **No final decision has yet been made.** This is a regulatory consultation and discussion of possible future changes to the MiCA framework, not an already adopted DeFi ban or new licensing requirement.
