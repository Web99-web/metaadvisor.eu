# US and Canada enter new round of trade conflict: 50% tariffs already in effect, Canada responds September 8

Donald Trump posted a new message on Truth Social on Sunday, August 23, at **1:06 a.m. US Eastern Time**, again escalating his rhetoric toward Canada. He wrote that Canada wants the “benefits of being a State, without being one” and once again raised the idea of Canada becoming the 51st US state.

The political tone of the message was predictably loud, but for markets and companies another part of the story matters more: **US tariffs of 50 percent on part of Canadian exports are already in effect**, while Canada has announced that it will respond with its own counter-tariffs in early September.

For European companies and investors, this is not simply a distant North American trade dispute. Changes in the prices of metals, auto parts, industrial equipment and other goods can quickly spill over into European supply chains, competitiveness and markets.

## What exactly is happening?

The new round of US tariffs took effect on **August 22 at 12:01 a.m. US Eastern Time**. The measures impose **50 percent tariffs** on Canadian goods with an estimated annual trade value of around **$20 billion**.

That does not mean all trade between the US and Canada is suddenly subject to new tariff rates. The measures affect part of the trade relationship and specific categories of goods, while other major sectors such as automobiles, steel and aluminium had already been subject to separate tariff measures and trade tensions.

Canada did not respond on the same day. Ottawa announced that its new countermeasures will begin on **September 8**, after the US Labor Day holiday. That leaves a period of slightly more than two weeks between the start of the US tariffs and the beginning of Canada’s response, during which markets and companies are trying to assess the real cost.

## Why does this matter economically?

The US and Canada have one of the most deeply integrated trade and manufacturing relationships in the world. Many raw materials, components and finished products cross the border several times before reaching the final customer.

That is why a tariff does not only hit the exporter on one side of the border. The additional cost can be passed on to the importer, a manufacturer using more expensive inputs, distributors, retailers and ultimately consumers.

This is why markets are not looking at the issue only through a political lens. The much more important question is: **who actually pays the 50 percent tariffs?**

## Which Canadian companies and industries could be most exposed?

Although not all details and the final impact on individual companies are yet fully clear, markets are already watching several Canadian names and industries that may be particularly exposed.

These include **Magna, Linamar and Martinrea**, major companies linked to auto parts and industrial manufacturing, as well as **Algoma Steel** in the steel sector and producers in the **wine, dairy and cement industries**.

For these companies and sectors, the issue is not only the direct tariff. Potential disruption to orders, margins and production planning may be equally important. If US customers reduce orders because of higher costs or begin looking for alternative suppliers, the economic impact on Canadian companies could become larger than the tariff rate itself suggests.

## Who could be hit by Canada’s response?

Canada has already indicated the direction of its retaliation, but **Ottawa has not yet published the final detailed list of all products** that will be subject to the new counter-tariffs from September 8.

Even so, markets are already watching several US companies and industries that could be among the more exposed. These include **Whirlpool** in home appliances, **Deere and AGCO** in agricultural equipment, and **Nucor, Steel Dynamics and Cleveland-Cliffs** in steel and industrial materials.

In other words, the abstract phrase “around $20 billion worth of goods” can quickly be translated into companies that investors and readers already recognise.

## Auto industry, steel and industrial goods under the most pressure

The biggest long-term risk remains connected to the **auto industry** and broader industrial supply chains. North American vehicle and component manufacturing operates as a deeply integrated system, meaning every new border cost can affect multiple factories on both the Canadian and US sides.

The same applies to **steel, aluminium, industrial materials, electronics and certain construction inputs**. At first glance, a tariff may look like protection for domestic producers, but companies that use those materials as inputs can quickly face higher production costs.

The real impact will therefore appear through several channels at once: commodity prices, manufacturers’ margins, possible declines in orders, supply-chain adjustments and changes in investment plans.

## What are markets watching now?

The first reaction is already visible in currency markets and investor sentiment. The Canadian dollar weakened after the negotiations collapsed, while Asian markets were weaker at the start of the week.

Over the coming days, markets will be watching three things in particular. First, whether Ottawa publishes a more precise list of products and sectors before September 8. Second, whether Washington introduces another round of pressure or instead signals some moderation. Third, how the affected companies themselves describe the impact on orders, input costs and margins.

In other words, although the US tariffs technically began on August 22, the economic story is only beginning. **The real cost of this new tariff round will not be measured only by political statements, but by who ultimately carries higher input costs, lower margins and potentially weaker demand.**
