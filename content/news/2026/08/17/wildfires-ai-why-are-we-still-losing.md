# Why, in the age of AI, are we still watching wildfires consume entire communities?

Europe is burning again. Canada is burning again. Large parts of the United States are once more fighting wildfires. And almost everywhere, the images look strikingly similar: hundreds of firefighters, kilometers of hoses, tankers, aircraft and helicopters trying to catch a fire that strong winds can sometimes push faster than the entire response system can reorganize.

**Defending one house is one thing. Trying to defend an entire part of a town while the wind creates new ignition points faster than firefighters can reach them is something entirely different.**

Across Europe, wildfires had affected around **576,000 hectares** by mid-August this year. Belgium is currently battling the largest recorded wildfire in its history, with around **3,000 hectares** affected in the High Fens and international assistance involved in the response. In Greece, strong winds again pushed fires toward populated areas, while major fires in Spain have burned vast areas and forced thousands of people to evacuate.

Canada is facing the same problem on an even larger scale. By the end of July, the country had almost **900 active fires**, with more than **3.7 million hectares** already burned. The United States, despite enormous technological, financial and logistical resources, also has no simple answer when a major wildfire turns into a fast-moving fire front.

All of this raises a question that is becoming harder to ignore in the 21st century: **we have artificial intelligence, autonomous vehicles, satellites, drones and robots — so why are we still unable to quickly stop a fire that is moving toward people?**

## AI can see the fire — but it still cannot stop it

The technology used to detect and track wildfires has advanced dramatically. Satellites can identify thermal anomalies close to real time, thermal cameras can spot hotspots through smoke, drones can map terrain from above, and computer models can combine wind, moisture, vegetation and topography to estimate where a fire may move next.

NASA and other institutions are already developing systems that attempt to follow wildfires almost like weather systems: not only showing where the fire is now, but where it could be in the next few minutes or hours. The real problem begins after that. There is still a huge technological gap between knowing that a fire will soon reach a particular location and being physically able to stop it.

Artificial intelligence can warn firefighters, identify a new ignition point and calculate the most likely direction of spread. But it cannot virtually remove combustible vegetation, deliver tens of thousands of liters of water to an inaccessible slope or stop the wind from carrying burning material across a defensive line.

## In extreme wildfires, there is no longer just one fire front

Under strong winds, a major wildfire can quickly stop behaving like one compact wall of fire. The wind does not only move flames. It can lift glowing bark, branches and other burning material and carry them far ahead of the main front.

New fires then start elsewhere. While firefighters are working on one location, another may ignite hundreds of meters away, followed by a third only minutes later. In extreme conditions, crews are no longer trying to catch one fire — they are trying to respond to a chain of new fires appearing almost simultaneously.

This is where the traditional model of firefighting begins to hit physical limits. The problem is not that firefighters do not know how to fight fire. The problem is that a wildfire can spread and create new ignition points faster than people, vehicles and aircraft can reach every one of them.

## A Croatian bodycam video shows what that looks like on the ground

The wildfire that devastated the area around Lokva Rogoznica and Omiš during the night of August 13 to 14 showed how quickly a situation can become almost impossible to control when strong winds, dry vegetation, difficult terrain and homes directly beside the fire zone come together.

According to figures released later, **106 vehicles, 28 homes, seven commercial buildings, three hospitality businesses and six auxiliary structures were completely destroyed**, in addition to further damage to homes and property. One person died.

But statistics cannot show what it looks like when the fire is no longer somewhere on a hillside and has already moved among homes and people. A bodycam video released by DVD Trogir shows that far more clearly.

**DVD Trogir is part of Croatia’s network of trained volunteer fire brigades.** The abbreviation DVD stands for *Dobrovoljno vatrogasno društvo*. These brigades are an organized part of Croatia’s firefighting system and respond to real emergencies alongside professional fire services.

The footage feels chaotic because the situation itself was chaotic. Visibility disappears in the smoke, fire appears from multiple directions, movement is restricted, and at some moments the firefighter is effectively surrounded by flames and dense smoke. There is no safe aerial overview showing the full map of the fire, and no time to calmly evaluate every decision. The camera shows only what a person on the ground actually sees: a few meters of road, houses, vehicles, approaching fire and the need to decide within seconds where to move next.

That may be the most important scene in the entire story. While AI can observe a wildfire from space, a firefighter on the ground still has to physically move through smoke and flames to try to protect people and buildings.

## When wildfire reaches homes, the rules change

Once a wildfire enters a populated area, vegetation is no longer the only thing burning. Facades, roofs, vehicles, awnings, gardens, wooden structures and everything between homes can ignite. People may be trying to escape along the same narrow roads that fire engines are using in the opposite direction, while smoke reduces visibility and the wind carries embers into new locations.

Difficult terrain makes the problem even worse. Large fire engines may not be able to get close enough. Roads may be too narrow, blocked by abandoned vehicles or already surrounded by fire, while flames move down a hillside toward homes and new fires appear behind a crew already fighting somewhere else.

At that point the question is no longer simply how to extinguish one fire. It becomes **how to protect dozens of locations in a very short period of time while the situation changes from minute to minute**.

## Why don’t we drop something in front of the fire before it reaches people?

That tactic already exists to some extent. Firefighting aircraft do not only drop water directly onto flames. Long-term fire retardants can be released **ahead of the fire front**, onto vegetation that has not yet burned, in order to reduce its flammability and slow the advance of the fire.

The idea is simple: rather than waiting for the flames to reach the last house, responders try to create an area in advance where the fire will move more slowly and with less intensity. In the best case, that gives firefighters time to defend a community, evacuate people or stop the fire at a more favorable location.

But today’s aerial systems have major limitations. A large aircraft can carry a significant amount of water or retardant, but after each drop it has to refill, it depends on weather conditions and safe flying conditions, and it cannot operate at ten separate locations at the same time.

A small drone, by contrast, can be precise, autonomous and sent close to a dangerous area, but it carries too little suppressant to stop a major fire front on its own. Between large high-capacity aircraft and small autonomous drones lies exactly the technological gap we have not yet solved.

## Could the future be a swarm of autonomous firefighting machines?

NASA is already developing technologies that could expand the role of unmanned aircraft in wildfire operations, including work at night and in conditions where conventional aerial operations are limited. The long-term idea is not simply to build one smarter drone, but an entire coordinated system.

Such a system could combine thermal imagery, satellite data, wind, terrain, moisture, vegetation and the location of people in real time. If AI calculates that a fire will approach a community within several minutes, it would not simply issue a warning. It could deploy aircraft to build a defensive retardant line ahead of the front while other drones immediately respond to new ignition points created by the wind.

On the ground, autonomous tankers or off-road vehicles could deliver water and firefighting agents, while robots equipped with water or foam cannons could remain in locations that have already become too dangerous for people. Firefighters would still command the operation and rescue people, but they would not necessarily have to be the first ones sent directly into the most dangerous part of the fire zone.

## Firefighting robots already exist — but a full autonomous ecosystem does not

Firefighting robots are no longer science fiction and are already used in real emergencies. Some can carry water cannons, thermal cameras and other equipment and enter areas where the heat or risk is too high for a person. Drones can rapidly map a fire and identify hotspots that crews on the ground cannot see.

The problem is that these are still mostly individual tools. One robot can fight one location, one drone can find one new hotspot, and one aircraft can make one large drop. A major wind-driven wildfire can create dozens of new problems during the same period.

What is still not widely deployed is **a network of dozens or hundreds of autonomous machines that can receive a shared picture of the fire within seconds and respond to multiple new ignition points almost simultaneously**.

That may be the level of automation extreme wildfires actually require.

## Have we advanced faster in the digital world than in the physical one?

The past decade has brought explosive progress in generative AI, autonomous driving, industrial robotics and machines capable of performing increasingly complex tasks on their own. By comparison, the basic physics of firefighting has changed much less: water or another suppressant still has to physically reach the fire.

That does not mean there is a simple solution, or that a single device could replace firefighters overnight. Fire is one of the hardest environments to automate. Smoke can blind cameras, heat can destroy electronics, strong winds can change direction within moments, communications can fail and terrain can become completely impassable.

That is exactly why the technological question is so interesting. If we can develop vehicles that navigate busy cities autonomously, can the next step be machines that autonomously respond to a wildfire before it reaches people?

## Our view (Metaadvisor):

* Major fires in Belgium, Greece, Spain, Croatia, Canada and the United States are again showing that extreme wildfire can spread faster than conventional firefighting forces can respond.
* AI, satellites, thermal cameras and drones are becoming increasingly capable of detecting fires and predicting their movement, but physical fire suppression still relies heavily on people, vehicles and conventional firefighting aircraft.
* In strong winds, the biggest problem is often not only the main fire front but new ignition points appearing ahead of it and among homes.
* Retardants can already be dropped ahead of wildfire fronts, but existing aircraft are limited by speed, safety and the number of locations they can cover at the same time.
* Autonomous drones, firefighting robots and AI systems already exist, but they are not yet connected into a large coordinated network capable of responding to dozens of new fires in real time.
* The next major technological step may therefore not be another better wildfire prediction system, but machines that can physically act before the fire reaches people.

We may never be able to completely stop every major natural wildfire. But in a world where artificial intelligence can see fire from space, calculate where it is going and warn us before it arrives, it is difficult not to ask why the final line of defense still so often depends on a human being moving through smoke and flames with a hose.

Maybe we do not only need a smarter system that can tell us **where the fire will be**.

Maybe we need technology that can get there **before the fire does**.
