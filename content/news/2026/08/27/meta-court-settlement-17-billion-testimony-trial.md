# Meta agrees to a court settlement of up to $17 billion after uncomfortable testimony from former and current employees

Meta agreed to one of the largest settlements in the history of the technology industry just as a federal court trial in Oakland began revealing what former and current employees had said about child safety, user engagement and internal decisions at Facebook and Instagram.

The lawsuit was brought by a coalition of US states that alleged Meta deliberately designed Facebook and Instagram to encourage compulsive use among children and teenagers, collected data from children under 13 without appropriate parental consent, and presented the public with a picture of the safety of its platforms that did not match what the company knew internally. Meta denies the allegations.

The trial began on August 18 and was expected to last six to eight weeks. Plaintiffs had discussed potential penalties reaching roughly $200 billion. However, little more than a week after the trial began, Meta agreed to a settlement, bringing one of the most significant cases against the company in recent years to an early end.

## Arturo Béjar: child safety was not a priority

One of the most important witnesses was Arturo Béjar, a former Meta engineering director and safety expert who worked at the company during two separate periods between 2009 and 2021.

Béjar told the jury that Meta's culture was heavily focused on user growth, engagement and time spent on its platforms, while safety often remained secondary. According to his testimony, Mark Zuckerberg held such centralized control over important decisions that significant product changes practically did not happen unless Zuckerberg himself made them a priority.

Béjar said Meta effectively operated a "don't ask, don't tell" policy toward children under 13: the company officially maintained that children under 13 were not allowed to use its platforms, but according to his testimony did not make sufficient efforts to actively identify and remove them.

A particularly uncomfortable part of his testimony concerned data Béjar sent to Zuckerberg in 2021. According to internal documents shown in court, research found that 51% of surveyed young users had experienced something bad or harmful on Instagram during the previous seven days, while content was removed in only a very small proportion of reported cases. Béjar said he personally sent the findings to Zuckerberg because experience had taught him that things changed inside the company when Zuckerberg made them a priority. Asked whether Zuckerberg responded, Béjar said he did not.

Before the trial, Meta had attempted to prevent or limit Béjar's testimony, challenging his credibility and some of the evidence he wanted to present. The court did not accept those attempts.

## George Volichenko: was the mental health team meant to protect the company?

Even more uncomfortable testimony came from former employee George Volichenko, who worked at Meta in marketing analytics and as a data scientist, and later joined Instagram's user mental wellbeing team.

Volichenko said that during his second period at Meta, he was told that the team's primary purpose was to protect the company from future lawsuits. He said that what he saw while working there largely confirmed that description.

His team also discussed Instagram's "Take a Break" feature, which prompts users to take a break after spending an extended period on the platform. Volichenko said the team wanted the feature to be automatically enabled for younger teenagers rather than requiring them to switch it on themselves.

The proposal was not approved. According to his testimony, the reason was its impact on key business metrics — how long and how frequently people used Instagram. Volichenko left the company again shortly afterwards, saying the company's values no longer aligned with his own.

## Francesco Fogu and the sentence about paying the fine

Francesco Fogu, Instagram's director of product design, also testified before the jury.

Plaintiffs showed him internal documents and conversations, including a particularly uncomfortable formulation suggesting that **companies sometimes decide not to comply with a regulatory demand and simply accept the penalty instead**, while in other cases they comply only to the minimum extent necessary.

Fogu repeatedly said he could not remember whether he had personally written those words. After repeated questioning, Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers even intervened.

**If a company deliberately compares the cost of complying with a regulatory demand against the size of a potential penalty and concludes that it is financially more advantageous not to comply and simply pay the fine, the sanction stops functioning as a deterrent and becomes another cost of doing business. In other words, a company can consciously decide not to follow a regulatory demand because doing so is more profitable.**

That does not automatically mean that every regulatory demand is equivalent to a statutory legal obligation, nor does it mean Meta admitted a specific violation of law through this internal wording. But it raises a much more serious question: **what happens when a company is so large and profitable that a potential penalty can simply be built into the cost of continuing an existing practice?**

## Adam Mosseri: only 1.8% of teenagers enabled "Take a Break"

Instagram head Adam Mosseri became the first senior current Meta executive to testify personally at the trial.

One of the central issues was again the "Take a Break" feature. When it was introduced, Meta publicly presented it as one example of the company's efforts to protect the wellbeing of younger users. But the trial revealed that initially only around 1.8% of teenagers enabled the feature.

Mosseri defended the company, arguing that Meta does not publish every internal statistic and that he had publicly acknowledged that adoption of the feature was low. Only later, following the introduction of Teen Accounts, did the feature become automatically enabled for teenagers.

Mosseri was due to continue his testimony the following day.

He did not.

The settlement was announced before his testimony resumed.

## Former researchers and a psychologist

The jury also watched previously recorded testimony from former Meta UX researchers Elena Davis and Natalie Troxel.

Jean Twenge, a psychology professor at San Diego State University known for research into digital technology use and the mental health of young people, also testified. Her testimony was expected to continue the following week.

The plaintiffs were therefore beginning to build a combination of internal documentation, testimony from people who had worked directly on Meta's products, and external expert analysis of the consequences of their use.

## Zuckerberg was also expected to testify

The most important testimony was still to come.

Mark Zuckerberg was scheduled to appear as a witness as the trial continued. Meta's witness list also included several senior executives and employees, including Chief Privacy Officer Michel Protti, Global Head of Safety Antigone Davis, youth product management director Allison Hartnett, and Sayed Otaru, director of product staff for Instagram Teen Accounts.

The settlement ended the trial before Zuckerberg had to appear before the jury in this case and before many of Meta's planned witnesses could undergo full examination and cross-examination.

That is one of the most important consequences of the settlement, and one that can easily be obscured by the enormous financial figure.

Meta did not merely limit its potential financial exposure. The settlement also ended a process in which people who made or implemented decisions inside the company would have spent the following weeks testifying under oath while plaintiffs presented internal emails, research, presentations and conversations to the jury.

The settlement itself is not evidence that Meta was guilty. Meta continues to expressly deny the allegations. From a procedural perspective, however, the settlement removed the risk of further public testimony and the creation of an additional court record that could potentially be relevant in other cases.

## How much will Meta actually pay?

Different figures appear in reports because they refer to different parts of the broader agreement.

The California Attorney General officially describes the settlement as worth up to approximately **$17 billion**, while Meta itself describes the broader financial framework as approximately $18 billion.

Most importantly, the entire amount will not be paid immediately.

**The guaranteed portion of approximately $12.7 billion is spread over ten years.**

A further approximately $5.3 billion is tied to specific conditions, including whether TikTok and YouTube adopt comparable measures to protect teenagers and corresponding financial commitments.

**That means Meta may never have to pay the full maximum amount presented in headlines as $17 billion or approximately $18 billion. Part of the total value of the settlement is not a guaranteed payment by Meta, but depends on additional conditions being met.**

Meta expects to record approximately $10 billion in legal expenses related to the settlement in the third quarter of 2026, even though the actual payments will be distributed over a longer period.

## How much is $17 billion for a company like Meta?

The number sounds almost unimaginably large. But it has to be compared with the scale of the company itself.

Meta generated $200.97 billion in revenue and $60.46 billion in net income in 2025.

Using $16.68 billion as the benchmark, that represents approximately 8.3% of Meta's 2025 annual revenue. Compared with net income, the same amount represents roughly 27.6% of Meta's entire 2025 net profit.

Meta's average monthly net income based on those annual results was approximately $5.04 billion. In other words, $16.68 billion is equivalent to roughly 3.3 months of Meta's average 2025 net profit.

However, that comparison can create the false impression that Meta has to pay the entire amount at once.

**If the guaranteed $12.7 billion is divided across ten years, it averages approximately $1.27 billion per year — around 2.1% of Meta's 2025 net income.**

That is an enormous amount in absolute terms. But for a company generating more than $60 billion in annual net profit, the actual annual financial burden is very different from paying the headline figure of $17 billion all at once.

## Meta did not admit wrongdoing in the settlement

It is important to emphasize that agreeing to a settlement is not legally the same as admitting liability.

Meta says it did not do what the states accuse it of doing and presents the agreement as a deal establishing a new industry standard for protecting children and teenagers.

The US states, however, obtained more than money. The agreement also provides for product changes including time limits for teenagers, nighttime restrictions, muted notifications during school hours, removal of visible like counts for younger users, restrictions on certain filters, an option for a non-personalized feed, stronger age-assurance systems, and an independent auditor with access to the information required to oversee implementation.

Another particularly interesting obligation is that Meta will be subject to a court order prohibiting future false, misleading or deceptive statements about its safety features.

## Did Meta effectively buy an end to the testimony?

That cannot be presented as a proven motive of the company.

But that is precisely one of the consequences of the settlement.

A trial that was expected to continue for several more weeks ended after testimony from whistleblowers, former employees, researchers, designers and the head of Instagram. Zuckerberg had not yet testified. The Chief Privacy Officer had not yet testified. The Global Head of Safety and other planned witnesses had also not yet undergone their scheduled questioning.

At the same time, the first days of the trial featured claims from former employees that safety measures were sometimes subordinated to engagement metrics, that certain protective features were not automatically enabled because of their impact on platform use, and that a team focused on user mental wellbeing primarily existed to protect the company from future litigation.

For a company that earned more than $60 billion after expenses in 2025, a settlement of up to approximately $17 billion is extraordinarily large.

But in return, Meta gets greater certainty over a significant portion of its legal risk, the guaranteed payments are spread across **ten years** — and the trial ends before some of the most important people inside the company have to testify publicly.

## Our view

* **The headline figure of $17 billion is not necessarily the amount Meta will actually have to pay.** The guaranteed portion is approximately $12.7 billion and is spread over ten years, while an additional part depends on specific conditions being met.
* **It is particularly significant that the trial ended before Mark Zuckerberg and several other senior Meta executives testified.** The settlement stopped a process that could have produced considerably more public testimony and internal documentation.
* **The testimony of former employees raises serious questions about whether safety measures were at times subordinated to engagement and business metrics.**
* **The internal wording about accepting a penalty rather than complying with a regulatory demand may be one of the most uncomfortable parts of the entire story.** If it is financially more advantageous for a company not to comply and simply pay the penalty, a regulatory sanction can stop acting as a deterrent and become merely the price of continuing an existing practice.
* **The settlement is not an admission of wrongdoing.** But it did end the trial before some of the most important people at Meta had to answer questions under oath and face cross-examination.
