# Meta's former safety director used Signal, not WhatsApp: why did Arturo Béjar choose the secure messaging app?

One detail from the major US court case against Meta stands out. Arturo Béjar, a former Facebook director who spent years working specifically on user safety, used Signal for part of his sensitive communications connected to cases against Meta — not WhatsApp, which belongs to the company where he had worked for years.

The fact that he used Signal does not prove that Béjar distrusted WhatsApp, and the publicly available court material does not support such a conclusion. But his choice of app is especially interesting because of his professional background: this was someone who had spent years working on safety at Facebook and who later explained that he takes the security of his own communications very seriously.

## "Professional paranoid": why did Béjar use Signal?

During an earlier examination of his communication habits, Béjar discussed his use of Signal and disappearing messages, meaning messages that automatically delete after a set period.

He described himself, in the context of communications security, as something of a **"professional paranoid"** and explained that he pays close attention to his communications OPSEC — operational security.

In the security world, OPSEC is not only about whether someone can read the content of a message. It also concerns which channel is used, what information remains stored, how long it exists, who can access it and what digital trail the communication leaves behind.

That approach is one reason Signal has become popular among security professionals, journalists, activists, lawyers and others for whom communication privacy is especially important.

## Did he use Signal privately and WhatsApp for work?

There is not enough publicly available evidence to support that kind of split.

We cannot say that Béjar used Signal for private conversations and WhatsApp for professional communication, nor can we say that he chose Signal because he did not trust WhatsApp. The material that became relevant in court concerned communications linked to legal proceedings against Meta.

The most precise formulation is therefore that he **used Signal with disappearing messages enabled for part of his sensitive communications**.

For a former Facebook safety director, the choice of a competing privacy-focused app remains a notable detail.

## Meta tried to use Signal against him

In the court proceedings, Signal became much more than an interesting side detail.

Before the start of the major US trial, Meta asked the federal judge not to allow Béjar to testify at all. One important part of the company's argument focused directly on his communications through Signal.

Meta argued that Béjar had used disappearing messages in communications with state attorneys general and other people connected to the cases and that, as a result, messages that might have been relevant evidence were no longer available.

The company argued that Béjar knew he had an obligation to preserve relevant communications and that continuing to use automatically disappearing messages amounted, at minimum, to gross negligence and, according to Meta's argument, potentially bad faith.

Meta sought a serious consequence: one of the states' key witnesses would not be allowed to appear before the jury at all.

## The judge did not accept Meta's attempt to exclude him

Federal Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers rejected the request to exclude Béjar completely as a witness.

Reports about the court ruling described Meta's move as effectively a **"Hail Mary" attempt to eliminate a strong witness for the plaintiffs**.

The court found that Meta had not sufficiently shown that Béjar had a legally enforceable obligation to permanently preserve those particular Signal messages in the way the company claimed. Béjar was therefore allowed to testify, although Meta could still question the circumstances under which those communications were no longer available.

In other words, the same feature that attracts many users to Signal — the ability for messages to disappear after a certain period — became the center of a procedural dispute over whether Béjar should be allowed to speak to the jury at all.

## Disappearing messages are not the same as destroying evidence

It is important to distinguish between two things.

Using disappearing messages is not, by itself, evidence that someone is trying to hide something. Signal offers the feature to all users as a standard privacy tool, and messages can automatically disappear after a period chosen by the users themselves.

The situation becomes legally more sensitive if a person already has a concrete obligation to preserve records because of an ongoing or expected court case. At that point, the issue is no longer whether the feature is lawful or normal, but whether specific communications were required to be preserved.

That was precisely the dispute between Meta and Béjar.

## Why do security professionals choose apps like Signal?

Signal was built around a very different philosophy from most large communication platforms. Message content is protected with end-to-end encryption, while the organization behind the app seeks to collect as little metadata about its users as possible.

The app does not rely on a traditional business model based on advertising and user profiling. It is developed by the nonprofit Signal Foundation, and its encryption protocol has become so influential that the Signal Protocol is also used in other communication systems.

WhatsApp also uses end-to-end encryption for personal conversations and uses the Signal Protocol, so it would not be accurate to describe WhatsApp as an app without security or encryption.

The difference is broader. Signal was designed from the outset around the idea of collecting as little information as possible about the user and their communications.

That is why Béjar's choice of Signal as a security professional remains interesting even without speculating about what he personally thought of WhatsApp.

## Our view

* **Béjar's choice of Signal is particularly interesting because of his professional background.** He spent years working specifically on Facebook user safety and used an app built around privacy and minimal data retention for part of his sensitive communications.
* **We do not know why he did not use WhatsApp.** There is not enough evidence to conclude that he distrusted WhatsApp or that he used Signal privately and WhatsApp professionally.
* **Béjar himself emphasized the importance of OPSEC.** As a security professional, he described himself in the context of protecting his communications as something of a "professional paranoid".
* **Disappearing messages became a problem only in the context of the legal proceedings.** The feature itself is not evidence of concealment, but preserving communications can become a legal obligation when they are relevant to an existing or anticipated case.
* **Meta tried to turn the Signal communications into a reason why Béjar should not be allowed to testify at all.** The court rejected that request and Béjar was still allowed to appear before the jury.
* **The paradox remains interesting.** A former safety director of a company that owns WhatsApp chose Signal for sensitive communications — but without additional evidence, that should be treated as an interesting fact rather than proof of distrust toward Meta's own messaging app.
