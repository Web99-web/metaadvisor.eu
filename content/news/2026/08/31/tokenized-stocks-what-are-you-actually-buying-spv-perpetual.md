# From Wall Street’s “paperwork crisis” to tokenized stocks: what are you actually buying?

Tokenized stocks are appearing increasingly often on crypto exchanges, blockchains, DeFi platforms and trading apps. On screen, a user may see a familiar name such as **Nvidia, Tesla or another major company**, a price that tracks the value of the stock and a simple Buy button.

But behind almost identical user interfaces can sit completely different financial and legal products.

One token may be **1:1 backed by a real share**. Another may represent economic exposure to a security through a special legal structure. A third may be a derivative. A fourth may be a perpetual contract where no actual share ever needs to be purchased for the user.

That is why Fairmint CEO Joris Delanoue warns that the tokenized stock market could repeat a problem Wall Street experienced in the late 1960s — only this time without physical paper.

## Wall Street already had a recordkeeping problem once — the “paperwork crisis”

In the late 1960s, the U.S. stock market faced what became known as the **“paperwork crisis.”**

Trading volume exploded while brokers, exchanges and transfer agents were still processing much of the system using physical ownership certificates, manual records and enormous amounts of paperwork. Infrastructure designed to handle a few million shares per day could no longer keep pace with a market processing many times that volume by the end of the decade.

The problem became so severe that it went far beyond slower settlement.

**Wall Street literally had to close markets to catch up with the paperwork.**

In January 1968, exchanges first shortened daily trading by 90 minutes. In June, they restored a full trading day but began **closing exchanges one day each week** so back-office employees could process the growing backlog of transactions. Wednesday became known as a non-trading day precisely so brokers could catch up with mountains of certificates, transfers and other documents. At the beginning of 1969, the five-day trading week returned, but once again with shortened hours. The SEC now notes that, at some points, the NYSE had to close for **as many as two days in a single week** so the system could catch up with the backlog.

By December 1968, the value of unresolved transactions had grown to approximately **$4 billion**, and the SEC later described the settlement system as having effectively broken down. Certificates were delayed, lost or sent to the wrong places, dividends were misdirected and some brokerage firms did not survive the operational chaos.

That crisis accelerated the development of more centralized infrastructure for clearing, settlement and securities recordkeeping, including the system that would later become the Depository Trust Company and today’s DTCC. Instead of physically moving certificates between buyers, brokers and sellers, ownership increasingly began to be transferred through electronic book entries.

Today, we no longer have clerks pushing carts full of paper certificates around Wall Street.

But we could get something else: **a large number of blockchains, tokens, SPVs, wrappers, derivatives and private records representing the same economic asset in different ways.**

## Fairmint warns about a digital version of the same problem

Fairmint is not a crypto exchange where retail users simply buy tokenized stocks. The company provides infrastructure for ownership records and securities transfers and operates as a registered transfer agent in the United States.

That makes its warning more interesting than ordinary criticism of tokenization.

According to Fairmint, the problem is not necessarily that a stock exists on a blockchain. The problem emerges if the **distribution of tokenized securities develops faster than the infrastructure capable of answering one basic question unambiguously: who actually owns what?**

If the same Nvidia share exists as a traditional NVDA stock, a 1:1 backed token, another token on another blockchain, a Robinhood derivative, an SPV position and a perpetual on a derivatives market, all of those products may track roughly the same price.

But they are not the same thing.

Blockchain can remove paper.

It does not automatically remove **the recordkeeping problem**.

## Why do we use Nvidia in the examples?

Below, we use **Nvidia and the ticker NVDA only as a simple illustrative example**, because it is one of the best-known publicly traded companies in the world and it is easy for readers to imagine multiple products trying to track the same stock.

We could just as easily use **Tesla, Alphabet or Google, Apple, Amazon or another well-known public company**.

With private companies such as **SpaceX**, the situation becomes even more interesting because there is no conventional publicly traded SpaceX stock with one continuously quoted spot price on an exchange. Products offering SpaceX exposure may therefore include SPV structures, private-market positions, tokens or derivative and perpetual products.

So NVDA below is not a recommendation or analysis of Nvidia stock. It is simply an easy-to-understand example used to answer one question:

**If five products on a screen all carry the name of the same company, do you own the same thing in all five cases?**

The answer is — no.

## What can you actually buy?

The easiest way to separate these products is by looking at what actually sits behind the price the user sees on screen.

| What you are buying             | Examples of products/platforms                       | What you actually have                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Real stock**                  | traditional brokers                                  | A share through the traditional brokerage and custody system                                       |
| **1:1 backed tokenized stock**  | xStocks, Ondo Stocks                                 | A token backed by a corresponding underlying share or other defined collateral                     |
| **Stock token derivative**      | Robinhood Stock Tokens                               | A financial derivative providing economic exposure to the underlying stock                         |
| **SPV-based product**           | private-market platforms                             | A stake or claim against an SPV that holds the underlying asset                                    |
| **Pre-IPO exposure**            | Republic and other private-market platforms          | Economic exposure to a private company through an intermediary legal structure                     |
| **Perpetual / synthetic stock** | Hyperliquid HIP-3 markets and other derivatives DEXs | A contract tracking a reference price or value without requiring ownership of the underlying stock |

That is precisely why the term **“tokenized stock”** alone is no longer enough to tell an investor what they have actually bought.

## A token backed by a real stock

One tokenization model uses a real security as backing.

With a 1:1 backed token, the issuer or its custody structure holds the underlying stock, or an equivalent amount of the security, as backing for the tokens that have been issued.

In simplified form:

**user → NVDA token → issuer/custodian → real Nvidia share**

xStocks is one of the better-known examples of this approach, while Ondo Stocks uses its own structure for tokenized stocks and ETFs.

But even with a product that is fully backed by a real share, investors should not automatically conclude:

**“I own an Nvidia token, therefore I am an Nvidia shareholder.”**

That depends on the legal structure of the product.

An investor should check who formally owns the underlying security, what rights the tokenholder has, what happens to dividends, whether voting rights exist, whether the token can be redeemed for the underlying asset or only for cash and what happens if the issuer or custodian runs into trouble.

## A token linked to a real stock is not the same as a derivative or perpetual

This may be the most important distinction for an ordinary user.

On screen, the two products may look almost identical:

**NVDA – $180**

But one product may have a real Nvidia share as backing, while another may only track its price.

|                                                      | Token linked to a real stock                          | Derivative / perpetual                                      |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Is there a real stock backing the product?**       | Yes, if the product is 1:1 backed                     | Not necessarily                                             |
| **What does the user own?**                          | A token providing economic exposure to a backed stock | A contract tracking the price of the underlying asset       |
| **Is the user a direct shareholder of the company?** | Generally no                                          | No                                                          |
| **Leverage**                                         | Usually not an inherent part of the token             | Often available                                             |
| **Long / short**                                     | Depends on the platform                               | A core function of the product                              |
| **What keeps the price close to the underlying?**    | Backing, redemption and arbitrage                     | Reference price, oracle/index, market and funding           |
| **Main additional risk**                             | Issuer, custodian and legal rights                    | Funding, leverage, liquidation, oracle and market structure |

Assume Nvidia stock is worth **$180**.

With a 1:1 backed token, the issuer may hold the corresponding Nvidia share and issue a token that tracks its economic value.

With a perpetual, the situation is completely different.

The user opens:

**LONG NVDA perpetual**

or:

**SHORT NVDA perpetual.**

If the reference price rises by 10%, the value of the long position also rises, subject to funding, fees and any leverage being used.

But because of that position, nobody necessarily has to buy one real Nvidia share and hold it for that user.

A perpetual is therefore primarily used for **trading the price**, not for owning the underlying asset.

## Where does Hyperliquid fit into this?

Hyperliquid is especially interesting because of its **HIP-3 builder-deployed perpetual markets**.

This infrastructure allows the creation of perpetual markets whose reference asset does not have to be a conventional cryptocurrency such as Bitcoin or Ethereum. Markets can also appear that track stocks, indices, commodities or the estimated value of private companies.

With this type of product, the user is not buying the company.

They are buying a **perpetual contract**.

They can go long if they expect the price to rise or short if they expect it to fall.

That is a completely different legal and financial position from buying a real share or a 1:1 backed token.

## SpaceX shows the difference particularly well

With a private company such as SpaceX, the distinction is even easier to see.

If a derivatives platform displays a product called **SpaceX** with a price of, for example, $120 or $140, that number does not automatically mean:

**“One official SpaceX share on a public exchange now costs $140.”**

SpaceX is not in the same situation as Nvidia or Tesla, which have publicly traded shares with transparent market prices.

A SpaceX perpetual may represent **synthetic exposure or a derivative position tracking a defined reference value or an implied valuation of the company**.

An investor may make or lose money from changes in that price.

But that does not make them a SpaceX shareholder.

## And what is an SPV?

SPV stands for **Special Purpose Vehicle**, meaning a legal entity established for a specific purpose.

In private-market investing, an SPV may be created specifically to buy and hold shares in a particular company.

The structure may then look like this:

**investor → SPV → SpaceX shares**

The investor therefore does not necessarily have to be directly registered as the owner of SpaceX shares.

The formal underlying position is held by the SPV, while the investor has a particular right or claim against the SPV.

That is not unusual or automatically problematic. SPV structures have been used in traditional finance for a long time.

But investors need to understand **how many legal and operational layers exist between them and the actual asset**.

Who owns the underlying stock? What happens if the SPV fails? How is the investor treated in liquidation? Can the investor transfer their interest? What happens if the company goes public or is acquired?

The answers depend on the documentation of the specific product.

## A Robinhood Stock Token may look like a stock, but it is not the same thing

Robinhood is another example of why the user interface and the company name alone are not enough.

A stock token may provide the user with economic exposure to the price of a particular security, but legally it does not necessarily represent the same ownership an investor receives when buying the underlying share through a traditional brokerage system.

On one screen, we could therefore have two products carrying the name **Nvidia** and showing an almost identical price.

One represents a stock.

The other represents a contract linked to the price of that stock.

To the user, they look very similar.

Legally, they are completely different.

## One Nvidia, six different products

Imagine a market where all of the following exist at the same time:

**NVDA on Nasdaq**

**NVDAx as a 1:1 backed token**

**another NVDA token on another blockchain**

**Robinhood NVDA Stock Token**

**NVDA perpetual on Hyperliquid infrastructure**

**NVDA synthetic product on another DeFi protocol**

All six could display almost the same price.

But behind them may sit:

different owners of the underlying shares,

different custodians,

different investor rights,

different blockchains,

different legal contracts,

different oracle systems,

and different records of who actually owns what.

That is the core of the warning about a possible new **“paperwork crisis.”**

The problem would no longer be physical paper.

The problem would be **digital fragmentation**.

## Blockchain can remove paper, but not necessarily the chaos

The irony is obvious.

Blockchain emerged as a technology that was supposed to enable more transparent, programmable and simpler records of assets.

But if every issuer, blockchain, broker, SPV and derivatives protocol creates its own representation of the same asset, we could end up with the opposite result.

Instead of one clear record, we could have:

**one company → ten products → five blockchains → several custodians → multiple legal structures → different investor rights.**

In other words, Wall Street had a problem in 1968 because it had to reconcile too much paper.

The Wall Street of the future could face a problem because it has to reconcile **too many digital versions of the same thing**.

## What should an investor check before buying?

Before buying a product carrying the name of a well-known company, it is not enough to look at the ticker and price.

At minimum, investors should know the answers to several questions:

* **Is there a real underlying stock?**
* **Who holds it?**
* **Is the product 1:1 backed?**
* **Do I own the security, or do I only have economic exposure?**
* **Is the product a token, derivative, perpetual or an interest in an SPV?**
* **Am I entitled to dividends?**
* **Do I have voting rights?**
* **Can I convert the token into the underlying stock, or only into cash?**
* **What happens if the issuer, custodian or SPV fails?**
* **Which record represents the final proof of my legal claim?**

That last question could become the most important of all.

## Our view

* Wall Street’s **“paperwork crisis”** is not just an interesting historical anecdote. In 1968, the situation became so serious that exchanges shortened trading hours and closed one day per week so brokers could process the documentation.
* During some periods, the NYSE had to close the market for as many as two days in a single week so the system could catch up with the backlog of transactions.
* Tokenization can remove physical paper, accelerate settlement and open market access to a wider group of investors.
* But the company name and ticker are no longer enough to tell a user what they have actually bought.
* **A 1:1 backed token and a perpetual tracking the same stock are not the same financial asset.**
* With the first, defined underlying backing exists in the background. With the second, no real stock necessarily exists for that particular user.
* An SPV introduces another layer: the investor may have a claim against a special-purpose entity that then holds the underlying shares.
* Hyperliquid’s HIP-3 shows how quickly the boundary between crypto derivatives markets and traditional financial assets is disappearing.
* With private companies such as SpaceX, the distinction is especially important because the price of a derivative or perpetual is not the same thing as the official price of a publicly traded stock.
* Fairmint’s warning is therefore not an argument against tokenization. It is a warning that distribution without clear and interoperable ownership records could create a new form of market fragmentation.
* Wall Street’s crisis in the 1960s was caused by **too much paper and infrastructure that could not process it quickly enough**. The next problem could be caused by **too many digital representations of the same asset and too little clarity about what each one actually represents**.
