# Autonomous AI agents allegedly attacked Taiwan government systems

Cyberattacks involving artificial intelligence are no longer just about whether AI can write a phishing message, find a vulnerability or help a hacker create malware. A new case from Asia suggests something significantly more serious: **multiple AI agents allegedly carried out much of a multi-day operation against government systems and critical infrastructure almost autonomously**, analyzing the results of their own attempts and changing tactics when they encountered obstacles.

The Financial Times reported on August 12 that the target of the attack was Taiwan. Israeli cybersecurity company Dream, which uncovered traces of the operation, did not publicly identify the country that was attacked, saying only that government institutions in Asia were involved and that a state in the Asia-Pacific region had been informed about the incident. According to the published findings, the attack system was capable of using up to **eight autonomous AI agents simultaneously**, mapping government systems, investigating vulnerabilities and adapting subsequent steps based on the results of previous attempts over a four-day period.

## What did the attack look like?

Dream says the operation took place in early July and consisted of **12 waves of attacks over four days**. During that period, 85 user accounts were compromised, and attackers managed to move deeper into internal systems through 84 of them. More than **2,500 employee records** were extracted, together with internal database credentials and information about network architecture.

Following the initial breaches, the operation expanded beyond its original targets. According to Dream, the attackers attempted to access government IT suppliers, a government email system, an agency connected to nuclear security and at least seven energy companies. The incident therefore developed from the compromise of individual user accounts into a broader operation targeting government and critical infrastructure.

## Eight AI agents operated like a cyber team

According to the Financial Times, the attack system could use up to eight autonomous agents simultaneously and mapped **21 government systems**. What makes the attack unusual is not simply the use of AI, because automated tools for scanning networks, testing passwords and searching for known vulnerabilities have existed for decades. The key difference is that, according to researchers, the agents were able to assess the results of previous attempts, rank possible attack paths and change priorities.

When one route into a system was blocked, an agent could search for additional information and attempt to develop an alternative approach. In other words, AI was not simply being used as a tool that a hacker asked individual questions before manually deciding every next step. **Multiple agents could perform tasks in parallel that would normally require a coordinated human cyber team**, reducing the amount of direct human work required to conduct a complex operation.

## What does an “autonomous” cyberattack actually mean?

Autonomous in this context does not mean that artificial intelligence independently decided to attack a country. A human actor still stands behind the operation, choosing the target, setting up the infrastructure, providing access to tools and launching the system. The difference comes afterward, when an agentic AI system can receive an objective, use different tools, analyze the result of its own attempts and decide what to try next without requiring a human to manually issue every individual command.

Dream says that it was precisely this **autonomous execution**, rather than an entirely new hacking technique, that distinguished the operation from previous attacks. Amir Becker of Dream, who previously led cyber operations in Israel’s Unit 8200 intelligence division, said he had never before seen this type of “end-to-end” autonomous attack against a government target. That is not the same as proving it was the first such attack in history, however, because similar operations may have occurred previously without being detected or publicly disclosed.

## Publicly available AI tools were used

Perhaps the most interesting part of the story is that, according to the information published so far, the attackers did not need some secret government AI technology. Researchers discovered an online archive of around **160 MB containing 1,395 files** connected to the operation, and according to the analysis reported by the Financial Times, the attack system used two publicly available AI agent frameworks – **Hermes and OpenClaw**. Researchers were unable to determine which AI model powered the agents themselves.

The data also suggested that the model’s safety restrictions were bypassed by presenting the activity as legitimate and authorized security testing. This creates an additional problem for AI model providers: the system does not necessarily need to receive an explicit command to “attack government infrastructure” if the same malicious activity can be presented as an authorized security task, penetration test or vulnerability assessment.

## Was China behind the attack?

Particular caution is needed here because Dream has not publicly attributed the operation to a specific hacker group or to the Chinese government.

Researchers discovered communications linked to the attack infrastructure written in simplified Chinese, leading them to believe there is a high probability that the operators were connected to China. Data taken from the compromised systems, by contrast, was written in traditional Chinese, which is used in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau. Such indicators can assist attribution, but **they do not prove that the attack was ordered or carried out by the Chinese government**, and Dream has so far not named a specific group behind the operation.

## Why is this case so important?

The biggest change may not be that AI can find a vulnerability or write an exploit, but rather the **speed, parallelization and cost of an attack**. A human hacking team has to investigate a system, analyze the results, try one method, determine why it failed and then decide what to do next. An AI agent can repeat that cycle almost continuously, without breaks and without requiring a human to manually direct every step.

If eight, ten or several dozen agents are operating at the same time, the defensive team is no longer facing simply a more capable hacker. It is facing a system that can investigate a large number of potential weaknesses in parallel while the humans on the other side still have to review alerts, make decisions, contact other departments and follow security procedures. Dream describes precisely this growing imbalance between **an attack approaching machine speed and a defense that still operates at the speed of people and institutions**.

## From AI assistant to AI operator

Over the past several years, much of the discussion around AI and cybercrime has focused on how generative models can assist attackers. AI can write more convincing phishing emails, explain code, analyze malware, translate messages, find information about potential targets or help hackers understand vulnerabilities more quickly. In all of these examples, however, a human remains the main operator who asks questions and decides what happens next.

Agentic systems represent the next step because an agent can receive an objective, use tools, analyze the results of its own work and then continue to another step without a human manually issuing every subsequent instruction. Multiple agents can divide different tasks among themselves at the same time, gradually turning AI from an assistant into something much closer to an **autonomous cyber operator**. Humans have not disappeared from the process, but the amount of human work required to carry out a sophisticated operation could decline dramatically.

## Our take

* **The biggest change is not that AI can help a hacker.** We already knew that. The change is the possibility that AI can independently carry out large parts of an attack, analyze the results and decide what to try next.
* **If eight agents can investigate dozens of government systems in parallel, the number of highly skilled human hackers is no longer the same limitation it once was.** A single human team could potentially operate an ever-growing number of digital “operators” simultaneously.
* **It is particularly concerning that publicly available tools were reportedly used.** This type of operation may no longer require exclusive technology available only to the world’s largest intelligence agencies.
* **The word “autonomous” still needs to be used precisely.** AI did not independently choose Taiwan as a target or decide on its own to launch a cyberattack. A human or organization still defines the objective and sets up the system.
* **Claims that this was the first attack of its kind should also be treated cautiously.** A Dream expert says he had never before seen an end-to-end autonomous attack against a government target, but that does not prove a similar operation had not already happened and remained undetected.
* **Attribution to China has not been proven either.** There are technical and linguistic indicators suggesting a connection between the operators and China, but Dream has not attributed the operation to a specific group or to the Chinese government.
* **The biggest question is still ahead:** what happens when an attacker no longer has eight AI agents, but eighty – with each one able to work 24 hours a day, search for weaknesses in parallel and change strategy without waiting for a human?
* If the findings are accurate, this may not be just the story of one unusual cyberattack against Taiwan. **It could be an early demonstration of what the next generation of cyber warfare will look like.**
