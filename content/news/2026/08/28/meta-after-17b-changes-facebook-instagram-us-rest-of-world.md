# If the algorithm is dangerous enough that Meta has to change it in the US, why does the same system remain elsewhere?

Meta agreed to a settlement of up to **$17 billion** after just over a week of trial over allegations by US states that Facebook and Instagram were designed in ways that encourage addictive behavior among children and teenagers. Even that enormous amount comes with relatively favorable terms for Meta: payments are spread over as many as ten years, while several billion dollars are tied to additional conditions, meaning the guaranteed amount could remain at approximately **$12 billion**. Meta did not admit wrongdoing as part of the settlement.

Even more interesting is the moment when Meta agreed to settle. Current and former people from the company had already appeared before the jury, including former engineering director and safety expert **Arturo Béjar**, former data scientist **George Volichenko**, Instagram Director of Product Design **Francesco Fogu** and Instagram head **Adam Mosseri**, while recorded testimony from former UX researchers Elena Davis and Natalie Troxel had also been shown. Mark Zuckerberg was due to testify later in the trial, but the settlement ended the proceedings before he appeared before the jury.

## What had Meta's own people already told the court?

Arturo Béjar did not speak only as a former Meta employee, but also as a father. He said that when his daughter was 14, she began receiving **sexual messages and requests for intimate photos from strangers on Instagram**. He later testified about harassment as well and argued that Meta knew how frequently teenagers experienced negative incidents, but that safety did not receive the same priority as growth and engagement.

George Volichenko, who worked on Instagram's mental wellbeing team, said he had been told that the team existed primarily to protect the company from future lawsuits. His team wanted the **Take a Break** feature to be automatically enabled for younger teenagers, but the proposal was not implemented. According to his testimony, the reason was its negative impact on key business metrics – how long and how often people used Instagram.

Prosecutors showed Francesco Fogu an even more uncomfortable internal document. It stated that companies sometimes **do not comply with a regulatory request and instead accept the penalty**, while in other cases they comply only minimally or literally. Fogu said he could not remember whether he had written those words himself.

**Is it simply cheaper for a large technology company to pay a regulatory fine than to make changes that would reduce the time users spend on the platform, engagement and therefore profit?**

Adam Mosseri defended Meta's safety measures, but the court also discussed how few users actually used some of those protections before they became default settings. The trial was expected to continue for several more weeks, Zuckerberg had yet to testify – and then Meta agreed to settle.

## $17 billion sounds enormous – until you look at the terms

Meta does not have to pay $17 billion all at once. The agreement runs for ten years, part of the maximum amount depends on additional conditions, and the guaranteed amount could remain at approximately **$12 billion**. For a company that generated more than $60 billion in net profit in 2025, the annual burden looks considerably less dramatic than the headline figure of $17 billion.

In return, Meta avoided the continuation of a trial in which internal documents and testimony from senior company figures could have continued to emerge publicly for weeks. At the same time, it agreed to a series of protections for US teenagers: time limits, nighttime restrictions, fewer notifications during the school day, hidden like counts, restrictions on certain filters, stronger age verification and additional parental controls.

## The rest of the world is now asking: why not here too?

The US settlement triggered reactions outside the United States almost immediately. **Australian Communications Minister Anika Wells** stressed that social networks clearly have tools that can protect young people from addictive features. **South Korea's media regulator KMCC** went even further, saying improvements involving algorithmic recommendations, like displays and push notifications should apply to young users **around the world**, not only in places where regulators are powerful enough to force them.

**UK minister Pat McFadden** said he did not want a situation in which children in the US received a higher level of protection than children in Britain, and specifically highlighted endless scrolling as a highly addictive feature for young people. In the **Philippines**, minister Henry Aguda said after talks with the company that Meta had agreed to strengthen age verification, parental controls, removal of harmful content and cooperation with authorities. **Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum** said the government needed to consider what the US settlement meant for Mexico, while the **European Commission** expects concrete changes for European users as well.

If a particular feature can automatically be restricted for an American teenager, it can technically be restricted for a teenager in Britain, Croatia, South Korea or Kenya. It is becoming increasingly difficult to argue that the problem is technical. Protection increasingly appears to depend on the strength of the regulator.

## Infinite scroll remains – even though it is part of the problem

One very important feature was **not banned by the US settlement: infinite scrolling remains**. Nor were the fundamental algorithmic mechanisms removed that Facebook and Instagram use to find the next piece of content likely to keep a user on the screen.

Infinite scroll removes the natural point at which a user would otherwise stop. There is no end of the page and no conscious decision about whether to open something new – just another swipe, another video, another photo and another recommendation. Add an algorithm that continuously learns what keeps a person engaged for longest, and the result is a system whose basic business interest includes extending the time spent on the platform.

The European Commission has therefore specifically identified infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and highly personalized recommendations as elements of the **addictive design** of Facebook and Instagram. The US settlement may protect younger users in some areas, but it does not remove one of the fundamental mechanisms capable of keeping them scrolling.

## In other countries, much more may be at stake

The Guardian points to the case of Abrham Meareg, whose father, a chemistry professor in Ethiopia, was killed in 2021 during the country's civil war. Meareg alleges that before his father's killing, Facebook posts circulated containing his photograph, address and calls for him to be killed, and that Facebook's algorithm further distributed that content. The family says it had asked Facebook to remove the material. The case against Meta is being pursued in Kenya. A court has not yet finally determined Meta's responsibility for those allegations.

The case shows why the debate about Meta's algorithms is not simply a debate about whether a child spends two or five hours on Instagram. A system that optimizes engagement can increase the visibility of any content it predicts will provoke a strong reaction – from an entertainment video to political accusations, hate speech or calls for violence.

At the same time, other proceedings and legal disputes against Meta continue across Europe, Africa and the US. The American settlement could therefore become a powerful argument for other governments: if the product can be changed for US users, what prevents the same company from introducing those changes globally?

## And then there are cases of face recording during account recovery

There is another sensitive issue. Meta uses **video selfies, or face recordings**, in certain age-verification and identity-confirmation procedures, including restoring access to blocked or compromised accounts. There are cases in which an automated recovery process offers the user a face recording as a way to continue, without any prior conversation with a human support representative. **Even after the face recording, contact with human support does not necessarily follow – the process can remain entirely automated and end with the account being permanently disabled.**

This becomes particularly sensitive if Meta does not actually know the real age of the person behind the account at the time it is blocked. The company already confirms the use of facial age estimation for teenagers, yet a user can enter an account-recovery process for a completely different reason before the system reliably knows whether the person is an adult or a child.

Adult users, and especially children, are often not in a position to realistically assess all the possible consequences of providing a high-quality video recording of their own face. It is particularly important to know what happens if the original recording, copies of it or **data derived from facial analysis** are retained in any form. With biometric data, the consequences of potential unauthorized access can be more persistent than with an ordinary password or another piece of information that can simply be changed.

It is therefore not enough merely to ask whether a user clicked “Continue.” What matters is whether the user understands what is being recorded, what data can be created from it, how long those data are retained, who processes them and whether there is a genuinely accessible alternative that does not require recording the face. For children, the question of understanding and meaningful consent is even more serious.

## Global profits, but local protection?

Facebook and Instagram are global products. Their algorithms, advertising and business models operate around the world, yet user protections increasingly depend on how powerful the country in which a user lives is.

The US obtained changes after a major court case. The EU is threatening penalties under the DSA. Australia, Britain and South Korea are demanding stronger measures. The Philippines has already begun seeking additional protections.

But what happens to a user in a country that does not have the regulatory power of the United States or the European Union?

Meta demonstrated one important fact through the US settlement: **changes are possible**. The company can alter default settings, restrict certain features, silence notifications, change how recommendation systems operate and strengthen age controls. After that, the argument that something is technically impossible becomes considerably weaker.

## Our view

* **The US settlement of up to $17 billion looks enormous, but Meta has ten years to make the payments and part of the maximum amount depends on additional conditions. The guaranteed amount could remain at approximately $12 billion.**
* **The timing of the settlement is highly significant: Arturo Béjar, George Volichenko, Francesco Fogu and Adam Mosseri had already testified, former researchers had provided recorded testimony, and Mark Zuckerberg had yet to appear before the jury. The settlement is not proof of wrongdoing, but it stopped further public testimony and presentation of internal documents.**
* **Particularly uncomfortable is the fact that the court heard both about a safety feature that allegedly was not automatically enabled because of its impact on engagement metrics and about an internal document suggesting a company may sometimes choose not to comply with a regulatory demand and simply accept the penalty.**
* **The US settlement did not abolish infinite scroll or the core engagement algorithm. Those are precisely the kinds of features the EU is directly challenging as part of addictive design.**
* **Australia, Britain, South Korea, the Philippines, Mexico and the EU are already raising the same question: why should US users receive stronger protections than users elsewhere?**
* **Face recording raises additional questions about user rights and informed consent. Account-recovery procedures for blocked accounts can involve video selfies, while Meta may not necessarily know with certainty at that point whether the person behind the account is an adult or a minor.**
* **Neither adults nor especially children necessarily understand what data can be created through analysis of a face recording or what consequences could follow if original recordings, copies or derived data are retained or compromised.**
* **Facebook and Instagram generate global revenues. It is difficult to justify a model in which the risks are also global, but protection depends on whether an individual country can threaten a sufficiently large lawsuit or fine.**
