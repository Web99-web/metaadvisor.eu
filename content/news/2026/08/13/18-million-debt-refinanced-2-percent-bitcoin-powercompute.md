# $18M debt refinanced at only 2% — how did PowerCompute do it?

How can a company refinance more than **$18 million of debt at an initial interest rate of only 2% per year** while using Bitcoin as collateral? That is exactly what PowerCompute has done, using a new credit facility from Arch Lending to replace several existing loans.

Instead of selling Bitcoin to pay down its debt, the Nasdaq-listed company pledged **307 BTC** and received a new facility worth just over **$18.1 million**. The refinancing replaced three existing loans, including $11 million owed to Galaxy Digital and two other loans totaling $7 million that carried interest rates of 12%.

At first glance, the new financing looks extraordinarily cheap. But there is a catch.

## Why is the interest rate only 2%?

This is not a standard Bitcoin-backed loan. PowerCompute entered into what is known as a **collar loan**, a financial structure that sets both a lower and an upper Bitcoin price level for a defined period.

For the initial 30-day period, Bitcoin’s reference price was **$63,950**, with a floor at $58,860 and a ceiling at $66,370. PowerCompute gets a very low cash interest rate and some protection against a sharp downside move, but in return it accepts a limit on part of its potential gains if Bitcoin rises strongly.

In other words, **2% is not the full economic cost of the financing**. Part of the price is paid through the lender’s participation in value above the agreed upper level.

## What happens if Bitcoin rises above $66,370?

If BTC is above the agreed **$66,370 ceiling** on the settlement date, PowerCompute does not keep all of the additional value created by the pledged Bitcoin. Value above that level is included in the settlement in favor of the lender and can be settled in Bitcoin, US dollars or stablecoins.

That means this is not the same as borrowing $18 million at a simple fixed 2% interest rate. PowerCompute gets an unusually low nominal rate, but at the same time limits some of its short-term Bitcoin upside.

That is the central catch: **PowerCompute pays less interest because part of the Bitcoin risk and potential return is shared with the lender.**

## What happens if Bitcoin falls sharply?

With a traditional crypto-backed loan, a major decline in the value of the collateral can quickly trigger a margin call, a demand for additional Bitcoin or a forced liquidation. PowerCompute’s initial 30-day structure works differently and does not use a standard automatic liquidation simply because Bitcoin’s price falls.

The floor for the first period was set at **$58,860**. If Bitcoin finishes the period below that level, the agreement gives PowerCompute several options, including repaying the loan and recovering the BTC, adjusting the position for a new cycle or, under the terms of the contract, surrendering the pledged Bitcoin to satisfy the obligation.

That gives the arrangement a different risk profile from a standard loan where a sudden market decline can automatically trigger liquidation.

## The 2% rate is not guaranteed for years

Another important detail is that **the 2% rate applies only to the initial 30-day period**. The facility can roll into additional 30-day cycles, but the interest rate, floor and ceiling can all be reset at each rollover based on prevailing market conditions.

So this is not a traditional multi-year $18 million loan with a permanently fixed 2% rate. The initial terms are unusually attractive, but they can change from month to month.

For PowerCompute, however, the main advantage is clear: the company can refinance its business obligations **without selling its Bitcoin**.

## Why not simply sell the Bitcoin?

PowerCompute wants to retain its exposure to BTC. At the end of June, the company reported holding more than 318 Bitcoin in its treasury, and it has now used 307 BTC as collateral for the new facility.

Instead of following the model of “sell Bitcoin and repay debt,” the company is using a **“keep Bitcoin, pledge it and borrow dollars”** strategy. If Bitcoin rises over the longer term, PowerCompute still wants exposure to that value, even though the collar limits some of the potential short-term upside.

That makes the story interesting beyond PowerCompute itself. It shows Bitcoin increasingly moving from the world of speculative investment into traditional corporate finance, where companies can use BTC as collateral for real multi-million-dollar business debt.

## Our take

* **A 2% interest rate sounds spectacular, but it is not the full price of the financing.** In exchange, PowerCompute gives up part of the potential gain if Bitcoin moves above the agreed upper level.
* **A major advantage is the different treatment of downside risk.** During the initial period, an ordinary drop in Bitcoin’s price does not automatically create the standard margin-call and forced-liquidation scenario.
* **It is important that the 2% rate is not guaranteed long term.** The terms can be reset with each new 30-day period.
* **Perhaps the most interesting part is not the interest rate itself, but the fact that a Nasdaq-listed company is using Bitcoin as collateral to refinance conventional business debt.**
* If structures like this become more common, companies holding Bitcoin may increasingly be able to access dollar liquidity without selling their BTC. Bitcoin could therefore become more widely used as **corporate collateral**.

PowerCompute’s facility is not just a story about refinancing $18 million at 2%. It is also an example of the financial infrastructure developing around Bitcoin and increasingly beginning to resemble traditional banking — only with a very different type of collateral.
