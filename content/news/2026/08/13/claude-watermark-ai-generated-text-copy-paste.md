# Claude will watermark AI-generated text — and the mark survives copy-paste

You copy text from Claude, paste it into Word, a CMS, an email or a social media post, and at first glance nothing has changed. Yet the text could still contain an **imperceptible machine-readable mark indicating that the content was generated by AI**. Anthropic has announced plans to mark content generated by Claude as part of its compliance with new European transparency requirements for AI-generated content.

For text, this does not mean a visible label such as “Generated by Claude” appearing beneath every response. Instead, the idea is to embed the mark in a way that remains invisible during normal reading but can be detected by an appropriate system. What makes the approach particularly interesting is that the mark is intended to survive ordinary **copy-paste**, meaning that simply moving Claude-generated text from one application to another will not necessarily remove information about its AI origin.

## Claude will not simply add a visible label at the end of the text

Watermarking text is a very different problem from watermarking an image. With a photograph, it is easy to imagine a visible logo in the corner or information stored inside the file itself. Text, however, is constantly copied between different systems, often without any of the original metadata, meaning that a mark attached only to a document or webpage can disappear very easily.

Anthropic therefore describes an approach in which the marking must be connected to the generated content itself rather than only to the place where that content first appeared. The goal is to make machine detection of AI-generated text possible even after a user copies a Claude response and pastes it somewhere else. At the same time, the company acknowledges that no method of text watermarking is perfect and says it will publish more detailed technical documentation and information about detection tools as the system develops.

## The EU AI Act is behind the change

Anthropic has signed the European **Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content**, which is intended to help providers of generative AI systems comply with obligations under Article 50 of the EU AI Act. The new transparency requirements began applying on August 2, 2026, and require providers of generative AI systems to make content generated or manipulated by their systems identifiable as artificially generated through effective and, where technically feasible, reliable machine-readable markings.

That does not mean every AI-generated email or private note will suddenly display a large visible warning. The rules distinguish between **technical marking implemented by the provider of the AI system** and situations in which a person or organization publishing content must explicitly disclose its AI origin to the public.

## What does it actually mean that the mark survives copy-paste?

Consider the simplest example. A user asks Claude to write an article, receives 1,500 words and copies the entire response into WordPress. Visually, the finished article may no longer have any connection to the Claude interface, but the fact that the text was moved through copy-paste does not necessarily mean that the signal identifying it as Claude output has disappeared.

That is the key difference between this approach and ordinary metadata. If the information exists only inside the original document, it can easily be lost when plain text is copied. If the mark is associated with the structure of the generated text itself, it can potentially travel together with the content.

A machine detector could then analyze the text after it has been published somewhere else and potentially identify a signal indicating that it originated as AI-generated output. The reader, meanwhile, may see nothing unusual at all.

## The watermark is not indestructible

The phrase “the mark survives copy-paste” does not mean the watermark will be impossible to remove. Text is extremely easy to modify: sentences can be rewritten, paragraphs replaced, words changed, content translated into another language or combined with text from other authors. The greater the amount of editing, the harder it becomes to expect the original signal to remain equally strong.

This is one of the fundamental limitations of text watermarking. If a user simply copies a Claude response, the mark may survive. If a human takes the same text, rewrites half of it, completely restructures the article, adds original sections or runs it through another AI model, the situation becomes far more complex.

The opposite side of the problem is equally important: **if a detector does not find a watermark, that alone does not prove that the text was written by a human**. The mark may have been weakened or lost during editing, or the content may have been created using a different model or a different marking system.

## What if AI is not the author, but a collaborator?

This is where the story becomes much more interesting than simply catching students who copy complete AI-generated essays. The easiest case for watermarking is when someone tells Claude, “write the entire text for me,” copies the result without meaningful changes and presents it as their own work. In that situation, there is a relatively clear relationship between the AI output and the final document.

Professional use of AI often looks completely different. An author may carry out the research, choose the topic, collect sources and formulate conclusions, then use AI only to help structure the article. A journalist may write the first version and ask Claude to make it shorter or clearer. Someone else may use AI only to translate their own original article, while another user may begin with an AI draft and then rewrite almost every paragraph.

In all of these cases, artificial intelligence has participated in the writing process, but that does not automatically make AI the **author of the entire finished piece**. A watermark may indicate that a particular passage was produced by a model at some point, but it is much less capable of answering how much of the final article reflects human research, judgment and editorial work.

## The EU AI Act already recognizes the difference between AI text and editorial responsibility

This distinction is not merely philosophical. The EU AI Act specifically addresses situations in which AI-generated or manipulated text is published to inform the public about matters of public interest.

Such content may, under certain circumstances, need to be clearly disclosed as AI-generated or manipulated. However, the rules include an important exception: when text has undergone **genuine human review or editorial control** and a natural or legal person assumes editorial responsibility for the publication, the obligation to publicly label it as AI-generated does not apply in the same way.

The European Commission also distinguishes meaningful editorial oversight from simple proofreading. Correcting spelling or grammar alone is not sufficient. Human review means genuinely examining the content, sources and reliability of the information and giving an editor the ability to approve, change or reject the text.

That distinction matters because it shows that the mere involvement of AI in a workflow does not automatically mean that the finished text is treated as fully AI-generated content with no human responsibility.

## What does this mean for media, websites and professional writing?

If text watermarking becomes a standard among major AI companies, a large share of the content that passes through AI tools every day could eventually contain some form of machine-readable signal. This goes far beyond articles. AI is increasingly used for emails, reports, marketing copy, product descriptions, social media posts, translations, business documents and internal communication.

That could create a new problem for tools that try to answer the simple question: “Was this written by AI?” A much more useful question will be **how AI was used in producing the text**.

There is a major difference between content generated from the first word to the last by a model without meaningful human review and an article in which an author conducted the research, selected the sources, developed the argument, checked the claims and assumed editorial responsibility while using AI only for structure, wording or translation.

A watermark on its own cannot necessarily explain that difference.

## Will other AI models have to do the same?

The EU AI Act was not written only for Claude. The obligation to make AI-generated content machine-readable applies to providers of generative AI systems that fall under Article 50, meaning the issue also affects other major models available on the European market.

That does not mean every company will use the same technical approach as Anthropic. One provider may develop one form of watermarking, another may use a different technology, and future standards could combine several methods for proving the provenance of content.

Claude adopting a particular method therefore does not automatically mean that ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok or other models will carry an identical watermark. What they share is regulatory pressure to make AI-generated content **machine-readable and identifiable**, rather than allowing the internet to become increasingly filled with synthetic content whose origin cannot be determined.

## Our take

* **A watermark makes clear sense when someone simply copies a fully AI-generated text and presents it as their own.** In that situation, a technical signal could help establish where the content originated.
* **The much bigger problem begins with legitimate professional use of AI.** If an author conducts the research, selects the sources, draws the conclusions and seriously edits the final text, the mere presence of an AI watermark does not tell us how much of the content was actually created by the human and how much by the model.
* **Translations will be an especially interesting case.** AI may generate every single word of the English or German version of an article even though the original text, research, ideas and conclusions were entirely created by a human author.
* **The EU AI Act makes an important distinction here.** Text informing the public about matters of public interest may be exempt from a public AI disclosure requirement when it has undergone genuine human or editorial review and a person or organization assumes editorial responsibility.
* **A watermark is not the same as infallible proof of authorship.** Its presence may show that a particular output was generated with AI, but it does not necessarily prove that AI authored the entire final work. Its absence also does not prove that AI was not used.
* **If systems like this become an industry standard, a large part of the internet could eventually carry different machine-readable traces of AI models.** That could include articles, business emails, marketing materials, translations and social media posts.
* **The most important question may therefore not be whether we can detect that AI participated somewhere in the writing process.** The more important question will be whether we can distinguish between text that AI wrote instead of a human and text that a human researched, conceived and created with AI assistance.
* Claude’s watermark could be an important step toward more transparent AI-generated content. At the same time, it could open a much broader debate over **what the label “AI-generated” even means in a world where humans and AI increasingly work together.**
