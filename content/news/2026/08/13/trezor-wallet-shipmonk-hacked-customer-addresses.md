# Bought a Trezor wallet? Hackers may now know where you live

Trezor hardware wallets were not hacked, private keys were not leaked and there is currently no evidence that users lost cryptocurrency as a result of this incident. But something happened that may be almost as disturbing for people holding larger amounts of crypto: attackers gained access to personal data belonging to people who bought a Trezor wallet – including their delivery addresses.

A total of **13,689 customers** were affected. For 11,742 users, the exposed data included their name, email address, phone number and full delivery address, while another 1,947 users had their name, city and email address exposed. In other words, someone may now have a list of people known to own a hardware wallet, together with information about where some of them live.

## The Trezor wallet was not hacked – the shipping partner was

The incident did not result from a breach of Trezor's hardware wallets or systems used to protect private keys. The compromised company was **ShipMonk**, an external partner used by Trezor for warehousing, logistics and device delivery.

ShipMonk notified Trezor on August 10 that an unauthorized party had accessed systems containing customer information. The affected orders were shipped between May 10 and August 8, 2026, to customers in the United States, United Kingdom, Sweden, Colombia, Brazil, Italy and Portugal.

Trezor says its own systems were not compromised and that hardware wallets, private keys and wallet backup data remained secure. According to the company, customers who did not receive a notification from Trezor about the incident were not affected.

## A hacker may now know you own crypto – and where you live

If the customer database of an ordinary online store is leaked, a criminal might learn that someone bought shoes, a television or a smartphone. If a database of hardware wallet buyers is compromised, that information has an entirely different value.

Buying a Trezor wallet may suggest that someone owns cryptocurrency and wants to keep it outside an exchange or conventional software wallet. The leaked database can therefore potentially give an attacker several highly valuable pieces of information at once: a person's identity, contact details and the address where the hardware wallet was delivered.

That creates opportunities for much more convincing phishing attacks, fake messages from supposed Trezor support, phone scams, letters sent to a home address and attempts to steal a seed phrase – as well as much more serious physical threats.

## Seed phrases and private keys were not leaked

It is important to emphasize what did not happen. There is no indication that attackers gained access to users' seed phrases, private keys or the Trezor devices themselves.

If a user operates a hardware wallet correctly and never reveals the recovery seed, this breach alone does not allow an attacker to simply take Bitcoin or other cryptocurrencies from the wallet. That is exactly why users should now be especially cautious about messages that appear to come from Trezor.

An attacker who knows your name, email address, phone number, home address and the fact that you bought a Trezor wallet can create a phishing message that looks far more convincing than random crypto spam.

## The most dangerous message could look completely legitimate

A user could, for example, receive a message claiming that their Trezor wallet was affected by a security incident and must now be "verified," that new firmware needs to be installed or that the recovery seed must be confirmed. Such a message may now include the user's real name and other personal details, making it appear like a legitimate security notice.

The basic rule remains unchanged: a recovery seed should never be entered on a website, sent by email or given to someone claiming to be customer support. Anyone who obtains the seed phrase can potentially gain control over the funds in the wallet.

Trezor warned affected users that the leaked information could make them targets of more sophisticated phishing attempts.

## The problem is no longer only digital

The incident raises another uncomfortable issue that is becoming increasingly important in the crypto world – the physical security of cryptocurrency owners. A hardware wallet can keep a private key offline, but it cannot protect its owner if a criminal knows their identity, home address and assumes that a significant amount of digital assets may be stored there.

The Financial Times reports that crypto owners are increasingly becoming targets of physical attacks, including home invasions and kidnappings. A leaked home address belonging to a hardware wallet owner is therefore much more serious than an ordinary marketing email database leak.

Trezor said it is not currently aware of any confirmed fraud, hacking or physical threats directly linked to this incident.

## Trezor is preparing more anonymous delivery

One of the more interesting consequences of the incident may be a change in how hardware wallets are sold and delivered. Trezor is working on a more anonymous delivery option that should allow customers to order devices without linking the purchase to their home address or real identity.

According to information the company provided to the Financial Times, it wants to begin introducing such an option in the European Union as early as September.

Trezor also says the scale of the incident was limited by a rule requiring its fulfillment partners to delete or anonymize order data after 90 days. Older orders were therefore not part of the database accessed by the attacker.

## Our take

* **This was not a hack of the Trezor wallet.** According to currently available information, private keys, recovery seeds and the devices themselves were not compromised.
* **The incident is still serious because data linking real people to hardware wallet purchases was exposed.** Full delivery addresses were included for 11,742 users.
* **This kind of information can make phishing significantly more convincing.** A message or phone call containing your real name, phone number and address can more easily appear to be legitimate communication from Trezor.
* **A hardware wallet protects the private key, but it cannot protect the owner from a physical threat.** If an attacker knows who owns a hardware wallet and where that person lives, the security problem is no longer only digital.
* **The incident shows that cold-wallet security does not end with the device itself.** The online store, logistics partner, delivery service, customer database, computer and purchasing process are all part of the security chain.
* **Trezor's policy of deleting data after 90 days turned out to be extremely important.** If order records had been retained for years, the number of people exposed in this incident could have been far higher.
* **More anonymous hardware wallet delivery is therefore not merely a privacy feature.** It can become a concrete security measure by preventing the creation of databases linking identity, home address and ownership of a crypto wallet.

You can own one of the most secure hardware wallets in the world and still discover that your biggest security problem is the address printed on the box it arrived in. That is why this Trezor incident is not a story about stolen Bitcoin, but about how risk can move away from the wallet itself and into the infrastructure surrounding it.
