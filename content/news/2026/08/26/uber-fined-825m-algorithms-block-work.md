# When the algorithm decides you can no longer work

Uber was not fined over stolen data, a ransomware attack or leaked passwords. The nearly **€825 million** penalty concerns something that may be even more sensitive for many platforms: **automated decisions about people**.

The Dutch Data Protection Authority fined Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc. **€824,990,000** over automated individual decisions involving drivers on the platform. The regulator concluded that some drivers could lose access to their accounts and work without sufficiently clear human review and without the safeguards required by GDPR.

If the fine survives Uber's appeal, it will remain one of the largest GDPR penalties ever imposed.

## How Uber could leave a driver without an account

According to the regulator's findings, Uber's systems in certain cases reacted automatically to suspected fraud or very low customer ratings. The consequence could be temporary or permanent account deactivation.

For an Uber driver, an account is not simply a profile. When the account stops working, the person can no longer accept rides and can no longer earn income through the platform. That is exactly why the regulator considers such a decision significant enough to fall within the scope of **GDPR Article 22**, which concerns decisions based solely on automated processing when they produce legal or similarly significant effects.

There is another important point: the frequently cited figure of **126 drivers** is not the regulator's confirmed total number of affected drivers. It is **Uber's own claim** that this was the number of drivers in Europe deactivated because of low ratings during 2021. The full scale of the issue is not visible from publicly available data, once again showing how little insight the public has into the internal data of major platforms.

## What GDPR Article 22 actually says

Article 22 of the GDPR gives a person the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, when that decision produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them.

That does not mean companies are prohibited from using algorithms. It means an algorithm should not become the final judge without serious safeguards. If a decision can take away a person's access to work, income or something else of major importance, protections such as **meaningful human review**, the opportunity to express a point of view and the right to challenge the decision must exist.

And this is one of the most important nuances in the entire case: it is not enough to formally say that "a human was somewhere in the process." If a person merely rubber-stamps what the system has already decided, without any genuine ability to change the outcome, that may still be insufficient.

## Uber disagrees and plans to appeal

Uber argues that the fine is disproportionate and says the company now uses different processes, including human review and opportunities to challenge decisions. It also disputes parts of the regulator's findings, particularly those involving permanent deactivations.

The case therefore needs to be described precisely. At the moment, there is **a regulator's decision and a very large fine**, but there is also **Uber's announced appeal**. Even with that caveat, the case is already important because it raises a broader question about how digital platforms make decisions that can directly change someone's life.

## Why this case matters far beyond Uber

The Uber case is not only about drivers. It touches a much broader problem involving the platform economy and online business.

During Covid, many people moved a large part of their work online. Some started new businesses there, while others expanded existing businesses through social networks and digital platforms. For many small businesses, a profile or account was no longer simply a place to publish content. It became a hub for communication, marketing, sales, audience building and access to customers.

When such an account disappears in seconds, the consequences can be very real and very expensive.

## Facebook and Instagram: accounts gone, answers missing

Many Facebook and Instagram users have said for years that their accounts were disabled without a clear explanation, without genuine contact with human support and without a comprehensible answer to the question of **who actually made the decision**.

A particularly sensitive part of the issue is that many small business owners say they never learned whether their penalty and removal from the platform was decided by a human, an automated system, AI moderation or some combination of the three. For many users, the experience looks similar: the account disappears, the reasons remain unclear, appeal mechanisms appear opaque and there is a sense that no actual person on the other side genuinely reviewed the case.

That is exactly why the Uber case matters. Not because it automatically proves that Meta acts in the same way, but because it shows how serious the issue becomes when a platform uses algorithms or other opaque processes to decide whether someone can continue accessing work, an audience or a business.

Meta's Oversight Board itself warned this year that Meta disables **billions of accounts every year**. Many of those deactivations are legitimate, but many remain inexplicable to affected users. The Board specifically warned that small businesses can lose access to their business, income, contacts and years of built-up digital presence when an account disappears.

There is also an online petition against wrongful Meta account deactivations that, according to publicly visible figures, has already gathered **more than 64,000 signatures and is approaching 65,000**. That shows the problem is not limited to a handful of isolated cases. Nobody knows with certainty how many users worldwide may actually be affected. The real number could be much higher — possibly hundreds of thousands or even millions — but no reliable global figure is publicly available.

## The key question is not only whether AI is used

This is not just a story about artificial intelligence. It is a story about **accountability**.

Companies today use automation, scoring systems, fraud detection, automated moderation, recommendation models and increasingly sophisticated AI tools. But the central question remains the same: who is responsible when a system decides that a person no longer has access to work, customers or years of digital work they have built?

"The algorithm decided" is not an adequate answer. If the consequence of a decision can leave someone without income or without a business, users should at least have the right to know what happened, why it happened and whether the decision was genuinely reviewed by a human with real authority to change it.

## What the Uber fine really tells us

The biggest message from this case is not simply that Uber received an enormous fine. The real message is that European regulators are increasingly focusing on the point at which **a digital decision becomes a real-life decision**.

For a computer, deactivation may be nothing more than changing a status from ACTIVE to DISABLED.

For a real person, it can mean being unable to drive, advertise, manage a business page, communicate with customers or continue a business that took years to build online.

That is why this case matters not only for Uber, but also for social networks and every future platform that will increasingly rely on automated decision-making.

## Our view

* **Uber's €824.99 million fine is one of the largest GDPR penalties ever imposed** and an important signal that automated decisions about people are becoming a major regulatory issue.
* **The central problem is not algorithms themselves**, but situations in which an automated system can take away someone's access to work or income without sufficiently clear and meaningful human review.
* **The figure of 126 drivers is not a confirmed total number of people affected**, but Uber's own figure for one category of deactivations, highlighting how opaque platform data often remains.
* **This case goes far beyond Uber**, because more jobs, customer relationships and income now depend on accounts controlled by digital platforms.
* **During Covid, many small businesses moved their work online** or expanded existing businesses through social networks, making account deactivation a decision with very real economic consequences.
* **Many Facebook and Instagram users say they lost access to their accounts without a clear explanation**, without real contact with a person and without knowing whether AI moderation, an automated system or a human made the decision.
* **Meta's Oversight Board has already warned that Meta disables billions of accounts every year**, while petitions and numerous public complaints show that some users believe they were wrongly affected.
* **The key question for the future is not simply whether a company uses AI, but who is accountable when an automated decision shuts someone out of their work, audience or digital identity.**
