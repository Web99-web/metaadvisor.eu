# What does Meta do with your face recording?

In 2021, Meta announced that it was shutting down Facebook's large facial-recognition system and deleting facial-recognition templates belonging to more than one billion users. It would have been easy to conclude that Facebook's facial-recognition story had largely ended there.

It had not.

Meta now uses faces again in several different procedures. A video selfie can be used to recover access to an account, confirm identity or estimate age, while AI can simultaneously analyse photos and videos to assess whether an account may belong to a minor. These systems are not the same, but they raise one very simple question: **what actually happens to our face after we show it to Meta's camera?**

## Meta brought facial recognition back

Meta began testing facial recognition in 2024 for the recovery of compromised Facebook and Instagram accounts and later announced its expansion to additional markets. The user records a video selfie, and Meta compares the face in the video with profile photos on the account the person is trying to regain access to.

The company says the video selfie is encrypted and securely stored and that the **facial data generated during the comparison is deleted immediately after the verification is completed**, regardless of whether a match is found.

However, deleting data generated during the comparison is not necessarily the same as answering how long the original recording itself remains stored. For a different Instagram video-selfie procedure, Meta states that the video will be deleted **within 30 days**.

That alone shows that Meta uses several different video-selfie procedures, with differently described rules for processing and retention.

## Deleting the video is not necessarily the same as deleting everything derived from it

With facial recognition, a computer does not need to look at a face in the way a human does. Mathematical data can be created from an image or video in order to compare one face with another.

That is why asking **“Was the video deleted?”** is not enough. Users also need to know whether a facial template, embedding, match score, confidence score, liveness score or another derived data point is created, how long it exists, whether it is linked to the account and whether it can later be used again.

Meta says that its facial-recognition system for account recovery deletes generated facial data after the comparison. That is an important claim, but publicly available information is not equally detailed for every video-selfie process Meta uses.

The question is therefore not only where the recording ends up. It is also **what is created from it before it is deleted**.

## An external company also processes faces for age estimation

For age verification, Meta uses the British company Yoti. If a user chooses the video-selfie option, Meta says it sends Yoti an image of the face without other information about the user. Yoti's AI then estimates the person's age based on facial characteristics and returns the result to Meta.

Yoti says images used in this process are **not reviewed by humans**, but analysed by an automated system, deleted after the estimate and not used for subsequent model training.

This is not the same as facial recognition. Age estimation attempts to determine approximately how old a person is. Facial recognition attempts to determine whether one face matches another.

But in both cases, users hand over a highly sensitive recording of their own face and have to trust that it is handled exactly as the companies describe.

## Meta AI already analyses photos and videos for age signals

A video selfie is not the only way Meta analyses a user's appearance. During 2026, the company expanded AI systems intended to identify users who may be minors even when their accounts state that they are adults.

The system can analyse textual signals, but also photos and videos for visual indicators associated with age. Meta stresses that this **is not facial recognition**, that the system does not create “facial fingerprints” and does not attempt to establish a person's identity, but instead assesses general characteristics associated with age.

That still means a photo or video on a profile is not necessarily only content a user shares with other people. It can also become material analysed by AI to draw an additional conclusion about the user.

## When an “appeal” to recover a Facebook or Instagram account consists only of recording your face

A particularly important question arises when a video selfie is not an optional extra verification method, but the only step offered to a user who is at risk of losing an account.

In one specific case, a long-standing Facebook account suddenly became inaccessible. The user was not offered a conventional appeal process in which they could learn which rule had allegedly been violated or explain the situation. When attempting to log in, the only option presented was a camera check: recording the face while moving the head according to instructions shown on the screen.

A message then appeared stating that the “appeal” was under review. Approximately half an hour later, the account was permanently disabled. The user received no decision number that could be forwarded to the Oversight Board, and it was not clear whether a human had reviewed the decision.

That leaves an important question: what was the recording actually intended to establish? That a real person was behind the account, a comparison of identity, an automated security signal, or part of the process that ultimately resulted in permanent deactivation?

## Who has access to the video: AI, a Meta employee or an external contractor?

There is no single answer to who actually sees or processes a video selfie.

For Yoti's age estimation, the processing is carried out, according to the company, by an external partner's AI without human review. For Meta's facial-recognition account-recovery system, Meta describes an automated comparison between the video selfie and profile photographs.

For other Instagram video-selfie procedures, the Help Center states that the review can take up to two working days, but the public description does not always make clear whether this means an entirely automated system, human review in some cases or a combination of both.

Before handing over an image of their face, users should know who the data controller is, whether external processors are involved, whether humans can access the recording, how long the data is stored and what is deleted after the verification is complete.

## What about children?

The issue becomes even more sensitive when children and teenagers are involved. Meta can request additional age verification, and one available method can be a video selfie in which the face is analysed by automated technology.

Meta and Yoti say that an image used for age estimation is deleted after the process and that the age-estimation system does not identify a specific person.

There is no evidence that Meta secretly activates the camera and records children while they are simply scrolling Facebook or Instagram. But it is entirely legitimate to ask **whether a minor undergoing a video-selfie check actually understands what is happening to their face, who is processing it and what remains after the check is completed**.

For children, transparency rules should be considerably stricter than for adults.

## Can a face remain an identifier for the future?

If a person has once completed a video-selfie verification, could any data derived from that recording later help Meta recognise the same person during another login attempt, the creation of another account or a new security check?

For its account-recovery system, Meta says it deletes generated facial data immediately after the comparison. If that statement covers all mathematical data created from the face, no permanent facial identifier should remain from that specific process.

But the user has no simple way to verify exactly what was generated from the recording, which data were associated with the account and whether everything was in fact deleted.

That matters particularly because Meta uses several video-selfie procedures for different purposes and describes their rules in different ways.

## What if you cannot reach anyone at Meta at all?

For a blocked user, there is another practical problem: sometimes it is difficult not only to obtain an answer about a video selfie, but to find any communication channel through which a person at Meta can be reached.

When login access, the Help Center and automated procedures are closed, when no clear answer is available about why an account was blocked or about GDPR questions concerning a face recording, and every attempt leads in circles, one remaining option can look almost absurdly old-fashioned for one of the world's largest technology companies: **paper, an envelope and registered mail**. ## Meta shut down facial recognition — and later brought it back

Meta shut down Facebook's large Face Recognition system in 2021 after years of regulatory and social controversy and announced the deletion of facial-recognition templates belonging to more than one billion users.

But the company did not promise never to use facial recognition again.

Today, the technology has returned in more narrowly defined processes such as account recovery and identity verification, while other forms of facial analysis are used for age estimation and automated analysis of photos and videos.

So the question is no longer whether Meta uses faces.

It does.

The real question is **for what purpose, with what technology, who receives the data, how long it is retained and what exactly remains after the company says the recording has been deleted.**

## Our view

* **Meta now uses several different systems involving faces.** Facial recognition, video-selfie identity verification and AI age estimation are different processes and should not be presented as if they follow the same rules.
* **Deleting the original video is not the only important question.** Users need to know whether facial templates, embeddings, match scores or other derived data are created and how long they are retained.
* **It is not always clear who processes the recording.** In some procedures, an external company's AI performs the processing; in others, Meta's own technology is involved, while public descriptions do not always clearly explain whether humans can also access the data.
* **Transparency rules should be particularly strict for children.** Minors and their parents should clearly understand why a face is requested, who analyses it and what remains after verification.
* **A video selfie should not become a black box.** If recording a face is a condition for accessing an account or completing an appeal process, users should receive a clear answer about what the recording established and what happened to the data afterward.
* **The most important open question remains future identification.** If Meta says that all derived facial data are deleted, users should be clearly told that no permanent identifier remains after verification that can be used in other processes.

Meta once shut down its large facial-recognition system. Facial recognition has since returned in a different form and for different purposes.

Every request asking a user to turn a camera toward their face should therefore come with an equally simple answer to an equally simple question:

**What will you do with my face — and how long will you keep that information?**
