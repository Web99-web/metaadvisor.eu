# Copper takes the lead among metals

Metals are no longer a market accessible only to traditional commodity brokers, large funds and companies that physically buy or sell raw materials. Gold, silver, copper and other metals are increasingly available on crypto exchanges through perpetual futures and other derivative products, meaning their price movements can now be traded almost as easily as Bitcoin or Ethereum.

That does not mean copper has become a cryptocurrency. Its price is still driven by the global physical metals market, mines, industrial demand, inventories and major traditional markets such as the U.S. COMEX and the London Metal Exchange. What has changed is access to the market: crypto infrastructure now allows even small traders to gain exposure to metal price movements without buying physical copper or opening a traditional commodity brokerage account.

The amount of capital required is particularly important. On crypto exchanges where such derivatives are available, traders do not need to buy a tonne of copper, a large standardized futures contract or even a full pound of physical metal. For example, MEXC currently lists a minimum order size of around 0.67 USDT for its COPPER(XCU)USDT perpetual contract. This means a position can be opened with less than one dollar, although the actual minimum amount changes together with the market price.

The buyer does not become the owner of several grams, kilograms or a tonne of physical copper. Instead, they trade a derivative whose value follows the price of copper. At the same time, leverage can amplify both profits and losses, so a small initial amount does not necessarily mean a small risk.

This type of access significantly lowers the entry barrier. A trader holding BTC, ETH or a stablecoin on the same platform can quickly redirect part of that capital into a gold, silver or copper position. This is exactly why metals are becoming increasingly interesting when tracking capital rotation between crypto markets, AI and tech stocks, precious metals and industrial commodities.

And now a new shift is appearing within the metals trade itself. Gold remains at very high levels, but it is no longer the only metal attracting capital. Copper has surged toward historic highs, copper miners are also strengthening, and the move has spread to other industrial metals such as zinc.

## Copper around $6.7 per pound – but what does “pound” mean?

U.S. COMEX copper futures closed on August 25 at around $6.71 per pound of weight, the highest closing price in copper's history. The following morning, prices across different contracts and products continued to trade at approximately $6.7–$6.8.

Here it is important to explain the word “pound”, because for European readers it can easily sound like a reference to the British currency.

In this case, however, it does not mean the British pound sterling or GBP, but a unit of mass abbreviated as **lb**. One pound weighs approximately 454 grams.

The U.S. market traditionally quotes copper in U.S. dollars per pound of weight. A price of, for example, $6.80 therefore means approximately 6.80 U.S. dollars for 454 grams of copper.

The London Metal Exchange also uses U.S. dollars, but quotes copper per metric tonne. This is why copper can simultaneously be shown at around $6.8 on the U.S. market and around $15,000 on the London market even though these represent approximately the same value of metal expressed in two different units of weight.

One metric tonne contains around 2,204.6 pounds. A price of $6.80 per pound therefore corresponds to approximately $14,991 per metric tonne.

**$6.80/lb ≈ $14,991 per metric tonne.**

The difference, therefore, is not the currency. Both New York and London use the U.S. dollar. The difference is that the U.S. market quotes copper in USD per pound of weight, while the LME quotes it in USD per metric tonne.

## Copper rises while gold is no longer the only leader

The shift is particularly interesting because it follows a strong period for gold. Gold remains at very high levels and its safe-haven story has not disappeared, but copper has shown stronger momentum in recent days.

This changes the structure of the metals trade. After a period in which the main focus was on gold as protection against inflation, U.S. debt, a weaker dollar and geopolitical uncertainty, capital is increasingly appearing in the part of the market linked to industry, energy infrastructure, electrification and AI data centers.

It does not look as if investors are simply selling gold and buying copper. Instead, the metals trade appears to be broadening: capital remains in precious metals while simultaneously seeking opportunities in industrial metals and the companies that produce them.

## Why is copper so strong right now?

The long-term copper story is already well known. Power grids, electric vehicles, renewable energy, industrial electrification and large data centers require enormous quantities of copper. As a result, copper is increasingly viewed as one of the key commodities of the energy and AI infrastructure cycle.

But the current rise is not only a story about AI and future demand.

U.S. trade policy and the possibility of new tariffs on refined copper imports from 2027 are playing a major role. Fears of future tariffs have encouraged traders to redirect large quantities of physical copper toward the United States, where the U.S. price is higher.

COMEX inventories reached a record 675,185 metric tonnes and had risen for 46 consecutive days by August 25.

At the same time, available copper supplies outside the United States are shrinking. This is an important nuance: the world has not suddenly run out of copper, but a large amount of metal has ended up in U.S. warehouses and has become less accessible to markets outside the country.

A market that had previously been expected to have a global surplus therefore looks much tighter in practice. Additional pressure is coming from low inventories in some markets, problems at mines and disruptions in processing.

## London has already experienced a real copper squeeze

An event on the London Metal Exchange in mid-August showed just how tight the market has become.

The cash copper price briefly reached a record $14,912 per metric tonne, while the premium for immediately available copper over three-month delivery jumped to as much as $545 per tonne.

The reason was a shortage of readily available physical copper needed to close certain positions. Three large market participants held long positions that together exceeded the quantity of available metal in LME warehouses, forcing short traders to quickly find copper for delivery.

Such a squeeze is not the same as healthy long-term demand growth. However, it shows how sensitive the supply chain currently is and how strongly the movement of physical metal toward the United States can affect prices in the rest of the world.

## Copper miners confirm that capital is following the metal

For the Capital Rotation Watch, it is particularly interesting that the copper price itself is not the only thing rising.

The Global X Copper Miners ETF, COPX, gained 1.89% on August 25. Just a few days earlier it had also recorded daily gains of more than 5%, while between August 18 and August 25 it rose from $85.57 to $96.32.

That is a more important signal than a single short-term jump in a futures contract. If the market believes high copper prices could persist, the companies that produce copper become the natural next part of the trade because higher prices can increase their revenues and potentially their profit margins.

If copper rises for one or two days purely because of a squeeze, it may be a temporary market distortion. If copper, miners and other industrial metals rise at the same time, it becomes far more interesting to talk about a broader capital rotation.

## Zinc also reaches a four-year high

Copper is not completely alone.

Zinc reached $3,858 per metric tonne on the London Metal Exchange on August 25, its highest level in four years.

Zinc is also facing very low LME inventories and pressure on short positions, so its rise should not automatically be interpreted as proof of a strong global economic recovery.

But the combination is becoming interesting.

Copper is rising, copper miners are rising, zinc is rising, while gold remains very high but is no longer the only leader of the metals trade.

That is a different market structure from one in which capital is simply fleeing into gold.

## The metals trade is changing character

Gold and copper traditionally send different economic signals. Gold is often bought as protection against inflation, fiscal problems, geopolitical uncertainty or currency depreciation. Copper is much more closely linked to the real economy – power grids, construction, industry, energy, vehicles and data centers.

That is why copper is often called “Dr. Copper”, because its price movement is used as one indicator of expectations for future industrial activity. However, the current copper record is not a pure signal of stronger economic growth.

U.S. tariff threats, the movement of physical metal into the United States, record COMEX inventories, reduced copper availability elsewhere, mine problems and processing disruptions are currently having a significant impact on the price. What happens after this surge will therefore be more important than the record itself. If copper remains strong, miners continue to outperform the broader market, and zinc, aluminium and other industrial metals join the rotation, the signal would become considerably stronger.

The most important change so far is not that gold has become weak. It has not.

**The change is that the metals trade is no longer just a gold trade.**
