# Maya Protocol halted: hacker still holds 20.83 BTC

While the crypto market’s attention in recent days has been almost completely captured by Bitcoin’s strong rally, one serious DeFi incident has gone relatively unnoticed. Maya Protocol halted MAYAChain on August 18 after an exploit in which an attacker used a series of software bugs to extract Bitcoin and other assets from liquidity pools.

Four days later, the story is still not over. According to the latest available information, MAYAChain remains halted, swaps have not resumed normally, and the address linked to the attacker was still holding around 20.83 BTC on August 21, with no outgoing transfer recorded.

At the same time, Bitcoin’s price increase means that the value of this portion of the stolen assets has also risen. Around 20.83 BTC was worth approximately $1.34 million at the time of the attack. At the levels Bitcoin reached after its latest major surge, the value of the same coins increased to roughly $1.6 million.

## The attack was not caused by just one bug

Preliminary technical analysis showed that the attacker did not exploit one simple vulnerability, but rather a chain of six software bugs.

The attack was carried out in a single transaction containing 23 messages. A combination of issues involving trade accounts, outbound transaction processing and liquidity pool calculations managed to trigger an incorrect theft-detection mechanism.

The system then artificially increased the balance of one pool by approximately 49 million CACAO tokens, even though the corresponding assets did not actually exist in the reserves. When the transfer that was supposed to cover that balance failed, the altered balance nevertheless remained recorded.

The attacker was then able, with a very small deposit, to obtain an almost complete share of the affected pool and withdraw approximately 48.87 million CACAO tokens from Maya Protocol’s Asgard module.

Those tokens were subsequently used to extract real assets from other pools, including Bitcoin, Ether and stablecoins.

## How much was actually stolen: $1.36 million or nearly $11 million?

Very different figures appear in reports about the incident, but they do not represent the same thing.

Around $1.36 million refers to the value of assets that were actually transferred to external blockchains during the attack. The total value of assets and positions under the attacker’s control was estimated at approximately $1.65 million to $1.7 million.

However, the total decline in the value of Maya Protocol’s liquidity pools has been estimated at as much as around $10.9 million.

That does not mean the hacker stole nearly $11 million.

A large part of the additional loss resulted from the sharp collapse in CACAO and the arbitrage that followed after the token’s price lost its relationship with the value of other assets in the pools. Traders were able to buy heavily devalued CACAO and exchange it for Bitcoin, Ether, stablecoins and other real assets available within the protocol.

## CACAO lost nearly 89 percent of its value

CACAO, Maya Protocol’s native token, was trading at around $0.115 before the exploit.

During the incident, its price fell toward approximately $0.013, a decline of nearly 89 percent. After the initial collapse, the token partially recovered toward the $0.03 area, but remained far below its pre-attack value.

That price collapse further increased the damage suffered by liquidity providers.

This is why the Maya Protocol case is more interesting than a simple story about a hacker stealing $1.7 million. The technical exploit created a chain reaction through the protocol’s own internal economy, causing losses many times greater than the amount the attacker was able to extract directly.

## Hacker still has not moved the 20.83 BTC

According to the latest on-chain check published on August 21, the Bitcoin address linked to the attack was still holding approximately 20.8273 BTC, with no outgoing transfer recorded from the address.

Maya Protocol halted the network after the attack to prevent further losses and began working on fixes for the vulnerabilities.

The team also publicly expressed hope that the attacker would return the assets in exchange for a bug bounty. However, according to the latest available information, no final plan has been announced to resolve all liquidity-provider losses, nor has a complete timetable for restarting the system been published.

That may now be the more important part of the story than the exploit itself: four days after the attack, users are still waiting for a definitive answer on how the protocol will recover and who will ultimately bear the multimillion-dollar decline in pool value.

## Bitcoin rally increased the value of the loot

In the meantime, there has been an almost ironic twist.

Bitcoin rose sharply after the attack. This means that, without making any additional move, the attacker now controls Bitcoin worth more than it was on the day of the exploit.

Approximately 20.83 BTC was worth around $1.34 million at the time of the attack. After Bitcoin’s rise, the value of that amount approached $1.6 million.

The difference is more than a quarter of a million dollars purely because of the change in Bitcoin’s market price.

Still, 20.83 BTC is a negligible amount compared with the overall size of the Bitcoin market. Even if the attacker decided to sell the entire amount, such a sale by itself would not represent meaningful selling pressure on Bitcoin’s price.

## Our take

- The Maya Protocol exploit shows why, in DeFi incidents, it is not enough to look only at the headline figure for “stolen funds.”
- The attacker managed to move around $1.36 million worth of assets to external blockchains, while the total value of assets and positions under the attacker’s control was estimated at approximately $1.65 million to $1.7 million.
- The total decline in the value of Maya Protocol’s liquidity pools was nevertheless estimated at nearly $11 million, which does not mean that the hacker directly stole that amount.
- A large part of the additional damage resulted from the collapse of CACAO and arbitrage that made it possible to exchange heavily devalued tokens for Bitcoin, Ether, stablecoins and other real assets from the pools.
- The attack did not depend on a single spectacular security vulnerability, but on a chain of six software bugs that could be combined in one transaction containing 23 messages.
- Particularly concerning is that a protection mechanism designed to detect theft became part of the exploit itself and allowed a balance to remain recorded even though it was not backed by real assets.
- Four days after the attack, MAYAChain was still halted, CACAO remained far below its pre-exploit price, and the address linked to the attacker was still holding approximately 20.83 BTC.
- Bitcoin’s subsequent price increase raised the value of those 20.83 BTC from approximately $1.34 million to around $1.6 million without the attacker having to make any additional move.
- The key question is now no longer only how much the hacker took, but whether Maya Protocol can fully recover, secure its liquidity pools again and compensate users for the multimillion-dollar losses.
