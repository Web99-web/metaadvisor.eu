# EU Inc.: company in 48 hours for €100

Incorporate a company online for no more than €100, with no mandatory minimum share capital, choose the EU member state in which it will be registered and then operate across the European Union under a large set of common corporate rules – that is the basic idea behind **EU Inc.**

The European Commission describes the new legal framework as the **28th regime**, an additional system that would exist alongside the 27 national company-law systems. Entrepreneurs today choose between Germany’s GmbH, France’s SARL or SAS, Italy’s S.r.l., Estonia’s OÜ, Latvia’s SIA, Lithuania’s UAB, Denmark’s ApS, Bulgaria’s OOD, Romania’s SRL, Croatia’s d.o.o. and numerous other national legal forms. EU Inc. would become another option – this time a pan-European one.

However, it is important to stress from the outset that EU Inc. **is not yet a legal form that can be incorporated today**. The European Commission presented the legislative proposal on March 18, 2026, and as of August 22 the adoption process is still ongoing. The European Parliament is still shaping its position, while member states in the Council are working on a compromise text, meaning individual rules may still change before final adoption.

## What exactly is EU Inc.?

EU Inc. is designed as a harmonised limited-liability company form that would be available in all member states. It would not abolish existing national company forms, but would instead give entrepreneurs an additional option when they want a company designed primarily for operating across the European single market.

It could be established by one or more natural or legal persons, and the new legal form would not be reserved only for entirely new startups. According to the proposal, an EU Inc. could also be created through the conversion of an existing company, a merger, a division or a cross-border conversion.

Once registered in the business register of the chosen member state, an EU Inc. would have legal personality that would have to be recognised by the other EU member states. For matters not governed by the European regulation, the relevant national rules would continue to apply, meaning EU Inc. would not completely erase the differences between national legal systems.

## A company in 48 hours for no more than €100

The element attracting the most attention is the possibility of establishing an EU Inc. **within 48 hours at a registration cost of no more than €100**. Under the Commission proposal, this accelerated procedure would apply to digital registration using a standardised European template for the incorporation documents.

The process is intended to be fully online, while the maximum €100 should also include any necessary preventive administrative, judicial or notarial procedures required by a particular national system. Physical presence of the founder should be the exception, for example where there are justified doubts about a person’s identity or legal capacity.

The 48-hour deadline therefore does not necessarily mean that every complex company with a highly customised set of articles will be incorporated in two days. The fast-track procedure is intended primarily for standardised incorporation where the founder accepts the European template.

## Capital? Minimum €0

The €100 figure does not refer to share capital, but to the maximum cost of accelerated registration. Under the current proposal, EU Inc. **would have no mandatory minimum share capital**, while the proposed incorporation template even provides for capital of **€0**.

This is particularly relevant for startups and other companies whose initial value may not lie in machinery, property or large amounts of cash, but in software, knowledge, intellectual property or their ability to attract future investors. At the same time, creditor-protection rules would be intended to ensure that the possibility of incorporation without minimum capital does not mean an absence of financial responsibility.

## Registered in one country, operating in another

One of the most interesting parts of the proposal is the possibility for founders to choose the member state in which they want to register their EU Inc. The registered office would have to be located within the European Union, while the central administration or principal place of business would also have to remain within the EU.

However, **the registered office and the location of the main business activity would not necessarily have to be in the same country**. A company could, for example, be registered in France while most of its business operations, employees or investments are located in Germany.

This attempts to address one of the long-standing problems of the European market: a company can sell across borders almost as easily as it sells domestically, but once it wants to establish a company, bring in an investor, transfer shares or reorganise its business, it once again enters an entirely different national system.

## Data should only have to be submitted once

EU Inc. is not intended to simplify only the incorporation of a company, but also its interaction with government registers. One of the basic principles of the proposal is **“once only”** – if certain information has already been provided to one competent authority, the entrepreneur should not have to enter it again into numerous other government systems.

Information required for the business register, tax identification, VAT, social security and the beneficial ownership register should be exchanged electronically between the competent authorities. In practice, this should reduce the number of forms and situations in which one government institution asks an entrepreneur for a document that another public authority already holds.

If the system ultimately works as intended, this change could prove almost as important for many small companies as the promise of incorporation within 48 hours.

## Does EU Inc. mean the same tax across Europe?

No. EU Inc. attempts to harmonise a large part of **company law and administrative procedures**, but it does not introduce a single corporate income tax rate across the European Union. Taxation remains largely within national competence.

This means that registering an EU Inc. in one country would not automatically allow a company to tax all of its profits exclusively under that country’s rules regardless of where the business is actually conducted. The place of effective management, permanent establishments, employees, the location where income arises and other factors could continue to matter for tax purposes.

The European Parliament has meanwhile opened a separate discussion on a future tax module for the 28th regime, including the possibility of simpler cross-border tax procedures and easier management of VAT obligations. However, this is not the same as the current EU Inc. proposal and does not mean Europe is introducing a single corporate income tax rate.

## And what about employees?

EU Inc. would also not create a completely uniform European labour-law system. National rules concerning employees, social security, mandatory employment rights and worker protection would continue to play an important role.

A company could therefore not simply choose the country of registration with the rules it finds most favourable and then ignore the rights of employees in the country where they actually work. The purpose of EU Inc. is to simplify corporate structures and cross-border business, not to create a way of circumventing national labour and social legislation.

On the other hand, the proposal seeks to make it easier for startups and growing companies to use models for attracting employees, including different forms of employee options and ownership participation. This is an area in which European companies frequently argue that they struggle to compete with US companies because of significant legal and tax fragmentation between member states.

## A company in 48 hours – but what about payroll? Will businesses still need accountants for that?

EU Inc. could significantly simplify the process of establishing a company, but for now it does not solve one of the biggest everyday administrative burdens faced by small businesses – calculating salaries, taxes and social contributions.

The problem is not simply that different contributions exist, but that a small employer must know and continuously follow national rules, contribution bases, tax rates, sick-leave rules, annual-leave rules and legislative changes, or hire an accountant to do it. The current EU Inc. proposal does not harmonise this area.

In the longer term, a much greater administrative simplification would be an official digital system capable of automatically applying the current rules of the country in which an employee works. An employer could, for example, specify that an employee should receive €2,000 net, while the system itself calculates the gross salary, employee and employer contributions, tax and total labour cost, and prepares the required filings. If the EU wants a company to be established within 48 hours, it is also reasonable to ask how simple it will be to run that company every month afterwards.

## What happens with national and local business support?

This could become one of the most interesting practical questions surrounding the entire EU Inc. project. National, regional and municipal support programmes often have their own rules determining who may apply, where a company must operate, where an investment must take place, how many people it must employ or what local economic impact a project must generate.

With EU Inc., the situation becomes more complex because the country of registration, the nationality of the owner and the location of the actual business activity would no longer necessarily point to the same country. A German citizen could, for example, establish an EU Inc. in France while developing most of the company’s business in Germany.

That raises a very practical question: could such a company apply for German support because it invests and operates in Germany even though it is registered in France? Could it apply for French support because it is legally registered in France even though its owner is not a French citizen? The answer would not automatically be the same for every programme because governments would still be able to set objective criteria linked to the location of investment, employees, the nature of the project and its actual economic impact.

However, the basic logic of EU Inc. is that a company **should not be automatically discriminated against simply because its registered office is in another member state**. Likewise, in a genuinely single European market it would be difficult to justify a system in which a company cannot access a particular business-support programme solely because its owner is not a citizen of the country providing the support, provided all other objectively justified conditions of the programme can be met.

That does not mean an EU Inc. would automatically be entitled to every subsidy or incentive in every member state. Governments could still require that the money be invested within their territory, that jobs be created there or that the project provide a concrete benefit to their economy. But **such companies should not fall between different national systems merely because of the formal country of registration or the nationality of their owners**.

This is why EU Inc. raises a much broader question than simply how to establish a company within 48 hours: **what does a “domestic company” actually mean in a single European market?**

## EU Inc. is also designed to make attracting investors easier

A large part of the project is aimed at startups and scale-ups seeking to raise capital across several member states. Today, an investor who understands the rules governing an investment in a German company does not necessarily understand those applying to a French, Italian, Estonian or Romanian company, while the cost of legal advice increases with each additional market.

EU Inc. should therefore allow more flexible capital structures, different classes of shares or ownership interests, more digital transfer of ownership and the use of investment instruments more suitable for startups. The idea is that, over time, the EU Inc. label itself would tell an investor which basic corporate rules apply to a company regardless of the country in which it is registered.

This is particularly important for European technology companies that often begin in relatively small national economies but already need to seek customers, employees and capital across borders at an early stage.

## What if you already have an existing company?

EU Inc. is not intended only for entrepreneurs who are just starting a business. Under the proposal, an existing national company could be converted into an EU Inc. through a domestic or cross-border conversion, without necessarily having to close the existing company first and then establish an entirely new one.

This could be particularly interesting for businesses that started as local companies but over time acquired employees, customers, suppliers or investors across several member states. Instead of creating an entire series of separate national structures, EU Inc. is intended to offer one more recognisable corporate foundation.

Naturally, the final conditions for such a conversion will depend on the text ultimately adopted by the European Parliament and the Council.

## Easier to open – but also easier to close

The European Commission is attempting to cover the entire life cycle of a company rather than only its incorporation. The proposal therefore also includes more digital procedures for the liquidation of solvent companies and simpler processes in certain insolvency situations.

This is more important than it may initially appear. An entrepreneurial system is not competitive only when it is easy to establish a successful company, but also when a failed business project does not lead to years of administrative procedures, costs and legal uncertainty.

If EU Inc. is intended to encourage people to start companies more often and invest more easily across borders, it also needs to provide a relatively orderly way to exit a business when a project fails.

## When could EU Inc. actually become available?

As of August 22, 2026, the answer is simple: **we do not yet know**. The proposal exists, but the legislative procedure has not been completed, and individual elements may still change during negotiations between the European Parliament and the member states.

Only after the final rules are adopted and the necessary digital infrastructure is built will it be possible to determine the actual date from which entrepreneurs will be able to register an EU Inc.

The claim that it is already possible today to establish a “European company for €100” is therefore incorrect. More precisely, the European Union is preparing a system that, if the key elements of the proposal survive the legislative process, should allow **online incorporation within 48 hours, at a cost of no more than €100 and with no mandatory minimum share capital**.

## Our take

- EU Inc. could become one of the most important changes to European company law in recent years because it is the first serious attempt to create a legal form designed primarily for operating across the entire single market.
- The most attractive part of the proposal is digital fast-track registration within 48 hours at a maximum cost of €100.
- That €100 is not share capital. Under the current proposal, EU Inc. would have no mandatory minimum capital, and capital of €0 would even be possible.
- The possibility of having a registered office in one country and the main business activity in another could significantly change how European entrepreneurs choose where to establish their companies.
- EU Inc. does not, however, mean one European tax, one labour law or automatic access to every national support programme. National rules will remain important wherever the European regulation does not harmonise them.
- Public support will be a particularly important issue. Companies that genuinely invest and operate in a member state should not be automatically excluded solely because of their country of registration or the nationality of their owner, while governments should still be able to require genuine local investment, jobs or economic impact.
- Business-support programmes may therefore become one of the clearest tests of how far the EU is prepared to apply the idea of the single market in practice, rather than merely make company registration easier.
- The “once-only” principle could prove just as important to entrepreneurs in practice as incorporation within 48 hours because it aims to eliminate repeated submission of the same information to different government bodies.
- The possibility of converting existing national companies into EU Inc. means the project is relevant not only to new startups but also to existing companies seeking to expand across borders.
- The biggest unknown remains the final text. EU Inc. has not yet been adopted, and the rules may still change during the legislative process before the new company form actually becomes available.
