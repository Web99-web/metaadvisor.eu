# Facebook blocked India’s Prime Minister Narendra Modi - then apologized?

An unusual story from India may have gone largely unnoticed by many people around the world. Facebook temporarily blocked a post by Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The Indian government reacted in a way an ordinary Facebook user can only dream of: it immediately summoned senior Meta representatives and demanded that the company explain at the highest level what had happened. Meta then acknowledged an “operational error,” and its Chief Global Affairs Officer Joel Kaplan personally apologized to the Indian minister on behalf of the company.

## An apology for the Prime Minister, for an ordinary user - THE END!

At the same time, Facebook temporarily or permanently removes the profiles of ordinary people because of moderation decisions - including users who have spent 15 or 20 years on the social network. A ban can arrive in a second: the account is deactivated, the user is thrown out of Facebook, and years of photos, messages, contacts, groups, pages and business connections suddenly become inaccessible. **THE END!**

And the user often does not even find out **what exactly they did**. Instead, they receive a generic message such as **“We've disabled your account”**, accompanied by the broad claim that the account “doesn't follow our Community Standards on account integrity” — without identifying the specific post, comment, photograph or action that caused someone to be removed after decades of using the network.

Then another sentence may appear:

**“You cannot request another review of this decision.”**

In other words, Facebook can give a user only a general category of an alleged violation, fail to explain what they actually did, permanently disable their account and, at the same time, tell them they can no longer request another review of the decision.

## A Prime Minister can call Meta. An ordinary person cannot even reach customer support

Unlike the Indian Prime Minister, an ordinary user does not have a government behind them that can summon Meta’s senior executives and demand answers. There is no minister demanding a meeting, and no senior Meta executive personally examining what happened and then apologizing.

But the difference goes even further. An ordinary person not only cannot call Joel Kaplan or another senior Meta executive. During such a process, they often have no access at all to **HUMAN customer support** where they could explain their case.

A permanent deactivation message does not give them the name of the person who made the decision, the contact details of a moderator, a customer-service telephone number, or a Facebook/Meta email address where they can send their evidence and say: look at what actually happened - the system made a mistake.

**After a block, the Indian government can demand a human explanation from Meta at the highest level. Facebook can permanently shut down an ordinary person’s account without allowing that person to speak even to the lowest level of human customer support.**

A permanent exclusion can happen literally within a second. A user who moments earlier was talking to friends, managing groups or pages and using Messenger is suddenly no longer inside. They have no time to warn friends, leave another contact method or explain to people they may have known for years that Facebook has removed them.

**To their contacts, they simply disappear — and Facebook does not tell those contacts why.** **The disappearance can look voluntary: friends may think they have been unfriended, ghosted or deliberately blocked.** That matters especially after the Covid years, when so much of the world moved online and friendships, work contacts and entire communities were built through Facebook and Messenger. Some of those people may live thousands of kilometres apart and have no phone number, email address or other way to reconnect.

**Facebook does not only remove the person. It can also remove the explanation for their disappearance.**

That is precisely why permanent exclusion from one of the world’s largest social networks is no longer merely a matter of losing a profile. It becomes a question of access to communication, people and information.


## The same story has been repeating for years

For years, people around the world have described sudden suspensions and permanent bans of Facebook and Instagram accounts on Reddit, X, LinkedIn, Mastodon and other platforms. The scenario is often almost identical: an account that is ten, fifteen or twenty years old disappears overnight, the user receives a generic accusation of breaking the rules, tries to appeal and eventually reaches a screen that effectively tells them the decision has been made and there will be no further review.

In recent years, the problem has simply become increasingly visible. Groups of affected users are being created, their stories are being published, and petitions are being launched. One petition calling for Meta to be held accountable over allegedly wrongful account shutdowns has more than **63,000 signatories**.

## How many people does Meta actually remove?

The scale of the system is best illustrated by a figure that sounds almost unbelievable. In June 2026, the Oversight Board warned that Meta deactivates accounts on a scale reaching **billions of accounts per year**. Of course, a large number of those accounts are legitimately removed because of fraud, spam, criminal activity and serious violations of the rules. But the same Board warned that many deactivations are inexplicable to users, often come without a meaningful explanation, and that appeal mechanisms can be confusing, impractical or inaccessible.

We have already covered this issue in <a href="https://metaadvisor.eu/news/meta-oversight-board-ai-moderation-problem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><strong>Meta Oversight Board: AI moderation — a years-long problem is only now coming into focus</strong></a>.

In other words, we are no longer talking about a few angry users complaining on the internet.

We do not know how many of those billions of deactivated accounts belonged to real people who were wrongly permanently banned. Meta does not disclose that number to the public. We therefore cannot responsibly claim whether the figure is 100,000, one million or more real users. But that is precisely part of the problem: a platform that can deactivate accounts on a scale of billions per year does not publish how many of those decisions were later found to be wrong or how many real people managed to recover their digital lives after an error.

Meta itself, for example, said that during 2025 alone it automatically removed more than four million accounts that its systems flagged as suspicious as part of efforts to combat child sexual exploitation. That does not mean those accounts were wrongly banned — this is an extremely serious category in which removing actual offenders is essential. But the figure demonstrates something else: decisions to remove millions of accounts are already being made automatically and at enormous scale.

At the same time, people have reported receiving suspensions over completely harmless content, including family holiday photographs.

## AI does not always understand what a human understands immediately

Automated systems and AI moderation do not always understand what is obvious to a human being: sarcasm, a joke, a question asked in disbelief, the context of a conversation or the meaning of an innocent photograph. Something a person can read and understand within seconds may be interpreted by a system as hate speech, sexual content, violence or another serious violation of the rules.

Meta is also accelerating its use of AI in moderation. The Financial Times reported in June 2026 that large language models had already taken over around half of requests for human content review, while for some types of content the goal is to automate more than 90 percent of those reviews.

It has not been publicly confirmed that AI specifically blocked Modi’s post. Meta did not disclose technical details and described the incident as an “operational error”. But that is precisely why the case raises an even bigger question: if a moderation system can wrongly block a post by the Prime Minister of one of the world’s most populous countries, how many such errors happen to people who do not have a government ready to immediately call Meta?

## Losing Facebook is not just losing the right to post

When someone loses their account, they do not merely lose the ability to write a status update. If their Meta services are connected, they can lose access to **Messenger** and years of private conversations, management of Pages and Groups, as well as **Business Suite** and business resources they may have spent years building.

**But a permanently excluded person is no longer even a normal member of Facebook’s audience. Many people do not understand that a banned person literally NO LONGER HAS ACCESS TO THE CONTENT AND CANNOT SEE what other people are saying or posting on Facebook.**

They can no longer normally use Facebook to follow news, politicians, media, local communities, institutions, warnings and information distributed through the network. A permanently excluded person becomes a kind of **“media leper”**, isolated from a huge part of the information and social communication taking place on the platform.

**Facebook does not only throw them off the stage. It throws them out of the audience as well.**

And that may be the part of the problem politicians still do not understand.

## Permanently banned people also disappear from the political information space

There is another consequence that is almost never discussed. If Meta deactivates accounts on a scale of billions every year, those accounts do not belong only to bots, spammers and scammers. Some of them belong to real people.

**And real people are voters.**

Politicians, governments, ministries, cities, political parties, media and public institutions now use Facebook to publish information instantly - often before a citizen sees it on television, a news website or in a newspaper. Election campaigns are conducted on social networks, prime ministers and presidents speak directly to voters there, and political messages, warnings, press conferences, changes in legislation, emergency information and reactions to events unfolding in real time are published there.

When a real person is permanently removed from Facebook, they therefore do not merely stop being a user of a social network. **They also stop being politically exposed to part of the information that other voters receive instantly.**

Through its own moderation decision, a private company can therefore decide that one voter is no longer part of the digital audience that politicians and institutions address every day.

If the ban is justified because of a serious violation of the rules, that is one thing. But if the system made a mistake, then a real person has not merely been wrongfully silenced. They have also been removed from part of the political and media space in which modern democratic society increasingly operates - and that voter may never get the opportunity to explain the mistake to a real person at Meta.

## One set of rules for important people, another for ordinary users?

After the Modi case, another interesting piece of information emerged: Meta spoke about new protocols for accounts belonging to **“prominent persons”**.

Why only for prominent persons?

If Facebook’s system is capable of wrongly blocking the prime minister of one of the world’s largest countries, it is capable of wrongly blocking a teacher, a pensioner, a student, a small business owner, a journalist, a mother posting a family photograph or someone who has been on Facebook for twenty years.

State laws should apply equally to prime ministers, billionaires, actors and completely unknown citizens. We would not accept a legal system in which an important person receives additional protection from a wrongful decision simply because they are already important. Why should we accept such a principle from one of the largest communication platforms in the world?

Today’s anonymous user may tomorrow become a journalist, entrepreneur, artist, activist, politician - or future prime minister. Nobody is born with a “prominent person” label.

## The EU pretends it protects users from Big Tech — but in reality decisions can remain dead letters

It is also worth looking at what is happening in the European Union, which has for years presented itself as a leader in regulating Big Tech.

Through the Digital Services Act, the EU gave users the possibility of turning to certified out-of-court dispute settlement bodies. One of them is **Appeals Centre Europe**, based in Ireland - just like Meta’s European headquarters.

The problem is almost unbelievable: Appeals Centre Europe can review a case, involve a human reviewer and even decide that the platform was wrong, but its decision **is not binding**. Ultimately, it remains up to the platform whether it implements that decision — or simply ignores it without consequences.

The platform is supposed to participate in the process, but can it simply fail to respond? The user can ultimately be left holding a decision in their favour - but still without their account being restored. In practice, they receive a piece of paper they can do nothing with.

The system has another almost satirical dimension: Appeals Centre Europe’s dispute-processing fees are financed by the very Big Tech platforms whose decisions users are challenging.

**Real-life political satire, not genuine protection of people from Big Tech.**

Perhaps Narendra Modi and the Indian government will be more successful than the EU and its Appeals Centre Europe have been so far. A user whose account has been permanently disabled does not need another document that can remain a dead letter. They need a mechanism that **obliges** the platform to explain its decision, provide a genuine human appeal and correct its mistake.

## Modi has now experienced the shock of being blocked himself

Narendra Modi has now, at least briefly, experienced first-hand what perhaps hundreds of thousands or already millions of ordinary people around the world experience: **shock and disbelief when Facebook suddenly blocks content or an account — and the question: what just happened?**

Unlike them, Modi had the opportunity to get an answer. His government could summon Meta, demand an explanation and receive an apology.

Perhaps the Indian government accepted the explanation that this was an “operational error” because, before this incident, it was not aware of the scale of permanent exclusions affecting ordinary users. But if it had already been informed - or if it now becomes aware of how many such cases are being reported around the world — then the story can no longer end with Meta simply providing better protection for the Prime Minister and other “prominent persons” in the future.

**The Indian government should also help protect its own citizens from the arbitrary power of Big Tech. And laws introduced by a country of India’s size could become an example for protecting ordinary users elsewhere in the world.**

If a person’s account is permanently removed, they must receive a specific explanation of what they did, which post, photograph, comment or action was the problem, which rule they violated and why the punishment is permanent exclusion.

For most violations, there should be more warnings before permanent removal — more “strikes”, an opportunity to correct the behaviour and temporary sanctions before a 15- or 20-year-old account disappears in a second.

And before a final permanent ban, a real person must have access to **HUMAN customer support** and the possibility of having their case reviewed by another real person.

Because there are still people behind the automation.

**SOMEONE CREATED THAT AI. SOMEONE REVIEWED THAT AI. SOMEONE APPROVED THAT AI.**

AI is not a legislator, regulator or court. Responsibility for a system that decides who stays and who is removed from one of the world’s largest communication spaces cannot end with the words — **“technical error”.**

India now has a rare opportunity. Its Prime Minister has personally experienced a problem that ordinary users have been trying to explain for years.

We do not need only new protocols for “prominent persons”.

**We need laws and protocols that protect every person.**

Because the right to a clear explanation, a fair appeal and a conversation with a real human being should not depend on whether you are a prime minister or a completely unknown Facebook user.

**If Facebook made a mistake with Modi, apologized and changed its protocols, why should ordinary people simply disappear from the “social network”?**

For important correspondence, keeping a copy of the letter and proof of delivery may also be useful.

There is something almost symbolic about having to return to paper mail to reach one of the world’s largest technology companies.

And it would certainly be interesting to see what would happen if the mail arriving at Meta’s offices began to reflect the real number of people who say they have been permanently removed from Facebook without ever being able to speak to human customer support.

## Our take

* **Modi’s case shows that Facebook’s moderation can make a mistake even with one of the world’s most powerful politicians.** The difference is that a state immediately stood behind him, while an ordinary user often cannot access even basic human customer support.
* **A permanent ban on an account that is 15 or 20 years old should not be based on a generic message.** The user must know exactly what they did, which rule they violated and why the most severe punishment was chosen.
* **There should be proportionality before permanent removal.** For most violations, users should receive warnings, temporary sanctions and an opportunity to correct their behaviour before their entire digital life disappears in a second.
* **Human support must exist before the final decision.** If someone says an automated system misunderstood a photograph, comment, sarcasm or context, they must have the opportunity to explain the case to a real person.
* **A permanently banned user loses more than the right to post.** They can lose Messenger, Pages, Groups, business tools, contacts and access to part of the social, media and political information space in which communication now takes place every day.
* **Special protocols for “prominent persons” raise a serious question of double standards.** Protection from system errors should not depend on political power, fame or social status.
* **Modi now has the opportunity to do more than accept Meta’s apology.** Having experienced the shock of an unjustified block himself, his government could help create legal protections that make a detailed explanation, proportionate sanctions and a human appeal the right of every user — rather than a privilege of the powerful.
