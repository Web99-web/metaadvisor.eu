# Japan Defends the Yen: Will the Dollar Rise Above 160 Again?

Japan and the United States jointly intervened in the foreign exchange market after the US dollar rose above 163 yen and approached levels not seen for roughly four decades. By buying yen and selling dollars, they attempted to stop further weakness in the Japanese currency, after which the dollar fell back towards the area of approximately 155 to 157 yen.

The market is now watching whether USD/JPY will return towards 160. That level has not been officially declared a line that Japan must defend, but in recent years it has repeatedly become a point at which warnings from Japanese authorities, speculation about intervention and actual yen purchases intensified.

The USD/JPY exchange rate shows how many yen are needed to buy one US dollar. When the value rises, for example from 150 to 160, the yen is weakening. When it falls from 163 to 156, the yen is strengthening.

## What Did Japan and the United States Actually Do?

The Japanese government bought yen and sold foreign currency reserves, primarily US dollars, to increase demand for its own currency. This time, the intervention was carried out in coordination with the United States, sending a much stronger message to the market than if Japan had acted alone.

In Japan, the Ministry of Finance makes the decision to intervene in the currency market, while the Bank of Japan carries out the transactions. That is why the event is often described as a Bank of Japan intervention, even though the BOJ did not independently make the political decision to buy yen.

Data on money flows through Bank of Japan accounts suggested that Japan may have spent almost $59 billion in one of its recent efforts to support the yen. Later figures also pointed to a possible additional intervention worth up to approximately $36.6 billion.

## Why Is the 160 Level So Important?

The number 160 has no special mathematical power. It matters because it has become a psychological threshold for traders and a politically sensitive point for Japanese authorities.

Japan also intervened in 2024 after the dollar moved above 160 yen, and in the spring of 2026 it again bought its own currency after the yen weakened towards similar levels. When the market sees authorities repeatedly reacting around the same area, traders begin to assume that intervention may happen again.

However, Japan usually stresses that it is not defending a specific number, but responding to movements that are too rapid, one-sided and speculative. In theory, the dollar could therefore move above 160 without immediate intervention if the rise is gradual, while a sudden jump towards that level could trigger a response earlier.

## Why Is the Yen So Weak?

The main reason for the weak yen is the wide gap between US and Japanese interest rates. The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1%, the highest level in several decades, but it remains significantly below US rates.

Investors can therefore borrow yen more cheaply, exchange them for dollars and invest in US bonds, stocks, cryptocurrencies or other assets with potentially higher returns. This strategy is known as the yen carry trade.

The more investors sell borrowed yen and buy dollars, the greater the pressure on the Japanese currency. If the Bank of Japan does not raise rates more quickly or US rates do not fall, the same mechanism could push the dollar back towards 160 yen even after a very large intervention.

Additional pressure comes from Japan’s expansionary fiscal policy, high public debt and heavy dependence on imported energy. When oil and gas prices rise, Japan needs even more dollars to pay for imports, further increasing demand for the US currency.

## Is 160 Yen per Dollar Historically Unusual?

That depends on how far back we look. During the 1970s and the first half of the 1980s, one dollar was regularly worth more than 200 yen. The annual average in 1971 was approximately 348 yen per dollar, while in 1985 it was around 238 yen.

After the 1985 Plaza Accord, the major industrialised countries agreed to weaken the then-strong US dollar. The yen strengthened rapidly afterwards: the average exchange rate fell to approximately 168 yen in 1986, 145 yen in 1987 and 128 yen in 1988.

For that reason, 160 is not a record when the entire post-war period is considered. However, in the modern era following the yen’s sharp appreciation at the end of the 1980s, a dollar above 160 yen represents an exceptionally weak Japanese currency.

## How Did the Intervention Affect Japanese Stocks?

The sudden strengthening of the yen hit Japanese exporters the hardest. Companies such as Toyota, Honda, Suzuki, Nissan and Sony earn a large share of their revenue in dollars, euros and other foreign currencies.

When those earnings are converted into a stronger yen, their value in Japanese financial statements falls. Japanese products may also become more expensive for overseas buyers, prompting the market to reduce expectations for future profits.

Automotive, technology, robotics and industrial companies came under pressure, including Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Suzuki, Sony, Fanuc, Advantest and Kawasaki Heavy Industries. However, the movement of an individual stock does not depend only on the exchange rate. Earnings, forecasts, demand for chips and company-specific news may matter more than the yen’s movement.

## Who Can Benefit From a Stronger Yen?

A stronger yen can benefit Japanese companies that import large amounts of goods while selling most of their products to domestic customers. They must spend fewer yen for every dollar of imported goods, fuel or raw materials.

Potential beneficiaries include airlines, electricity and gas companies, food producers, major retailers and transport companies that pay for fuel and goods in dollars.

Japanese households also benefit from a stronger yen because it lowers the cost of imported food, energy and other products. Rising living costs are one of the main reasons why currency weakness became a political issue.

On the other hand, Japan becomes more expensive for tourists from Europe and the United States when the yen strengthens, while overseas travel becomes cheaper for Japanese residents.

## Why Did Bitcoin and Ethereum React?

The effect on crypto comes mainly through the yen carry trade. Some investors borrow yen at low interest rates and invest that money in riskier assets, including Bitcoin, Ethereum, technology stocks and emerging markets.

When the yen strengthens suddenly, the value of the debt investors must repay increases. They may then be forced to sell part of their assets to close positions, reduce leverage or repay loans.

That is why a large move in USD/JPY can trigger a crypto decline even when no negative event has occurred within the crypto market itself. Bitcoin is usually affected first because of its size and liquidity, while altcoins can experience larger percentage moves.

Ethereum has proved more resilient in recent weeks than many expected. Despite short-term declines caused by reduced risk appetite, ETH remained well above earlier levels around $1,500. This shows that the currency shock from Japan alone was not enough to reverse broader market flows and demand for Ethereum.

## Can the Dollar Quickly Return Above 160?

Yes. Intervention changes the balance of supply and demand in the short term, but it does not remove the fundamental causes of yen weakness.

If US interest rates remain high, the Bank of Japan continues to raise rates only slowly and energy prices rise again, investors may resume selling yen and buying dollars. In that case, USD/JPY could gradually recover part of its decline.

However, joint intervention with the United States increases the risk for traders betting against the yen. They now know that Japan is not acting alone and that the authorities could intervene again, possibly with an even larger amount.

Approaching 160 is therefore no longer a simple bet that the yen will continue weakening. A trader buying dollars at that level risks a new intervention suddenly driving the exchange rate lower within minutes or hours and causing significant losses.

## Why Might the Intervention Not Be Enough?

Currency interventions work best when they support a broader monetary policy. If Japan buys yen while simultaneously maintaining much lower interest rates than the United States, the market receives two conflicting messages.

One message says the authorities want a stronger yen. The other says it still makes sense to borrow yen and invest in higher-yielding dollar assets.

That is why analysts are now watching whether the Bank of Japan will accelerate rate increases. Without that, intervention may buy time and temporarily deter speculators, but it may not permanently change the direction of the currency.

Faster rate increases would create new problems. They would raise financing costs for the Japanese government and companies, could slow the economy and increase the risk of falling stock prices. The Bank of Japan therefore has to balance a weak yen, inflationary pressure and the consequences of tighter monetary policy.

## What Will the Market Watch Next?

The most important indicator will be the speed of the dollar’s return. If USD/JPY gradually moves back towards 158 or 159, the market will test how much renewed yen weakness Japanese authorities are willing to tolerate.

A rapid return towards 160 could trigger warnings from the Ministry of Finance, large daily market moves and renewed speculation about intervention. A move above 160 would not automatically mean another yen purchase, but it would significantly increase the likelihood of a response.

At the same time, investors will monitor Bank of Japan decisions, US interest rates, oil prices, inflation in Japan and the behaviour of large carry-trade positions.

For Japanese exporters, the key question is whether the yen remains stronger. For importers and households, it is whether imported energy and food become cheaper. For stocks and crypto, the main concern is whether yen appreciation triggers a broader unwinding of positions financed with cheap Japanese money.
