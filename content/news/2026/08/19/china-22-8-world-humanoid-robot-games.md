# China opens World Humanoid Robot Games 22.8.

Beijing will once again become a major global meeting point for humanoid robotics from August 22 to 26. The second World Humanoid Robot Games will take place at the National Speed Skating Oval, known as the “Ice Ribbon”, and the numbers show just how much the event has grown in a single year.

This year's Games will bring together a record **666 teams and 2,056 humanoid robots from 16 countries across six continents**. Over five days, they will compete across **51 disciplines and as many as 1,301 competition sessions**.

Among the countries explicitly named by organisers are the United States, Germany, Japan and Brazil. China overwhelmingly dominates participation, sending 641 teams and 1,975 robots, with 157 Chinese companies involved alongside teams from 27 universities.

The number of teams has increased by 138% compared with the inaugural event, while CGTN says the number of participating robots has quadrupled.

## Long jump, weightlifting, table tennis and tug-of-war

The programme has expanded significantly this year. Alongside running, football, combat and dance, the Games now include long jump, weightlifting, tug-of-war and table tennis. Organisers want to test not only robot speed and stability, but also strength, coordination, precision and the ability to make decisions without constant human control.

The rules have also become stricter. The time limit for the 100-metre race has been cut from three minutes to one minute, for 400 metres from 15 minutes to five, and for 1,500 metres from 40 minutes to 15.

With the exception of the 100- and 400-metre obstacle races, robots must complete the sporting events fully autonomously, without remote control.

Even the football rules have changed: a goal only counts if at least two robots from the same team touched the ball during the build-up. The aim is no longer simply to showcase one “super robot”, but also to test coordination across an entire robotic team.

## The most interesting part may not be sport at all

Of the 51 disciplines, **30 are competitive events, while 21 are based on real-world scenarios**.

Last year, there were only six such scenario-based events.

Robots will now be tested across nine types of environments: households, hotels, industrial facilities, emergency response and rescue, logistics, libraries, retail, office services and vehicle charging.

This is where things become much more interesting. A new “Dexterous Hand Challenge” includes assembling tools, weighing powder, stacking blocks, hammering nails, opening bottles, unpacking items, gripping objects with tweezers and connecting cables.

The tasks test capabilities including very close-range vision, motion planning, force control and precision.

Another event, the “Integrated Five Challenge”, requires robots to combine precise hand operations, stable object carrying and heavy lifting — while also completing a 100-metre race.

## Firefighting sounds impressive — until there is a real fire

The scenarios also include firefighting and emergency rescue. This is exactly where comparisons between controlled demonstrations and the real world need to be treated cautiously.

A robot extinguishing a controlled fire on a flat surface may look impressive. But a real fire does not provide a level track, known obstacles or ideal conditions.

We wrote about this problem only a few days ago after seeing footage of real firefighters battling flames on extremely steep streets. There, a person has to run uphill, negotiate stairs and uneven ground, drag a heavy fire hose, avoid cars, people, smoke and debris — and react almost instantly.

Humanoid robots would currently have enormous difficulty keeping up with a firefighter running uphill with a hose toward a house that is already burning. And in a fire, even a few lost seconds can be critical.

We recently looked at how AI, drones and robots might help firefighters — and where their limitations remain today. The article includes real footage of firefighters battling a blaze between homes. Could a humanoid robot realistically cope there, or would it stumble on the stairs or simply fail under the heat?

## Full autonomy counts twice as much

This year, organisers are making a particular effort to distinguish genuine autonomy from a polished remote-controlled demonstration.

In scenario-based events, a fully autonomous completion receives a coefficient of 1.0, while the score of a robot controlled remotely by a human is limited to a coefficient of 0.5.

That is an important distinction. A humanoid robot is not particularly revolutionary if a person behind a screen still has to control almost every movement. The real technological leap happens when the robot can see its environment, recognise a problem, decide what to do, correct its own mistake and continue the task without operator intervention.

Those are exactly the capabilities the Games will attempt to test under much more demanding conditions than last year.

## China wants to prove robots can actually work

The World Humanoid Robot Games come as China's robotics industry attempts to move from impressive demonstrations toward the real economy.

Reuters reports that investors and buyers are becoming less interested in whether a robot can dance, perform a backflip or win a race, and more interested in how much useful work it can perform, how much human supervision it requires and whether it can justify its price.

Humanoid robots are already being used in some limited roles, including food delivery in hotels and selected tasks on production lines, but widespread commercial adoption has not yet arrived.

One of the biggest problems remains cost. Guotai Securities estimates that an industrial humanoid robot including maintenance would need to cost around **160,000 yuan, approximately $22,000**, to become financially comparable over two years with a worker earning 80,000 yuan per year.

In reality, such robots often cost between **300,000 and 500,000 yuan** today — roughly **$42,000 to $70,000**.

So the question is no longer simply: “Can the robot do this?”

The real question is: **can it do it well enough, for long enough and cheaply enough that someone would actually want to employ it instead of a human?**

## Unitree is facing another major test right now

The timing of the Games is particularly interesting because of Unitree, one of China's largest humanoid robot companies. The company made its debut on Shanghai's STAR Market on August 19, only days before the World Humanoid Robot Games begin.

Interest was extreme. The retail portion of the IPO was around 8,000 times oversubscribed, while Reuters Breakingviews reported that the share price rose around 630% above its IPO price of 150.80 yuan on its first day of trading, with market capitalisation at one point reaching around $67 billion.

## The Robot Games are really a huge public laboratory

Running, football, dance and robot combat will almost certainly produce the most spectacular footage.

But the more important moments may be when a robot tries to connect a cable and misses the socket, drops an object, fails to open a bottle or has to recover from a mistake on its own.

Those are the problems that determine whether a robot can one day actually work alongside people. This year's competitions require longer, continuous execution of tasks in complex environments. A robot must recognise unfamiliar objects, manipulate them correctly multiple times, recover from errors and finish the job without an engineer repositioning it every few minutes.

That is a much harder test than a few seconds of perfectly rehearsed choreography.

The World Humanoid Robot Games may therefore be at their most valuable precisely when the robots do not look perfect. A fall or mistake reveals where the technology is not ready yet.

And the progress from one year to the next shows how quickly that boundary is moving.

## Our view

* The second World Humanoid Robot Games are significantly larger than the inaugural edition: 666 teams, 2,056 robots, 51 disciplines and 1,301 competition sessions.
* The sporting spectacle remains important, but 21 disciplines now test scenarios that increasingly resemble real jobs.
* Full autonomy is being rewarded much more heavily this year. That is a far more meaningful measure of progress than a robot being controlled by a human behind the scenes.
* Firefighting is an excellent example of the difference between a controlled test and reality. A flat arena is not the same as a steep street, stairs, smoke and a burning house where every second matters.
* The enormous investor interest in Unitree shows how financially important humanoid robotics has become. Now the technology has to prove it can move from spectacular demonstrations to useful work.
* The most interesting moments may not be robot records, but the mistakes that reveal how much work remains before humanoid robots truly become everyday workers.
