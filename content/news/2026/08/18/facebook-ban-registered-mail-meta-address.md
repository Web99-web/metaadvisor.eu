# Banned From Facebook? Maybe Registered Mail Is All That's Left.

Facebook can suspend a user account almost instantly. A generic message appears on the screen alleging a rules violation, the user tries to appeal, and then the system logs them out of an account they may have used for ten, fifteen or twenty years. But that is where another part of the story begins — one that sounds almost unbelievable for one of the world's largest technology companies: once you can no longer get into Facebook, you may also lose normal access to the very channels through which you are supposed to tell Facebook that it may have made a mistake.

You can no longer log in normally. Some support and troubleshooting tools sit precisely behind the login you no longer have access to. Meta is increasingly introducing AI support, but even an AI assistant is not much help to a user who has been locked out of the very system through which that assistant is supposed to be reached. Facebook does not provide a public customer-service phone number that a disabled user can simply call to explain the problem, nor is there a universal public email address for these cases.

But there is — **a postal address**.

**It is hard to imagine a better example of Big Tech irony — Facebook has AI. Banned users have a postal tracking number.**

Yes. It is 2026. Meta is developing artificial intelligence and automating vast parts of moderation and customer support, yet one of the few direct records left to a locked-out user may consist of paper, an envelope, a postage stamp and registered mail.

## Facebook managed to block even India's Prime Minister

How much confidence should we place in a moderation system that can decide within seconds who stays on the platform and who disappears from it? That question became even more interesting after Facebook temporarily restricted a post by Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi. This was not an anonymous account with a handful of friends. It was the Prime Minister of the world's most populous country.

The Indian government reacted and called Meta representatives in to explain what had happened. Meta later acknowledged that Modi's post had been restricted unintentionally because of an “operational error”. The content was restored, and Meta's Chief Global Affairs Officer Joel Kaplan apologized to an Indian minister on behalf of the company.

We do not know whether AI made the specific decision in Modi's case, and that should not be claimed without evidence. But the incident comes at a time when moderation and customer support are becoming increasingly automated, making one obvious question difficult to avoid: if Facebook can mistakenly restrict even a post by India's Prime Minister, how many ordinary people may have ended up on the wrong side of the same or a similar system without anyone ever calling Meta to account on their behalf?

## An apology for the Prime Minister. For an ordinary user — THE END!

This is where the difference between a mistake affecting a politically powerful person and one affecting an ordinary user becomes striking. When Modi's post was restricted, the Indian government reacted. Meta had to explain what happened, one of its most senior executives apologized to a minister, and the content was restored.

When something similar happens to an ordinary person, there is no government standing behind them. No minister calling Meta to account. No meeting with company representatives, no media inquiries from around the world and no senior Meta executive issuing a public apology.

Often, there is only a generic message on the screen: the account has been suspended or disabled, the appeal has failed, and the user has been logged out. **THE END.**

And what disappears is not necessarily just a Facebook profile. Over the past two decades, people have moved enormous parts of their private and social lives online and, particularly since the COVID pandemic, much of their business lives as well. One disabled login can mean losing access to Messenger and years of private conversations, photos, contacts, Facebook Pages, Groups built over years, Business Suite, advertising accounts and business relationships.

Facebook may meanwhile show the user **a limited period of around 180 days during which they can still try to challenge the suspension before the account becomes permanently disabled. And this is where the whole situation becomes almost Kafkaesque: you have a deadline to save the account, but the door you are supposed to walk through to save it no longer exists. In fact — it has been cemented shut.**

## Meta disables billions of accounts a year?? Really??

Perhaps the whole issue could be dismissed as a handful of unfortunate mistakes if we did not know how enormous the system actually is. **On June 5, 2026, the Oversight Board warned that Meta disables accounts on a scale reaching billions of accounts every year.** The Board also clearly emphasizes that a large proportion of those removals are justified because of genuine rule violations, fraud, spam and other abuse.

But the more important part of the warning comes next. **The Oversight Board says many account disabling decisions are unexplained or inadequately explained to users, and that people who lose their accounts may find appeal mechanisms unclear, impractical or unavailable. The Board also specifically warned that users should continue to have access to information about their account status, previous violations and pending appeals even after an account has been disabled.**

That is almost a precise description of the problem we are discussing: Facebook removes you from your account, and then that very loss of access can make it harder to prove that Facebook may have made a mistake because you no longer have normal access to customer support.

## How many people were “accidentally” banned?

For years, Reddit, X, LinkedIn, Mastodon and other platforms have been filled with strikingly similar stories from users. A profile exists for ten, fifteen or twenty years and then one day it is simply gone. The user receives a generic statement saying they violated a rule, tries to find out what exactly they supposedly did, goes through an appeal process that may be extremely short and automated, and eventually reaches a screen that effectively says the decision has been made and there is no normal further review.

Of course, many Facebook accounts should be removed. The platform has to remove fake accounts, fraud, spam, serious abuse and criminal content. No reasonable person is arguing for Facebook without moderation.

The problem begins when a system operating at an enormous scale makes mistakes. If accounts are disabled in the billions every year, even a very low error rate can mathematically translate into a huge number of real people. And for an individual who loses fifteen or twenty years of digital life, it is not particularly comforting to hear that the system “works most of the time”.

And after a case in which Facebook managed to mistakenly restrict even a post by India's Prime Minister, the question is no longer simply whether the system can make mistakes. **The question is how many ordinary users have been “accidentally” banned in the same way — except there is no government behind them to call Meta to account, no minister for Meta to apologize to, and no media covering what happened. Because by removing them from the platform in a matter of seconds, Facebook did not merely block them — it simultaneously silenced them.**

## And now — off to the post office.....

And so we arrive at perhaps the most bizarre suggestion one can make in 2026 to someone having a problem with one of the world's largest technology companies: **if you can no longer reach Meta online, you can try sending it an actual paper letter.**

Not because there is any guarantee Meta will answer. There isn't. And not because registered mail will magically restore a disabled Facebook profile. It may not.

The reason is much simpler: **registered mail can leave a record.**

If a Facebook account matters to someone, an ordinary untracked letter offers little proof of what happened after it was posted. Registered mail with tracking and, where the postal service offers it, proof of delivery can document when the item was sent and when it was delivered. A copy of the letter, the postal receipt and the tracking number can also be retained. Once tracking shows delivery, that status can be saved as a screenshot or PDF.

Then there is no longer only the statement: “I tried to contact Meta.” There is a documented trail showing when the letter was sent, what was sent and when the shipment reached Meta.

**If a case later ends up before a regulator, an out-of-court dispute-resolution body, a lawyer or a court, that may become an important distinction.**

## The letter still has to be linked to an account — what would you write?

Naturally, what someone chooses to tell Meta depends on their individual case. One person may have lost only a personal profile, another may have lost years of Messenger communications, Facebook Pages or Groups as well, while someone else may have had Business Suite, advertising and other business resources connected through the same account. Each user knows best what matters in their particular situation and what they want to explain or request from Meta.

**However, if a physical letter is being sent to a company managing billions of user accounts, it needs to contain enough information for Meta to connect it to a specific user and event in the first place. That may include information associated with the account, the email address, profile name or URL, the date of disabling, the message shown by Facebook, information about a previous appeal or other details the user considers important for understanding the case.**

It may be especially relevant if the disabling did not affect only a personal profile but also removed access to Messenger, Pages, Groups or business resources, or if Facebook is displaying a particular deadline after which the account or associated data may be permanently lost.

**The central point remains the same: a user who believes the decision is wrong is trying to explain the case to Meta precisely because, after being disabled, they no longer have normal access to the customer-support channels through which they would otherwise do so online.**

What someone specifically asks for — an additional explanation, human review of the decision, restoration of the account, preservation of data or something else — depends on the circumstances of each individual case.

Of course, a physical letter is not the place to send passwords, payment-card details or other sensitive security information that Facebook and Meta do not need in order to identify an account.

Sending a letter to either of these addresses is not an official promise of individual customer support and does not guarantee that an account will be restored. That distinction matters. The point of registered mail is not to promise an outcome nobody can guarantee, but to leave a record that a user attempted to notify the company directly about the case before any relevant deadlines expired.

## Facebook has artificial intelligence. You have a postal tracking number.

Perhaps nothing better illustrates the current absurdity of customer support. Meta is building some of the world's most advanced AI systems. Facebook can automatically analyze unimaginable volumes of content and user accounts. Its systems can flag content, restrict it, remove it and suspend a user within moments.

But when the user wants to say, “Wait, maybe you made a mistake,” all that technological magic can suddenly disappear.

Now you need a printer, paper, an envelope, a postage stamp, registered mail and a tracking number.

For Narendra Modi, the Indian government reacted. Meta had to answer to Indian institutions, explain the error and apologize. For an ordinary user with a twenty-year-old profile, there may be no minister, no meeting with Meta, no headlines and no public apology. There may not even be a human being to whom they can explain what happened.

But there is an address. And there is the post office.

At one point, we also saw **a petition with more than 64,000 signatures from people who believed their Facebook accounts had been disabled by mistake and who said they had been unable to reach real human customer support**. It would therefore be extremely interesting to see what would happen if the volume of physical registered letters arriving at Meta's offices began to reflect the real number of affected users — and what would happen if even some of those people decided to move their problem out of the digital world and into the physical one by sending Meta a registered letter.

Digital complaints can disappear somewhere inside an automated system. **Sixty-four thousand physical envelopes, however, have to arrive somewhere. Someone has to receive them.**

And perhaps that is the ultimate irony of the entire story: **to try to speak in 2026 with a company building the future of artificial intelligence, you may first have to go — to the post office.**

## Our take

* The problem is no longer simply whether automated moderation can make mistakes, but what happens to a user after a mistake when both login access and normal support channels are simultaneously taken away.
* The case involving India's Prime Minister shows how quickly an incorrect moderation decision can be corrected when a government, institutions and media attention stand behind the affected person. Ordinary users generally do not have that option.
* The Oversight Board's figure of billions of disabled accounts each year shows why even a relatively small error rate could affect a very large number of real people.
* A registered letter is not a magic solution and does not guarantee account restoration, but it can give a user something an automated system often does not — a record of what was sent, when it was sent and when the company received it.
* Perhaps the greatest irony of the entire system is that a user whose access to a digital platform can disappear in seconds may ultimately have to rely on one of the oldest forms of communication: a letter on paper.
