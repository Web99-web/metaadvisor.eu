# Tech stocks fall as bond yields rise

US technology stocks fell on August 18, with semiconductor companies coming under particularly strong pressure. At the same time, long-term government bond yields in the United States, Japan and parts of Europe reached levels not seen in years or even decades.

The S&P 500 closed 0.67% lower, the Nasdaq Composite lost 1.31%, while the Dow Jones declined 0.22%. The drop was significantly stronger in the semiconductor sector.

## Chip stocks take the biggest hit again

The decline was not evenly spread across the market. Technology led the losses, with semiconductor manufacturers and other companies that had previously benefited from the artificial intelligence boom among the hardest hit.

Micron lost around 7.6% during the session, AMD more than 5%, Nvidia around 2% and Broadcom roughly 3%. Western Digital and Sandisk came under even stronger pressure. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell more than 5%.

That distinction matters. The S&P 500 lost less than 1% and the Dow just over 0.2%, while losses in technology and semiconductor stocks were considerably larger. So far, this is not an evenly distributed collapse across the entire stock market, but a much sharper selloff in a segment that has been driven by very high expectations and valuations in recent months.

## US 30-year Treasury yield highest since 2007

One of the key reasons for the pressure can be found in the bond market. The yield on the 30-year US Treasury rose to around 5.33%, its highest level since 2007. The 10-year Treasury yield was around 4.7%.

When yields on relatively safer government bonds rise, investors can demand higher expected returns from riskier assets as well. Technology and other growth stocks are particularly sensitive because much of their high valuation depends on earnings expected further into the future.

## The problem is not limited to the US

Rising yields are also visible outside the United States. Japan's 10-year government bond yield reached 2.945% during the session, its highest level since September 1996.

Reuters also reported multi-year or multi-decade highs in long-term borrowing costs across other major markets, including Germany.

Common concerns include inflation, high government debt levels and large financing needs.

## Higher oil prices add to inflation fears

Additional pressure is coming from rising oil prices linked to tensions between the United States and Iran and weaker expectations for political de-escalation.

More expensive energy can push inflation higher again. If inflation remains elevated, central banks have less room to cut interest rates, which can in turn help keep government bond yields high.

## The AI boom has another side

Reuters points to another factor receiving increasing attention from markets: massive investment in AI infrastructure is increasing the capital needs of major technology companies.

Governments are already issuing large amounts of debt, while companies are simultaneously investing enormous sums in data centres and other artificial intelligence infrastructure. Greater competition for capital can add further pressure to financing costs.

That creates an interesting situation: the companies that benefited most from the AI-driven stock market boom are also among those most sensitive to rising capital costs.

## Is this the beginning of a bigger decline?

So far, there is not enough evidence to describe today's move as a broad stock market crash. The S&P 500 lost less than 1%, while the much larger declines were concentrated in the Nasdaq and particularly in semiconductor stocks.

The more important question is whether high bond yields persist and whether selling in technology stocks spreads to the rest of the market.

If long-term US, Japanese and European government bond yields continue to rise, investors may increasingly reconsider how much they are willing to pay for high valuations in technology and AI companies.

## Our view

* Today's decline is not yet a broad market crash, but pressure on technology and semiconductor stocks is significantly stronger than the moves in the major indexes.
* Rising yields are not limited to the United States. Simultaneous moves in the US, Japan and Europe point to a broader issue in global bond markets.
* Higher oil prices are increasing inflation risks again, raising the possibility that interest rates may remain elevated for longer than many investors would prefer.
* The key question now is whether yields remain high and whether selling in chip and AI stocks spreads to the broader market.
