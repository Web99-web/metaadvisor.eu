# Bank of Italy tested stablecoins: costs 0.3–9%

Stablecoins are often presented as a faster and cheaper way to send money across borders. Blockchains operate 24 hours a day, transaction fees can be very low, and USDC can move between wallets almost instantly. But the Bank of Italy decided to test something more important than the cost of the blockchain transaction itself: how much does the entire journey of the money from sender to recipient actually cost?

Researchers therefore followed the transfer from beginning to end — from **fiat, meaning ordinary money such as euros, dollars or another national currency held as cash or in a bank account**, through the purchase of USDC and its transfer over the blockchain, all the way to the sale of the stablecoin and the final payout to the recipient in local currency.

The result shows why the simple claim that “stablecoins are cheap to send” does not tell the whole story. Total costs in the tested corridors ranged from just 0.3% to nearly 9%, and most of that difference did not come from the blockchain. The main costs came from buying and selling stablecoins, exchange-rate differences, currency conversion and local payment infrastructure.

## The Bank of Italy actually sent 200 USDC

The study was not simply a theoretical comparison of published fees. Researchers used a “mystery shopping” approach, behaving like ordinary users and carrying out real transactions to measure how much the entire process actually cost and how long it took.

Transfers of 200 USDC were tested across ten bidirectional corridors connecting Italy with Brazil, Argentina, Japan, the United Arab Emirates and South Africa. USDC is a stablecoin pegged to the US dollar, designed to maintain a value of approximately one dollar.

Importantly, researchers did not measure only the moment when USDC moved from one blockchain wallet to another. They followed the entire chain: fiat money at the beginning, the purchase of USDC, the stablecoin transfer, its sale at the other end and the final payout in a currency the recipient could actually use.

## Blockchain fees were the smallest problem

The results showed that blockchain transaction fees made up only a small share of the total cost. Much larger amounts were lost when entering and exiting the crypto system — through so-called on-ramp and off-ramp processes — as well as through exchange rates and currency conversion.

An on-ramp is the point at which a user converts ordinary money, such as euros, into USDC. An off-ramp is the reverse process, when the recipient sells USDC and converts it back into euros, reais, pesos, yen, dirhams, rand or another local currency. In many cases, these steps were considerably more expensive than actually sending the token over the blockchain.

For that reason, the Bank of Italy concluded that stablecoin solutions do not have a **systematic** cost advantage over traditional methods. That does not mean they are always more expensive. It means the result depends heavily on the country, service provider, exchange rate, deposit and withdrawal method, and the infrastructure available at both ends of the transfer.

## Stablecoins were still cheaper in most corridors

The range from 0.3% to nearly 9% shows how dramatically the outcome can vary from one corridor to another. A transfer that looks exceptionally cheap on one route can cost almost a tenth of the amount sent on another.

However, the study does not show that stablecoin remittances are generally too expensive. Compared with the global average cost of traditional international remittances used as a benchmark, stablecoin transfers were cheaper in most of the corridors examined.

The picture changes when stablecoins are compared with more efficient fintech services such as Wise, an international platform for money transfers and currency conversion. According to the study, the stablecoin route was cheaper than Wise in only three of the seven corridors where a direct comparison was possible.

That is an important distinction. Stablecoins can have a significant advantage where traditional international remittances are expensive, but they are not automatically cheaper than the most efficient digital payment services already available.

## Speed did not depend only on the blockchain

The same was true for speed. A blockchain transaction itself can be very fast, but the researchers wanted to know when the recipient would actually receive money they could use.

Where efficient instant-payment systems were available at both ends, the entire process could be completed in less than 20 minutes. Where users had to wait for traditional bank transfers, the process took one to two business days.

In other words, the same USDC and the same blockchain technology can produce very different results depending on the infrastructure of the country where the user enters or exits the system. The blockchain can finish its part almost instantly, while the user may then spend hours or days waiting for the traditional financial system.

## The biggest problem is at the entry and exit points

This may be the most interesting result of the entire experiment. A stablecoin can cross a blockchain quickly and at very low cost, but first someone has to convert ordinary money into the stablecoin, and afterwards someone else has to convert that stablecoin back into ordinary money.

At those points, exchanges and other service providers reappear, along with spreads between buying and selling prices, deposit and withdrawal fees, conversions between national currencies and limitations in local banking systems.

Blockchain can therefore remove or shorten part of the intermediary chain in the middle of an international transfer, but it has not removed the traditional financial system at either end. According to the Bank of Italy’s findings, that is precisely where most of the cost was created.

## What if the recipient does not convert USDC back into fiat?

There is an important limitation to the study here. The Bank of Italy experiment examined stablecoins as a way of handling traditional international remittances: ordinary money enters at one end, USDC acts as the transfer layer, and ordinary money comes out at the other.

If the recipient does not want to convert USDC into fiat but instead wants to hold it, send it elsewhere or use it directly for payment, the off-ramp cost disappears. In that situation, the total economics can look very different from those measured in the traditional remittance model.

The study therefore does not show that sending USDC from one wallet to another is expensive. Quite the opposite — the small share of blockchain fees in the total cost shows that an on-chain transfer can be very cheap. The problem arises when the digital dollar has to pass through traditional financial channels at the beginning and end of the transaction.

## Stablecoins solve the middle, but not necessarily the endpoints

A traditional international transfer can involve multiple banks, correspondent accounts, different currencies, time zones, regulatory checks and local payment systems. A stablecoin can significantly simplify the middle of that chain because value moves over a blockchain without every step having to pass through the traditional banking system.

But if the user must buy the stablecoin with euros at the beginning and the recipient must sell it for local currency at the end, both endpoints still depend on infrastructure that has little to do with the speed of the blockchain.

That is why a country with a strong instant-payment system can support a very fast stablecoin transfer, while the same technology in another corridor can result in a process that takes a day or two. The Bank of Italy therefore stresses that improving domestic payment infrastructure may be just as important as developing the blockchain system itself.

## A cheaper blockchain alone will not solve the problem

The findings have another interesting implication. If blockchain fees make up only a small part of the total cost, then a blockchain that is ten times cheaper will not automatically make the entire international transfer ten times cheaper.

If a user loses several percent through spreads, stablecoin purchases, currency conversion or withdrawals, the difference between a blockchain fee of a few cents and a fee of one cent is barely visible in the final price.

That means future competition between stablecoin solutions is unlikely to focus only on which blockchain can offer the cheapest transaction. It will be just as important to make the journey from euros into a stablecoin — and from a stablecoin back into the money the recipient uses every day — simple and inexpensive.

## This is not an argument against stablecoins

It would be easy to turn the findings into a headline claiming that stablecoins are “no better than banks,” but that would not reflect what the research actually found. In some corridors, costs were very low, and stablecoin transfers were cheaper than the global average for traditional remittances in most of the routes examined.

The Bank of Italy also does not conclude that stablecoins should be dismissed. Its officials have instead stressed that evidence about their efficiency remains mixed and that, with appropriate regulation, stablecoins may have a useful role in certain cross-border payment corridors.

Above all, the study shows that it is not enough to look at a blockchain explorer, see a transaction fee of a few cents and conclude that this is the cost of an international transfer. For the actual user, what matters is how much money they had at the beginning and how much the recipient ultimately received.

## Our view

- The most interesting result of the Bank of Italy study may not be whether stablecoins beat Wise or traditional remittances, but **where the money actually disappears**.
- Blockchain itself was one of the smallest components of the total cost. The main bottleneck appeared when ordinary money was converted into USDC and when USDC was converted back into ordinary money.
- The cost range from 0.3% to nearly 9% is therefore not a final verdict on how much stablecoin remittances may cost in the future.
- Cheaper on-ramps and off-ramps, greater competition between providers, better liquidity and wider availability of instant payments could significantly reduce total costs.
- If stablecoins can one day be widely used without constantly having to convert them back into fiat, one of the most expensive parts of today’s process could simply disappear.
- The study therefore reveals an interesting paradox of modern digital payments: **money can cross a blockchain almost instantly and very cheaply, while the most expensive part of its journey may still be entering the traditional financial system — and exiting it again.**
