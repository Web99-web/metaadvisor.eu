# Facebook paid for content linked to neo-Nazism?

Meta has spent years saying it invests billions in safety, content moderation and systems designed to detect hate speech. A new investigation now raises an uncomfortable question for the company: how were profiles and pages linked to white-supremacist and neo-Nazi content able not only to remain on Facebook, but also to earn money through its monetization program?

Futurism published a story on August 8 under the provocative headline “Meta Caught Paying Nazis to Post on Facebook.” The headline makes it sound as though Meta directly hired extremists to produce such content. According to the information published, however, the situation was somewhat different — and potentially just as problematic for Facebook.

Some accounts linked to far-right content were part of Facebook Content Monetization, a program through which Meta pays creators based on how their content performs. That means Meta was not simply allowing the content to remain on the platform. Some of its creators were also receiving money through Facebook’s own monetization system.

## Facebook Content Monetization is invite-only

That detail is exactly what makes this story especially important. Facebook Content Monetization is not a program that any user can simply join. **Participation is available only by invitation**, and Meta decides which creators can take part and earn money from Reels, videos, photos and text posts.

Meta has said it paid creators almost $3 billion during 2025. Facebook monetization has therefore become a significant source of income for some creators, but also a system in which Meta decides who gets the opportunity to earn in the first place.

That is why this case cannot simply be explained as problematic content slipping past moderation among billions of posts. If an account linked to extremist content could receive an invitation to the official monetization program, continue posting and receive payouts, the question is no longer only why the content was not removed. The question is **how such an account gained access to a program that users cannot simply sign up for themselves**.

## Who was receiving money from Facebook?

According to the investigation reported by Futurism, Hugo Lennon was among the people receiving payments. Lennon is an Australian far-right anti-immigration influencer with a history of promoting far-right and racist content. According to the published claims, he had been receiving Facebook payments since September 2025.

The investigation also mentions a Facebook page linked to the Australian outlet The Noticer. ABC News Australia previously reported that The Noticer regularly publishes material promoting white-supremacist and neo-Nazi ideologies and has published work by prominent Australian neo-Nazis. According to the new investigation, the outlet’s Facebook page had been part of Facebook’s monetization program since November 2025.

The central issue is not only the ideology of the people behind those accounts. According to the investigation, some of the published material appeared to violate Facebook’s own hate-speech rules. Even so, the accounts did not simply remain available. They were generating money, and the payouts were coming through Meta’s own system.

## While some earn money, ordinary users are permanently removed from Facebook

The contrast with Facebook moderation is difficult to ignore. While accounts linked to extremist content were able to take part in the monetization program and receive money, ordinary users have spent years reporting the opposite experience — sudden suspensions or permanent account removals, often without a clear explanation of what they were actually accused of doing.

Those affected are not only anonymous or newly created profiles. They include long-term users who had been on Facebook for ten, fifteen or twenty years — teachers, students, professional photographers, social workers, small-business owners and others for whom Facebook had become part of their personal or professional lives.

Some of those users still do not know which specific post, photo or activity triggered their permanent removal. Instead of receiving a clear explanation, they may get only a generic notice about a policy violation, an automated appeal process, or simply a message saying that the decision is final.

The problem of automated decisions became serious enough to draw the attention of the Meta Oversight Board. Meta itself has acknowledged that its moderation systems made too many mistakes and removed content that should not have been taken down.

In that context, the monetization of extremist-linked content takes on an entirely different dimension. The same ecosystem that can permanently remove a long-term user from the platform — sometimes without clearly explaining what they supposedly did — apparently did not prevent accounts linked to extremist content from entering a program through which Meta paid them money.

## A 15 or 20 year old profile appears to count for very little

Such decisions are even harder to explain when they involve users who have been on Facebook almost since the platform’s early years. A long history of use, many years without serious violations, a real identity and thousands of normal interactions do not necessarily seem to carry much weight when an automated system decides that an account should be removed.

That raises the question of why a Facebook profile that has existed for fifteen or twenty years receives no meaningful reputation credit. Banks, insurers, marketplaces and almost every other risk-assessment system take a user’s history into account. On a social network, however, a single automated decision can effectively wipe out decades of legitimate use.

## Meta distinguishes offensive content from real-world danger

Meta told journalists there is a difference between content that is offensive and content that poses a genuine danger or could lead to violence outside the internet. That distinction is reasonable in principle. A platform should not remove every piece of content simply because it is controversial, politically extreme or offensive to someone.

But this is where one of the biggest contradictions in the entire story appears. **While Meta explains why certain extremist content may not be dangerous enough to remove, its automated moderation systems have at the same time permanently removed ordinary users whose content posed no comparable real-world danger — and some of those users still do not know what exactly led to their ban.**

There is also another crucial distinction: **allowing content is not the same as financially rewarding it.** Even if Meta decides that a particular post does not cross the threshold for removal from Facebook, it does not follow that its creator should gain access to an invite-only program through which the company pays them money.

## How did such an account pass monetization checks?

That is the question Meta should answer much more concretely. If Content Monetization is available only by invitation and there are rules governing who is allowed to earn money, then there must also be a system for checking whether creators meet those requirements.

How did accounts linked to extremist content pass those checks? Were the checks automated? Was an account’s history reviewed before monetization was approved? Are creators reviewed again after they enter the program? And what happens to money earned from content that is later found to violate platform rules?

Those questions go beyond one profile or one Facebook page. They concern the workings of an entire system through which one of the world’s largest social networks decides who it will pay.

## Monetization changes the platform’s responsibility

Whether a particular piece of content should remain on Facebook is one question. Whether Facebook should pay its creator for it is another.

A platform can adopt a broad position on freedom of expression and decide that controversial or offensive content should not automatically be removed. But monetization is not a free-speech right. It is a business program controlled by Meta, with rules set by Meta and participation decided by Meta.

Once Meta enables monetization for an account, the company is no longer merely the technical intermediary through which content is published. It enters into a financial relationship with the creator and pays money through its own system based on the reach and engagement that content generates.

That is why the case of accounts linked to neo-Nazi and white-supremacist content raises a broader question about whether Meta’s monetization and moderation systems are sufficiently connected to one another.

## Our view

The easiest way to reduce this story would be to say “Meta pays Nazis.” It would be dramatic, but it would not describe what we currently know with enough precision.

What matters more is that Facebook’s monetization system enabled payouts to accounts linked to content promoting far-right, white-supremacist or neo-Nazi ideas. At the same time, the other side of Meta’s system — automated moderation — has shown for years that it can be extremely strict toward content that poses no comparable real-world danger.

Meta is not simply a platform deciding what may remain published and what may not. It simultaneously decides which content to distribute, which content to restrict, which users to punish and which creators to pay.

A user can lose a fifteen- or twenty-year-old account because of a mistaken automated decision and have no meaningful way to get that decision reviewed by a human, while an account linked to extremist content can remain on the platform and at the same time participate in a monetization program.

This is no longer only a question of free speech, and it is not only a question of moderation. **It is a question of accountability for a system that decides, at the same time, whom Facebook permanently removes — and whom Facebook pays.**
