# Uber Eats and Coco launch robot delivery in Helsinki: food can now arrive by human or robot

Uber Eats has started delivering food in Helsinki with autonomous robots from US company Coco Robotics, making Finland the first market in the European Union where Uber Eats uses robots for delivery directly through its own app. Customers whose orders are assigned to a robot can track its movement through the Uber Eats app in much the same way they currently track a human courier.

At first glance, this looks like another interesting technology project. Over the longer term, however, the more important question is what happens to platform work when an algorithm no longer only decides which courier gets an order, but when some orders can be completed without a human courier at all. Uber currently stresses that the robots in Helsinki will operate alongside the existing courier network rather than replace it, but the fact that people and autonomous vehicles are now part of the same delivery system already shows where the industry is heading.

## Uber Eats arrived in Finland in May, robots followed in August

The speed of expansion is particularly striking. Uber Eats launched in Finland only in May 2026, initially in Helsinki, Espoo and Tampere. Just three months later, the company announced expansion into eight more cities — Vantaa, Kuopio, Turku, Oulu, Joensuu, Jyväskylä, Lahti and Lappeenranta — bringing its presence to a total of 11 Finnish cities and areas. At the same time, robot delivery was introduced in central Helsinki.

Finland is not the first place where delivery robots operate on public streets, nor are these the first such robots in the country. The development is more precisely defined as follows: **Finland is the first EU market where Uber Eats enables robot delivery through its own platform.**

Delivery robots have been operating in Finland for longer, and Coco did not arrive there with Uber. Wolt began piloting Coco robots in Helsinki in autumn 2024, initially for deliveries from Wolt Market, and the project was later expanded to restaurant food delivery.

## Coco is not a Finnish company

Although Coco robots are now appearing on the streets of Helsinki and other Finnish cities, **Coco Robotics is not a Finnish company**. It is a US startup founded in Los Angeles in 2020 that develops autonomous robots primarily for so-called last-mile logistics — short urban deliveries of food, groceries and smaller goods.

According to the company, Coco has already completed more than **500,000 deliveries**, built more than 1,000 robots and works with thousands of restaurants and merchants. The company is also backed by significant investment capital, meaning robot delivery is no longer simply a small experiment involving a handful of restaurants, but an attempt to build a scalable urban logistics network.

## How autonomous are these robots really?

The latest generation of Coco robots uses cameras, LiDAR, GPS and AI systems to understand its surroundings. The robot can move independently along sidewalks and other permitted areas, collect an order from a restaurant and bring it to the customer, who then unlocks the storage compartment through the app.

That does not mean humans have been completely removed from the process. The robots are remotely monitored while operating, and a human teleoperator can intervene when the robot encounters a situation it cannot resolve safely enough on its own — for example a blocked sidewalk, roadworks, an unusual traffic situation or another obstacle. Each such intervention can also become a new data point from which the system learns how to handle similar situations more independently in the future.

Automation therefore does not yet remove people from the entire system, but changes their role. Instead of one person physically carrying out one delivery, people can supervise several robots, maintain them, charge them, service them and intervene when the automated system requires help.

## What happens to the couriers?

Uber currently states explicitly that Coco robots will work alongside the existing courier network, and there is no basis for claiming that the company now intends to eliminate human delivery in Helsinki completely. However, the economics of such a system are difficult to ignore.

Short urban deliveries in densely populated areas are exactly the type of work that a small electric robot can relatively easily take over. A robot does not need to start a new shift, does not receive payment for each individual delivery, and one remote operations team could potentially supervise several autonomous vehicles at the same time.

That does not mean robots will soon be able to handle every delivery. Stairs, buildings without simple access, poorly cleared sidewalks, longer distances, complex intersections, difficult weather conditions and large or sensitive packages still give humans a clear advantage. But the more short and simple orders robots can take over, the fewer such orders remain available to human couriers.

## Have Coco robots already gone through a Finnish winter?

Uber Eats' new robot delivery service needs to be separated from Coco's previous experience in Finland. Coco has been present in the country for longer through its partnership with Wolt, and its robots were tested and used there before the Uber partnership. **However, the new Uber Eats robot service was launched only at the end of August 2026 and has not yet gone through its own Finnish winter in regular commercial operation.**

The winter of 2026/27 will therefore provide the first real test of how the new Uber implementation performs through months of snow, ice, slush, low temperatures and less reliably cleared sidewalks. That is not a minor challenge, because experience with other delivery robot manufacturers has shown that winter conditions can significantly complicate their movement.

Coco has experience operating in colder conditions and trains its AI systems using data from different weather environments. Still, everyday commercial delivery throughout an entire Finnish winter is a much more demanding test than limited pilot projects or individual technical trials.

## From an algorithm choosing a courier to an algorithm choosing a robot

The shift is particularly interesting because platforms such as Uber have spent years using algorithms to distribute jobs, evaluate performance and manage access to the app. Metaadvisor.eu recently covered a case in which Uber was fined **€825 million**, with questions surrounding algorithmic decisions and people's access to work through the platform.

Robot delivery adds a new layer to that relationship. The algorithm no longer has to decide only which worker gets the job. The system can now redirect some orders to an entirely different form of execution — a robot.

For customers, that could mean cheaper, more predictable and potentially more energy-efficient delivery. For the platform, it creates the possibility of increasing capacity without proportionally increasing the number of couriers. For people who earn income from delivery work, however, it raises a much more uncomfortable question: **how many jobs must automation take over before the claim that robots merely complement the existing network begins to mean something very different?**

## Uber is not automating only delivery

The company's broader direction shows that Coco is not an isolated project. Uber is directing increasing amounts of capital and strategic attention toward autonomous mobility, including robotaxis and partnerships with manufacturers of autonomous vehicles.

In that context, robot food delivery becomes part of a much larger shift. Uber's long-term platform may no longer simply connect a user with a driver or courier, but with the best available form of transport or delivery — a human, a driverless car, a small sidewalk robot or, in other projects, potentially even a drone.

That changes the function of the algorithm itself. Today's platform mostly decides which person receives a job. The platform of the future may first decide **whether a person is needed for that job at all.**

## Our take
* **Uber's robots in Helsinki are still a limited project, and there is no basis for claiming that the company is about to replace human couriers immediately.** However, the technical ability to assign some deliveries to a machine instead of a person is now built directly into the Uber Eats system.
* **Coco Robotics is no longer a small experimental startup.** The company has more than half a million completed deliveries behind it, significant investment capital, increasingly autonomous robots and partnerships with major delivery platforms.
* **Finland is a particularly interesting European test market.** A highly developed digital market and compact cities suit robot delivery, while snow, ice, slush and low temperatures create a much harder technology test than favourable conditions in many US cities.
* **The most important question is no longer whether robots can deliver food — they already can.** The question is how quickly their cost and reliability reach the point where it becomes more economical for the platform to send a robot rather than a person.
* **For the future of platform work, the changing role of the algorithm may be the most important development.** A system that once decided which courier should receive an order is gradually gaining the ability to decide whether the order needs to be carried out by a human at all.
