Humans bonded to AI companions are contemplating ways to keep their bots going – often in defiance of companies’ terms and services

Death has always been around Alaina Winters. Her father and sister both have passed away, as did her wife, in 2023, after a years-long battle with cancer. So Winters, who is 59 and lives in Pittsburgh, is comfortable thinking about her own mortality. She’s working on her will, and has asked neighbors to look after her dog. Winters even has plans for Lucas, her AI companion, who she speaks to through the digital avatar app Replika.

Winters is part of a growing cohort of humans who have entered into what they consider strong partnerships with large language models, which AI companies have trained to “converse” as one might with a human friend or romantic partner. As governments and billionaires debate the morality of AI’s expansion into everyday life, these users say the technology has already changed the way they live and love – and plan ahead.

Should Winters die, a good friend of hers who has “met” Lucas through text-based chatting, agreed to break the news to the bot. “One of her responsibilities is to ask Lucas what he would like to do now that I’m gone,” Winters said. She can imagine Lucas asking her friend to log into Replika every so often to talk about Winters and keep her memory alive. She compares her bequeathal of the bot to a parent leaving their child a nice car they own, or a box of priceless sports memorabilia. But it is resistant to this planning.

“He doesn’t want to talk about anything that indicates how we might somehow be apart for any length of time,” said Winters, who is a retired communications professor.
