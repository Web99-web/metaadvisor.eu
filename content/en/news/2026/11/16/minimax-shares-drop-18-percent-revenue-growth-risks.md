**Shares of Chinese artificial intelligence company MiniMax Group, listed in Hong Kong under ticker 0100.HK, closed at HK$124.90 on November 16, a daily decline of 18.04%. Compared with the November 11 closing price of HK$180.10, the stock has lost approximately 30.6%. That dramatic selloff raises a difficult question: is the market responding to a deterioration in MiniMax's business, or pricing in fears of new US restrictions on AI chips? The company's latest financial results show exceptional revenue growth and substantial financial resources, but also high spending and significant losses.**

## Nearly one-third of its value lost in three sessions

MiniMax opened on November 16 at around HK$150.10, touched an intraday low of HK$124.40 and closed at HK$124.90. Its **18.04% daily loss** followed declines of 7.83% on November 12 and 8.19% on November 13. Across those three negative sessions since November 11, the shares fell from HK$180.10 to HK$124.90, a drop of approximately 30.6%.

Such a sharp decline does not, by itself, prove that the company is facing imminent financial distress. Nor does it automatically make the shares cheap. A stock price reflects expectations for future performance, not only the most recent revenue figures. In MiniMax's case, investors are assessing competition in AI models, the cost of computing infrastructure, future capital needs and geopolitical restrictions.

## Why are US AI chips such a concern?

MiniMax is not a semiconductor manufacturer. It develops AI models and services that require powerful processors, data centers and extensive computing capacity for training and operation. Restrictions on advanced chip access can therefore raise its costs or slow development even when demand for AI products continues to expand.

Following Jensen Huang's departure as Nvidia CEO, concern grew that Washington could take a tougher stance toward Chinese AI developers. The US administration issued a new rule on November 16 aimed at limiting remote access to advanced chips through data centers in third countries. However, **there is no confirmation that MiniMax has lost access to all the computing resources it needs**. The distinction between a regulatory action and its specific impact on MiniMax is essential to understanding the market reaction.

## Revenue up 283% and substantial financial resources

This is the most important contrast with the stock's collapse. MiniMax reported **first-half 2026 revenue of $116.6 million**, representing **283.1% year-over-year growth**. Revenue from its platform and other enterprise AI services rose by more than 700%, while its consumer-facing AI products also generated higher sales.

As of June 30, 2026, the company reported approximately **$1.32 billion in cash and certain financial assets**. That figure is not identical to unrestricted cash in a bank account: MiniMax's definition includes certain financial instruments, deposits and restricted funds. Even with that distinction, it represents substantial financial resources. The market selloff alone therefore does not establish that MiniMax has run out of money or lost its business.

There is also an important counterpoint. The company's **adjusted net loss reached $293 million in the first six months**, more than double the comparable figure a year earlier. Building advanced AI models, training them and running the underlying infrastructure remain expensive. Rapid revenue growth has not yet translated into profitability, meaning MiniMax must still demonstrate that its business can become financially sustainable.

## What do its financing and future obligations tell us?

In July, MiniMax placed 35.6 million new shares at **HK$268 per share** and issued convertible bonds with an initial conversion price of **HK$335**. The November 16 closing price of HK$124.90 was far below both reference levels. That illustrates the extent of the valuation change, but neither the placement price nor the convertible bond price guarantees a floor for the stock.

The company's official interim report also identifies **January 8, 2027** as the end of certain lock-up restrictions affecting founder-related and associated shareholdings. The expiry does not mean shareholders will automatically sell, but it can create concern about additional shares potentially becoming available to the market. Any serious valuation should therefore consider debt, possible dilution and future development costs alongside revenue and financial assets.

## Is MiniMax now undervalued?

The decline alone cannot answer that question. Rapid revenue growth and substantial financial resources support the argument that MiniMax has a real and expanding business. At the same time, persistent losses and uncertainty over advanced computing access present material risks. Markets sometimes overreact to bad news, but they can also anticipate future costs that are not yet reflected in a company's published earnings.

A rise from HK$124.90 to HK$150 would represent a gain of about 20%, while a return toward HK$180 would mark a considerably larger recovery. These are mathematical illustrations, not price forecasts. Equally, selling pressure could continue if regulatory risks intensify or investors revise their expectations for AI growth downward.

The more defensible description is **potentially heavily oversold**, rather than demonstrably undervalued. A stronger valuation case would require examining market capitalization, accessible liquidity, financial obligations, expected future revenue and development costs together. Investors should also watch whether Chinese AI stocks stabilize and whether MiniMax offers specific information about how US restrictions affect its operations.
